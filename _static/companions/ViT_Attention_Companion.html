AI for Mobility · Module 2 · Sept 17 · Transformers

Where is it looking?

A Vision Transformer has no convolution, no feature map, and no receptive field — so the trick we used on the CNN two weeks ago does not transfer. What it has instead is a number for every pair of image patches saying how much one looked at the other. This page computes those numbers on three real road scenes, turns them into a picture, and puts that picture next to the Grad-CAM you already know — same frame, same input size, same score.

An image is worth 16×16 words — and 196 of them fit in one frame

You are handed a classifier that flags signal head dark from a roadside camera, or a vendor deck claiming their model detects work zones. Before it goes on a pole you need to know it used the signal head and not the sky. For a CNN we answered that with Grad-CAM. A ViT deletes the machinery Grad-CAM leans on, so we need a different read-out — and it turns out the transformer hands us one for free.

The bridge

The sentence-to-vectors machine you saw this morning does not know what a word is. It knows how to let a set of vectors trade information. Dosovitskiy et al. cut the image into fixed squares, call each square a token, and feed them to the same encoder. A 16×16 patch is the “word”; a × frame is a -word sentence in each direction — patch tokens, plus one extra [class] token that carries the answer, for tokens in all.

Every square below is one token. The model never sees anything finer: inside a square it has a single 768-number summary, and that is all.

Why the read-out is not optional

The paper is blunt about what ViT gives up (§3.1, Inductive bias): in a CNN, “locality, two-dimensional neighborhood structure, and translation equivariance are baked into each layer throughout the whole model”. In a ViT, only the per-token MLP is local; the attention layers are global from layer one, and “all spatial relations between the patches have to be learned from scratch.”

The engineering consequence. A CNN cannot look at the far side of the frame in its first layer even if it wants to — the architecture forbids it. A ViT can, and whether it does is a property of the weights, not the design. Two ViTs with identical architecture can behave completely differently, so “it is a transformer” tells you nothing about what it looks at. You have to measure. Tab 3 measures it, and finds exactly that split between two checkpoints of the same model.

Going deeper — where √Dh comes from, and why the 12 heads are cheap

Suppose the entries of q and k behave like independent draws with mean 0 and variance 1. Their dot product is a sum of Dh such products, so it has variance Dh and typical size √Dh — here . Feed scores of that size into a softmax and the largest one wins by orders of magnitude, the weights go one-hot, and the gradient through the softmax goes to zero: the head stops learning. Dividing by √Dh puts the scores back on a scale where the softmax still has a gradient. It is the same reason you standardise inputs before fitting a logit model — and the demo above lets you see it fail.

Multi-head costs nothing extra because Dh = D / heads = 768 / = . The heads split the same 768 numbers rather than duplicating them, which is why a head can afford to specialise — one on the patch under your feet, one on the far side of the intersection — and why the next tab has 144 dots on it rather than 12.

How far does a head look?

Before asking where a ViT looks on a given frame, ask what it is capable of looking at. The paper's answer is a measurement it calls attention distance, and it is the closest thing a transformer has to a receptive field. We ran it on our three road scenes, on two checkpoints of the same architecture, and the two disagree completely.

The measurement

Stand on one patch. You have weights that add to one, and every patch has a location. Take the weighted average distance to all the others. That is how far, in pixels, this head reached from this patch; average over patches and you have one number per head per block.

The head that refuses to look anywhere

Block , head has an attention distance of . It puts of its weight on the query patch itself and on the [class] token. It is, functionally, a pass-through: a 1×1 convolution that the model taught itself, because nobody gave it one.

Read that twice. The architecture contains no notion of “nearby”. Locality is not in the design — it is a pattern in the weights, and the weights only find it if the data makes it worth finding.

The small-data model has no local heads at all in its first block. Every one of its heads averages over px or more of a 224-px image before it has learned anything about edges. Its block means run down to and back up — the opposite shape. This is the paper's central claim, visible in a table: with a CNN's inductive bias removed, data is what puts it back. It is also the reason a ViT fine-tuned on your 4,000 labelled work-zone frames, from scratch, will disappoint you — and the same one started from a large-scale checkpoint may not.

ImageNet-21k = 14M images / 21k classes and JFT = 303M images / 18k classes are the paper's §4.1 figures. The paper's own scaling result (Figure 3) is this same story measured in accuracy instead of pixels: below ImageNet-21k scale, ResNets win; above it, ViT wins.

From a 197×197 matrix to a picture of the road

Every block has matrices and there are blocks, so “the attention” is not one thing. Three read-outs are in common use, they disagree with each other, and the disagreement is the lesson. All three are computed here on the same forward pass.

A · last-block [class] row

Take the final block, average its heads, and read the row belonging to the [class] token — the token the classifier actually reads. One line of code. Ignores the other blocks entirely, and by block 12 every head is global (tab 3), so it is being read at its blurriest.

B · attention rollout

The paper's own choice for its Figure 6. Multiply the blocks together so the mixing compounds, adding an identity matrix at each step to account for the residual connection. Uses every block, but the identity terms and the head-averaging pile up into something smooth.

C · class-conditional relevance

Same rollout, but each block's attention is first weighted by the gradient of one class score and clipped at zero, so only the parts that support that class survive. The only one of the three that changes when you change the question — which is exactly what Grad-CAM does.

Where the heat actually lands

The boxes come from a COCO detector run on the frame at score ≥ 0.5, so nothing here is hand-drawn. Lift = the share of the map's heat inside those boxes, divided by the share of the picture they cover. 1.00 means the heat is spread as if the object were not there; 2.00 means twice its fair share. Same definition as the Grad-CAM companion.

The two hard scenes fail for a reason that has nothing to do with attention

On the night crossing the model's top answer is , and on the work zone it is . Neither is wrong, exactly — ImageNet-1k has no class for a pedestrian, a lane line, a work zone or a road-closed sign. There are 120 breeds of dog. You cannot ask a class-conditional method to explain a decision the label set cannot express, which is why column C is near 1.00 for the vulnerable road users in both scenes while the class-free maps still put more than their share of heat there.

This is the single most useful thing on the page for your own projects. Interpretability is downstream of the label set. If the head on your model was fine-tuned on classes that matter to you — work zone present, signal head dark, queue spillback — then method C answers a question you care about. If you are borrowing someone's ImageNet head, only the class-free maps mean anything, and they answer “what is salient here”, not “why that decision”.

Class-free maps still carry signal: on the night frame the last-block [class] row puts its fair share of heat on the one pedestrian the detector finds, with no word for “person” anywhere in the model's vocabulary.

Why the CNN cannot point at a signal head at this input size

Nothing to do with transformers. A Grad-CAM is drawn on the last convolutional feature map, and at a -pixel input that map is cells. The ViT map is cells, because the patch grid never downsamples. That is a difference in how finely either map can point, before either model is asked anything.

Neither method resolves a signal head, and the ViT is only less bad. A head is of a ViT cell. When the ViT map lights up “on the signal heads” it is lighting up cells that contain the mast arm, the heads, sky and a palm tree. It is telling you the answer came from up there, not from that head. If your question is which of five heads is dark, no 224-pixel heat map answers it — you crop, or you raise the input resolution, and then you re-measure. The Grad-CAM companion made the same point at 672×416; at 224 it bites harder.

You can run Grad-CAM on a ViT too — treat the patch tokens of some block as the feature map. It works, and it is often the right thing to do. The reason to prefer an attention-based read-out is not that it is more faithful; it is that attention is the only thing in a transformer with an unambiguous spatial address.

Evaluation close

  • Baseline. Lift 1.00 — heat spread as if the object were not in the frame. A map only earns the word “correct” by beating it, and two of the numbers in the table above do not.
  • The split, and why it is honest. There is none, and that matters: these are pretrained classifiers run on three frames they never saw, and the ROI boxes come from a third model, not from us. Nothing here was tuned to make a map look good. Equally, three frames is an anecdote — it establishes what the methods do, not how often they do it.
  • The ugly cases. The work zone, where both methods give lifts near 1.00 for the cyclist; the night crossing, where the class-conditional map goes flat because no class fits; and every signal head, which neither map can resolve at this input size.

What these maps are worth, and when to reach for one

A heat map is persuasive in a way that is out of proportion to what it proves. The literature on this is unusually pointed — the title of one of the standard references is “Attention is not Explanation” — so the closing move on any map you show is a test, not a screenshot.

A test you can run in ten lines: delete what the map points at

If a map is telling the truth about what the model used, blanking the patches it ranks highest should destroy the prediction faster than blanking random patches. Below: the stop-bar frame, patches blanked in the order each map ranks them, and the class probability that survives. The dashed line is random deletion — the bar every map has to clear.

Where it breaks

  • Attention is a weight, not a reason. Jain & Wallace showed attention weights can be substantially altered without changing a model's output — so a weight is evidence about information flow, not proof of importance. Wiegreffe & Pinter pushed back that this depends on what you wanted the word “explanation” to mean. Both are worth reading before you put a heat map in front of a client.
  • The residual stream routes around attention. A token keeps most of itself at every block. Information the model uses can arrive without a large attention weight ever being involved, and no attention map will show it.
  • The [class] token is a sink. of the heads in block 1 send more than 30% of every patch's attention to the [class] token — one of them — and the [class] token sits nowhere in the image. Dropping it and renormalising — which every attention-distance number on this page does — is a choice, and it flatters the map.
  • Head averaging hides the disagreement. The heads of a block routinely point at different things; averaging them is what makes rollout smooth. Look at the per-head maps before you trust the average.
  • Resolution beats interpretation. At a 224-pixel input, one ViT cell is on the ground at the lead car. Most objects a transportation engineer cares about — a signal head, a lane line, a sign face, a pedestrian at range — are smaller than that.
  • Three frames is an anecdote. Nothing here is a claim about how often a ViT looks in the right place. It is a claim about what the read-outs measure and how to check one.
The discipline, carried over from the Grad-CAM page. There we ran the cascading-randomisation sanity check of Adebayo et al. before believing any map. The equivalent here is the deletion test above, and it is the minimum. If you show a map in a report, show the deletion curve next to it.

Fit card — attention read-outs (rollout / class relevance)

Idea in one sentence
A transformer computes, for free, how much every patch drew from every other patch; collect those weights across blocks and you get a map of where the answer came from.
Why it works (the logic)
Information in a transformer moves only two ways — along the residual stream, which keeps a token where it is, and through attention, which moves it between tokens. Reading the attention weights traces the second path back to the image.
Fits these tasks
Auditing a vision transformer before deployment; localising the evidence for a frame-level label you have no boxes for; showing a client or a review panel what a model keyed on. Leverage labels: judgment, and extraction when you use the map to propose regions.
Needs this data
One forward pass on one image. No labels, no retraining, no held-out set. For the class-conditional version, a backward pass and a class your model's head can actually name. Anyone with the checkpoint can do it — including, usefully, you, on a vendor's model.
Breaks when
The object is smaller than a patch; the label set has no word for what you care about; you read one block or one head and call it the model; you treat a weight as a cause; you skip the deletion test.
Keep the classical method when
You need a location, not an explanation — then train a detector and report boxes with precision and recall, which is auditable in a way no heat map is. And if the model is a CNN, Grad-CAM is simpler, is class-specific by construction, and was sharper on the big object in tab 5.

When a run flags a sign as damaged, the map either shows heat on the sign face or on the guardrail behind it, and only one of those is a usable inspection record. Consulting & air mobilityAny deliverable where a model's output goes into a decision someone can be sued over — a safety audit, a warrant study, a drone-based inspection report. How every number on this page was produced

Models, unmodified and pretrained: (the ViT authors' original JAX checkpoint, ImageNet-21k pre-trained and ImageNet-1k fine-tuned, converted to PyTorch by timm); torchvision vit_b_16 IMAGENET1K_V1 as the small-data contrast; torchvision resnet50 IMAGENET1K_V2 for the Grad-CAM; fasterrcnn_resnet50_fpn COCO_V1 at score ≥ 0.5 for the ROI boxes, used only as a ruler. Attention, q, k and v are extracted by a hand-written re-implementation of the attention block, checked against each library's own forward pass to a maximum logit difference of (timm) and (torchvision). ResNet receptive fields are measured, not quoted: ReLUs replaced by identities, a gradient run back to the input from one unit, and the width of the non-zero support read off — giving / / / / / px, which is what the standard tables say.
