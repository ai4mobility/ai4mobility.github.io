How far can one pixel see? MobileViT's two hops, drawn and then measured

MobileViT puts a small transformer inside a MobileNet. The paper says that inside its block “every pixel sees every other pixel.” That is true, but only in two hops, and only when the patch is no wider than the convolution. Tab 1 lets you trace the two hops cell by cell. Tabs 2 and 3 measure how far a feature actually reaches in a pretrained MobileViT-S, next to the MobileNetV2 it is built from, on Florida road frames.

The key equation: what attention over patches costs

pairs per attention layer = P · N² = (H·W)² / P P = h·w pixels per patch = number of separate attention sequences N = H·W / P patches = tokens in each sequence H·W cells in the feature map, e.g. 32 × 56 at stride 8 for a 448 × 256 frame

In words: unfold splits the map into P sequences, one per position inside a patch, and each sequence holds one cell from every patch. Attention runs inside each sequence, so it costs P times less than letting every cell attend to every cell. On this 24×24 map with 2×2 patches that is pairs against .

The reach rule: after the n×n conv, each blue cell already summarizes its own n×n neighbourhood. Attention then passes those summaries to the red cell. The summaries tile the whole map only if the blue cells are close enough together, which means the patch side must not exceed the kernel: h ≤ n. Try 4×4 patches with a 3×3 kernel in step 3 to see the gaps.

Cells that can influence the centre cell (11, 11) of the 24×24 map; 576 is the whole map. “Full map from every cell”: share of all 576 output cells whose step-3 region is the entire map. Cells near the border can lose a strip when h = n (pick Corner with n = 3, h = 3).

The paper reached the same rule by experiment. MobileViT-XS with patch sides (2, 2, 2) in its three blocks scored % top-1 on ImageNet at ms on an iPhone 12. With larger patches (8, 4, 2) it ran faster, at ms, but fell to %. The authors write that MobileViT “can aggregate information more effectively” when h, w ≤ n.

The colouring follows five rules. Local conv: the n×n square around the cell. Attention alone: every cell at the same position inside its patch (row and column equal modulo h). Step 3: the n×n squares around those cells. Step 4: the union of step 3 over every cell inside the fusion conv's n×n square, plus that square itself, which arrives through the concatenated input.

Each rule was compared with the gradient support of a real timm MobileVitBlock with random weights, built with the same n and h, at five output cells including two on the border. comparisons, mismatches. Each comparison takes the union over three random initializations, because one initialization can underflow an attention weight to exactly zero.

Why h ≤ n: neighbouring blue cells sit h cells apart, and an n×n square (n odd) covers n consecutive cells, so the squares leave no gap exactly when h ≤ n. At the border there is no blue cell outside the map, so a strip can stay dark unless the first blue cell is within ⌊n/2⌋ of the edge. Does the brake light find its partner?

Follow the right brake light on the stop-bar frame. A braking detector needs both lights, and the left one is px away, outside the reach of every convolution at stride 8. The table asks how much sensitivity lands on other red lights in the frame, found by a colour rule rather than drawn by hand. Lift is the share of sensitivity on the region divided by the region's share of the image; 1× means no special attention.

What changes at block 1. Before any attention, MobileViT-S puts nothing on the left brake light. One block later, the left light gets its area share, and each of its pixels carries the sensitivity of an average pixel at the same distance. MobileNetV2 cannot reach the left light at stride 8, and even at stride 32, where it can, the light gets . Attention matches content wherever it is: here the left brake light, and to a lesser degree the red SUV's tail light (). The small third brake light, only px away, gets .
What it does not show. The red signal heads get after block 1, which is no special attention at all. These weights were trained on ImageNet, not on driving. The block can reach the signals, but nothing taught it that a signal matters to a braking car. Reach is a property of the architecture; what flows through it depends on training.

Evaluation close

Baseline
MobileNetV2 with ImageNet weights. MobileViT-S is built from the same inverted-residual blocks, so the comparison isolates the transformer, not a different family.
Data and why it is fair
Two forward-camera frames in tab 2 and roadside frames here, all real Florida roads. No labels are used and nothing was tuned on these frames. Both networks see the same pixels, with each one's own input normalization applied inside the measurement.
Ugly cases
Sensitivity is a gradient around one image, not a decision. It shows what could move a feature, not what the model relied on for a label. Both nets were trained on ImageNet, so these maps describe the architecture plus generic weights. A braking or lane model fine-tuned on driving video would redistribute them.
Going deeper: the estimator (optional)

For a feature vector y at one cell, the sensitivity of pixel u is the size of the Jacobian column ∂y/∂xu summed over RGB: S(u) = √( Er[ Σrgb (∂(r·y)/∂xu)² ] ), with r a random ±1 vector over channels. That expectation equals the squared Frobenius norm, so random draws estimate it without one backward pass per channel ( per frame here). This follows the effective-receptive-field idea of Luo et al., NeurIPS 2016, but uses every channel instead of one. The dashed square is the exact theoretical receptive field of the convolution-only path (the network's convolutions, plus the 3×3 local and fusion convs of each MobileViT block).

What the reach costs, and when to use it

Attention pairs in MobileViT-S on a 448×256 frame

Per head, per transformer layer. 2×2 patches give the factor of 4 at every stride.

Latency, measured here and on a phone

Fewer pairs is not the same as fast. On the phone the paper reports MobileNetV2 at ms and MobileViT-XS at ms, and explains that mobile runtimes lack the optimized transformer kernels that GPUs have. Unfold, fold and small attention matrices are cheap in arithmetic but slow on hardware tuned for convolutions. Measure on the device you will ship to.

Fit card: MobileViT

Idea in one sentence
Convolutions summarize each neighbourhood, attention among same-position pixels of every patch spreads those summaries across the whole image, and a fusion conv merges the global result with the local features.
Why it works
Two hops reach every pixel when the patch side h ≤ kernel n, at 1/P of the cost of all-pixel attention, and every pixel keeps its own token, so fine detail is not squashed into a 16×16 patch the way ViT does it.
Fits these tasks
On-device classification, detection or segmentation where a local cue needs scene context: paired brake lights, lane continuity past a worn stretch, how far a queue extends. classificationextraction
Needs this data
Same as a MobileNet: ImageNet-pretrained weights plus a few thousand labeled frames from your cameras to fine-tune. Context needs no extra labels.
Breaks when
The runtime has no fast attention, so a phone pays several times MobileNetV2's latency. Patches grow past the kernel. The weights' license limits use: the timm checkpoints carry the non-free cvnets license, so check it before a class project or product ships.
Keep the classical method when
The cue is purely local (a sign crop, a plate) and the latency budget is tight: use MobileNet. Lanes are clean and the camera is fixed: an edge detector plus a Hough or polynomial lane fit is cheaper and easier to audit.

Check yourself

  1. A city wants a phone app that warns transit drivers when the vehicle ahead brakes, at 30 frames per second. MobileNetV2 or MobileViT-XS? What would you measure before deciding?
    One way to answer

    Tab 2 shows block 1 linking the two brake lights ( lift) where MobileNetV2 cannot reach at stride 8. On an iPhone 12 both fit a 33 ms frame budget ( vs ms for classification), and detection at 320×320 costs vs ms. Measure latency on the drivers' actual phones, and accuracy on night and rain clips with whole routes held out.

  2. A teammate switches the patches to 4×4 to speed up inference, keeping 3×3 kernels. What happens to the reach, and what do you expect for accuracy?
    One way to answer

    Step 3 leaves holes: of 576 cells at the centre, under half the map. The fusion conv fills them, but with one more hop of mixing. The paper's (8, 4, 2) variant was faster ( vs ms) and lost a point of accuracy (% vs %). Decide by whether that point matters for your task.

  3. “After block 1 the brake-light feature is sensitive to the signal heads, so the model knows the light is red.” Do you agree?
    One way to answer

    No. The signal heads get lift after block 1, which is no special attention at all. Reachable is not the same as used, and these are ImageNet weights. Even a high lift is a gradient around one image, not evidence that the model's decision depends on the signal. Test that by fine-tuning on labeled driving data and occluding the signal.
