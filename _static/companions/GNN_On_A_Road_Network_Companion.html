A GNN on a road network: one shared rule, any number of intersections

Every network on this page is generated in your browser, and every number is computed live from it. Press New network as often as you like — the intersections change, the degrees change, the size changes, and the same rule keeps running. The point of the page is to show you exactly where the structure of the city enters the computation, and how a one-hop rule ends up modelling a disturbance that travels for miles.

The rule, written once

for every directed street segment v → u (however many that is): m(v→u) = tanh( Wm · [ h_v ‖ h_u ‖ e_(v→u) ] + bm ) then at each intersection u, collapse however many arrived: a(u) = mean over v ∈ N(u) of m(v→u) and update: h_u' = tanh( Wu · [ h_u ‖ a(u) ] + bu )
SymbolIn transportation terms
h_uthe state vector the model is carrying for intersection u. On the first layer it is just the measured congestion c_u; after that it is whatever the model has worked out about u and its surroundings.
e_(v→u)what this connection is: here, +1 if v lies downstream of u (between u and the CBD, so a queue at v can back up into u), −1 if v is upstream, 0 if neither.
m_(v→u)the message — what v's condition means for u specifically, given the kind of connection between them.
W_m, b_mthe message weights. One set, shared by every segment in the city. This is the whole model.
N(u)u's neighbours. Its size is the node's degree: 1, 2, 3, 4 or 5 in this network. Nothing in W_m or W_u knows or cares.

In words: for each street touching this intersection, work out what the far end's condition means for this end; then average those meanings, whatever their number; then use the average to update what you believe about this intersection.

The arity problem never appears. ψ always takes exactly two nodes and one connection. A 5-leg intersection calls it five times and a dead end calls it once. The variable part of the city is absorbed by how many times you call, not by the function you call — the same way conservation of flow at a node is one equation summed over however many approaches, not a different equation for 3-leg and 4-leg intersections.

Structure is not something the GNN "figures out" — it is the wiring

This is the part that is hard to see from the equation. The model has one set of weights, so how can it possibly treat a downtown five-leg intersection differently from a cul-de-sac? The answer is that the same function gets different arguments, because the neighbourhoods differ. To make that unmistakable, the demo below wipes out every difference in the input: every intersection is given exactly the same feature value. The blind spot worth knowing about

Plain message passing sees a node only through the shape of its surroundings. If two intersections have the same degree, and their neighbours have the same degrees, and so on out to L hops, then no number of layers will separate them — they receive identical messages forever. Press the button to find such a pair in the current network.

—
Why you should care. Two intersections can be structurally identical and operationally nothing alike — one is a school crossing, one is a freeway ramp terminal. The fix is not more layers; it is to put the difference in the features: lanes, control type, speed limit, distance to the nearest ramp, betweenness. Notice that all of those are computable in any city, so unlike a learned per-sensor embedding they cost you nothing in transferability.
Going deeper — the name for this ceiling

What the demo shows by hand is a known limit: message-passing GNNs distinguish nodes exactly as far as the 1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman colour-refinement test does, and no further. Each layer is one round of refinement — a node's new colour is a function of its old colour and the multiset of its neighbours' colours. Two nodes WL never separates, a GNN never separates.

The practical consequences are the ones above: add structural or positional features to the input (degree, centrality, Laplacian eigenvector coordinates) rather than adding depth. See Xu et al., How Powerful are Graph Neural Networks? (ICLR 2019) for the equivalence and for why a sum aggregator with an MLP reaches the ceiling while mean and max fall short of it.

Cascading: a one-hop rule, applied L times, is an L-hop model

Here is the ground truth this page uses — a queue spillback rule, and deliberately a local one. A bottleneck pins one intersection at full congestion; every other intersection is pulled toward the worst of its downstream neighbours and otherwise recovers. Nothing in the rule mentions anything beyond one hop. Press play and watch a wide-area jam emerge from it anyway.

c_u(t+1) = clip( c_u(t) + β·( max over downstream v of c_v(t) − c_u(t) ) − γ , 0, 1 ) β = 0.70 spillback rate γ = 0.05 recovery per step bottleneck node held at c = 1

In words: if the road you are feeding into is jammed, you become jammed; otherwise you clear. That is the whole rule, and it is exactly the shape a single GNN layer can express — a pairwise message from each downstream neighbour, a max, an update.

The design rule this gives you. Congestion here travels about one hop per time step. So to predict K steps ahead you need the bottleneck inside your receptive field, which means roughly L ≥ K layers. In a real network, substitute the backward wave speed w and your forecast horizon T: the disturbance travels about w·T, so the hops you need is w·T divided by your mean link length. Pick depth from the traffic, then check whether your architecture can actually reach that far.
This is the answer to "how does it learn cascading relationships". It does not learn a cascade. It learns a one-hop rule, and the cascade is what you get when you compose that rule L times. Exactly like running the cell transmission model for L time steps: no step of it ever looked further than the next cell, but a shockwave crosses the whole corridor.

The split, and why it is honest

Training and test are different networks, not different time slices of one network. That matters: a model with per-node parameters would score beautifully on a random time split of a single city and could not be run on any other, so a time split would not have tested the thing we care about. Nothing here is per-node, so the trained weights load straight onto a bigger unseen city.

Depth versus horizon

Read across a row and you see depth barely matters for the one-step problem — one hop of reach is all a one-step prediction needs. Read down a column at K = 3 and the picture changes completely: a single layer simply cannot see where the disturbance is coming from, and no amount of training fixes it. Depth buys reach, and you need exactly as much reach as the physics of your horizon demands.

The aggregator is a modelling choice, not a hyperparameter

Same architecture, same depth (L = 3), same data, K = 3. Only ⊕ changes. 3 seeds each.

The ground-truth rule takes a max over downstream neighbours — a queue backs up from the worst road you feed into, not the average one. The max aggregator can represent that exactly; mean and sum have to approximate it. Pick ⊕ from what the quantity does: sum when it is conserved (flows into a node), mean when it is an intensive state (prevailing speed), max when a bottleneck governs, attention when the weighting is the thing you do not know.

Parameter counts — the litmus test

None of these contain N. That is the whole claim of size-independence, and it is checkable in ten seconds on any paper you read: if the parameter count mentions the number of nodes, the model cannot be moved to another city. Learned per-sensor embeddings and learned adjacency matrices both fail this test.

Attention: when you don't know which neighbour matters, learn it

The last tab measured something uncomfortable: max beat mean by 4.3×, purely because the rule generating the data takes a max over downstream links. We only got that by knowing the physics in advance. Attention is what you do when you don't — let the model decide, for each intersection and each moment, which neighbour to listen to. It is the same transformer layer you have seen on text, run on a street network instead of a sentence.

The rule, written once

every intersection turns its own state into a question — a query: q_u = WQ · [ c_u , 1 ] every street advertises what it is offering — a key and a value: k_(v→u) = WK · [ c_v , e_(v→u) ] val_v = c_v score every street that touches u, and normalise over those and only those: s_(u,v) = ( q_u · k_(v→u) ) / √d α_(u,v) = exp( s_(u,v) ) / Σ[ w ∈ N(u) ] exp( s_(u,w) ) and blend: a(u) = Σ[ v ∈ N(u) ] α_(u,v) · val_v
SymbolIn transportation terms
q_uwhat intersection u is asking about. Built from u's own congestion, so a jammed link and an empty link ask different questions.
k_(v→u)what the far end of this street is advertising: how bad it is (c_v) and what kind of connection this is (e = +1 downstream, −1 upstream). The key is a property of the pair, not of v alone.
val_vwhat actually gets mixed in if the model decides to listen — here just v's congestion, so the output stays readable.
√dd is the length of q and k (2 on this page). Dividing keeps the scores from growing with d and pushing the softmax into a hard max before training has started.
N(u)u's neighbours. This is the only place the street network appears. It sets the sum in the denominator — nothing else.
α_(u,v)the attention coefficient: the share of u's update that comes from v. Non-negative, and the shares over u's approaches add to exactly 1.

In words: each intersection asks a question shaped by its own condition; each street connected to it answers with what it has to offer; the match between question and answer sets how much of that neighbour's state gets mixed in.

Varying degree needs no special handling — again. A five-leg intersection sums five terms in the denominator and a dead end sums one. WQ and WK never change size. This is the same dissolution as tab 1, with one upgrade: the weights in the blend are no longer fixed at 1/deg, they are computed from the values.
And one thing you give up. The coefficients are forced to sum to 1, so this layer can only decide how to split attention — never that nothing is arriving. That is why plain attention is not automatically better than sum, which can see totals. It is a weighted mean with learned weights, and it inherits a weighted mean's blind spot.

So does it need the topology?

Mechanically, no — the softmax will happily run over all N intersections. The topology is not in the equation, not in the parameters, and not in the shape of any matrix. It enters in exactly one place: the set the denominator sums over. Switch the toy example above to Full and nothing about the computation changes; C is simply allowed to also score E, a jammed link on the other side of town with no street connecting it.

Note what full attention also destroys: there is no e_(v→u) for a pair with no street between them, so on a real network the direction feature collapses to the same constant for every pair. Dropping the mask does not just add candidates — it throws away the one feature that separates a link that drains into you from a link that feeds you. The panel above keeps A, B and D correctly labelled so you can see the denominator acting alone; the measurement further down prices the two losses separately.

Read it as two separate purchases. Restricting the softmax to real streets buys 0.0839 → 0.0556, a 34% cut. Telling the layer which way each street runs buys another 0.0556 → 0.0286, a further 49% cut. Both cost nothing: identical equation, identical ten parameters, one extra input the road inventory already contains. Neither is a modelling trick — both are the engineer declining to make the model rediscover the map. You can see it in where the attention lands, too: fitted with the direction flag, 89% of the coefficient mass sits on the genuinely worst downstream neighbour (46% before fitting). Without it, 55% — barely better than the random start.
Full attention barely clears the floor. 0.0839 against a persistence baseline of 0.0870 is a 4% improvement — for a model that is, mechanically, a transformer layer. Nothing is wrong with it. It was simply asked to discover from twelve rollouts on two small networks what the street inventory would have told it for free, and twelve rollouts is not enough to learn a city map. That is the whole argument for masking, priced.
Honest about the spread, and about one bad seed. Look at seed 5 in the typed row: it reaches 0.0143 on training data — the tightest fit of all six runs — and 0.0810 on the unseen networks. That is not a failed optimisation; every random restart lands in the same place. Its two training networks simply did not contain what the two test networks ask about, and the model learned those cities rather than the rule. It drags the typed mean from a median of 0.0208 up to 0.0286 and makes the typed and untyped ranges overlap even though five of six seeds are separated by a factor of two or more. Six seeds is not many. Report the spread and the median, look at the train-test gap, and never report one run.
And the result that should stop you reaching for attention by reflex. Lab 5 puts attention against fixed aggregators that encode the same domain knowledge by hand. Against a ground truth that takes a max over downstream links, a plain masked max — zero learned attention, half the parameters — scores 0.0081 against attention's 0.0080. A tie. Attention earned its keep only where the truth really was a weighted average (0.0110 against 0.0122), and it was the least stable method in the table, with a 4.2× seed range. Right support beats learned weighting; learned weighting beats a generic reduction. Attention is the tool for when you cannot write the selector down — not an upgrade over knowing it.
Going deeper — why √d, and why the original GAT cannot do what you just watched

The √d. If q and k have d independent components of roughly unit size, their dot product has a standard deviation of about √d. Let d grow and the raw scores spread out, the softmax saturates, and the gradient through every non-winning neighbour goes to nearly zero before the model has learned anything. Dividing by √d holds the spread constant as d changes. On this page d = 2, so the divisor is 1.41 and you can do it in your head.

The original GAT has no query and no key. Veličković et al. (ICLR 2018) score a pair with a single shared W and one attention vector applied to a concatenation: e = LeakyReLU(aT[Wh_u ‖ Wh_v]). Split a into its two halves and that is LeakyReLU(c_u + s_v) — a term that depends only on the asking node plus a term that depends only on the answering one. LeakyReLU is monotone, so adding c_u shifts every score in a row by the same amount and cannot reorder them. Every intersection in the city ranks its neighbours identically. Brody et al. (ICLR 2022) named this static attention and fixed it by moving the nonlinearity inside: e = aTLeakyReLU(W[h_u ‖ h_v]), which is GATv2.

If you teach graph attention with Q/K/V — and you should — label it GATv2 or graph transformer, not GAT-2018.

Fit card — graph attention

Idea in one sentence
Let each node score its own neighbours from their current state, and blend them with the resulting weights, instead of averaging them all equally.
Why it works
Which neighbour governs a link is not a fixed property of the network — it changes with conditions. A fixed aggregator has to commit once; attention recomputes the ranking every time step from the values themselves.
Fits these tasks
Network-scale state estimation and short-horizon forecasting where influence shifts with congestion; incident impact propagation; anywhere approaches are non-interchangeable and you cannot say in advance which one binds. prediction
Needs this data
A network with real connectivity (any DOT link–node inventory), a state per node over time (detectors, probe speeds), and enough episodes to cover the regimes you want it to distinguish. Labels come free from the next time step. No hand-labelling.
Breaks when
The truth is a total rather than a share — the coefficients sum to 1 and cannot express "nothing is arriving". Also when the binding neighbour is genuinely off-graph (diversion to a parallel corridor), when episodes are few (it was the least stable method in Lab 5), and when one run is reported without a seed spread.
Keep the classical method when
You can write the selector down. A masked max over downstream links matched attention here at half the parameters and a third of the variance. Node conservation and a sending/receiving function are still exactly right for a corridor, and they transfer to networks with no training data at all.

Fit card, failure modes, and what to check

Method
Message-passing graph neural network
Idea in one sentence
Learn one rule for what a single connection means, apply it to every connection in the network, collapse whatever arrives at each node, and repeat — so the size and shape of the network live in the wiring rather than in the weights.
Why it works (the logic)
Pairwise messages have fixed arity, so a shared function covers 3-leg and 5-leg intersections alike; a permutation-invariant reduction absorbs the varying degree; stacking L layers composes a one-hop rule into an L-hop model, which is how a local rule reproduces a wide-area cascade.
Fits these tasks
Link speed and travel-time forecasting, sensor imputation, incident impact propagation, signal-network state estimation, resilience and cascading-failure analysis on any infrastructure network. prediction classification
Needs this data
A network with node and edge attributes that describe what things are (lanes, control type, capacity, turn geometry, distance) plus a time series of node or link states. Volume: enough rollouts to cover the regimes you care about, and — critically — more than one network if you ever want to transfer. Labels come free when the target is a future measured state. Agencies hold all of it: detector and probe feeds, signal logs, an OSM or HPMS network.
Breaks when
The parameter count contains N (per-sensor embeddings, learned adjacency) — then it cannot leave its training city at all. Neighbours are non-interchangeable but nothing in the edge features says so. Degree distribution shifts and the aggregator was a sum. The horizon needs more hops than the depth provides. Two nodes are structurally identical but operationally different and the features do not distinguish them. The real coupling is not along the network — a detour, a special event, weather.
Keep the classical method when
The network is small enough to fit per-location models (a dozen intersections on one corridor: fit a dozen models and skip all of this). The dynamics you need are already a known conservation law — CTM and LWR are derived, correct everywhere, and need four parameters, not three thousand. You have one city and no intention of moving. Or a per-sensor historical average by time-of-day already meets the accuracy the decision needs, which on quiet days it very often does.

How this result was evaluated

The baseline. Persistence — predict that nothing changes — plus the comparison across depths, so no depth gets credit for what one hop already achieves.

The split, and why it is honest. Train and test are disjoint networks of different sizes, not time slices of one network. A time split would have let a model memorise locations and still look good, which is the failure mode we are trying to detect.

The ugly cases. Nodes whose bottleneck lies further away than the model's depth — these are the errors that dominate the K = 3 column and they do not go away with more data. Dead-end nodes with a single neighbour, where a mean over one message carries no smoothing. And the step right as the bottleneck clears, when the model's learned bias toward persistence is exactly wrong.

One honest caveat about this page. The ground truth here is a rule we wrote down, so a model that reaches far enough can nail it. Real traffic is not generated by a tidy one-hop rule, and the gains reported in the cross-city forecasting literature are far smaller than the ones in the table on tab 4. Take the shape of the result — depth buys reach; the aggregator should match the physics — not the magnitude.
