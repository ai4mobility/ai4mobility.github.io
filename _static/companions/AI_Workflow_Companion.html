What Is an AI Workflow?

Before you pick a project, you need one idea straight: this course is not about training a model. It is about rebuilding a piece of transportation work that someone does today — slowly, manually, or not at all — so that an AI system does part of it and a human still owns the result. That thing is a workflow. You have been building workflows since your first semester; you just called them procedures, pipelines, and models.

A model answers a question. A workflow gets a job done.

Most AI coursework stops at the model: data in, prediction out, accuracy on a held-out set. That is one box. A workflow is the whole diagram around it — where the input comes from, what happens to the output, who is accountable, and what happens on the day the model is wrong.

Working definition

An AI workflow is a repeatable sequence of steps that turns a real input into a decision or artifact someone acts on, where one or more steps are performed by an AI system and every step has an owner.

Three words carry the weight. Repeatable — it runs again next week without you. Acts on — something changes in the world: a ticket is filed, a timing plan is pushed, a figure enters a paper. Owner — for each step, a named human or a named model, and for the whole thing, a named human.

The same is true of an HCM level-of-service procedure, a crash-data screening for HSIP candidates, a signal retiming study, a simulation calibration loop, and the arc of your own thesis: collect → clean → fit → validate → compare to a baseline → write. Learning "AI workflows" is mostly learning where AI can take over a box, and — more importantly — which boxes it must never take over alone.

Why the course is built this way

Because agencies buy workflows, not modelsNo DOT procures "a 94% classifier." They procure a way to get inspections done, with a record of who approved what.
Because a workflow can be started before you know any AIMapping the current process and measuring its baseline needs zero machine learning. That is Round 1, and you can start it the week you pick a topic.
Because it makes your degree the scarce partAnyone can call an API. Knowing which step is safe to automate, and what the answer looks like when it is right, is a transportation question.

Anatomy: six parts, and one taxonomy you will use all semester

Every workflow in this course decomposes into the same six parts. Draw them in this order and you will not forget the two that students always skip — the checkpoint and the record.

1 Triggerwhat starts it: a schedule, an upload, a request, a drive
→
2 Ingestget the raw thing: video, PDF, CSV, CAN bus, API pull
→
3 Transformthe AI steps: extract, classify, generate, retrieve, predict
→
4 Actwrite to a system: draft a ticket, post a file, call a tool
→
5 Checkpoint ★a human approves, edits, or rejects
→
6 Recordlog the input, output, and decision — this is your evaluation data
Placement rule for the checkpoint: it goes immediately before the first irreversible action — before something is filed, published, dispatched, billed, or pushed to a field controller. Everything upstream of that point can be automated aggressively, because a mistake there is cheap. Everything downstream is expensive to undo.

Part 6 is not paperwork. The record of what the AI proposed and what the human changed is exactly the dataset you need to answer "is this actually working?" — and the only honest source of the failure modes you will report in Round 2. Design it in on day one; you cannot reconstruct it later.

The AI-leverage taxonomy

Label every step in your workflow with exactly one of these six. You will do it formally on slide 5 of your Round 1 presentation, so start now.

LabelWhat the step doesMobility examples
extractionPull structured fields out of unstructured input.Crash narrative → vehicle count, maneuver, lighting condition. Permit PDF → parcel ID, dates, applicant. Scanned as-built → sign inventory.
classificationAssign a label from a fixed set.Is this pavement distress a pothole, a patch, or alligator cracking? Is this crash a rear-end? Is this frame worth a human's attention?
generationProduce draft text, code, or scenarios.Draft the maintenance work order. Write the SUMO config for a scenario. Draft the methods paragraph. Synthesize a rare cut-in event for testing.
retrievalFind the relevant passage or record among many.Which MUTCD or Green Book clause governs this case? Which of 3,000 prior FDOT projects used this treatment? Which segment ID does this GPS point belong to?
predictionEstimate a number or a future state from data.Travel time in 15 minutes. Expected crash frequency on a segment. EV energy consumption on a trip. Time-to-collision in the next 3 seconds.
judgmentWeigh trade-offs, accept consequences, sign the result.Should this intersection get the money? Is this crash record correct enough to enter the state database? Is this finding publishable? Do we close the lane?
The line runs after row five. The first five are steps you can hand to a machine because you can write a test for them — you can hold out 200 labeled cases and measure. Judgment is not a step you can hand over; it is the reason a human stays in the loop. In your Round 1 presentation you must mark at least one step "AI must not act alone here" and give your reason. That slide is not a formality — it is the part practitioners on the advisory committee will read first.

A useful test if you are unsure: ask "if this step is wrong and nobody notices for six months, what does it cost?" Cheap and visible → automate it. Expensive and invisible → that is where the checkpoint goes.

Pipeline or loop?

A pipeline runs each step once, in order. Most inspection, coding, and reporting workflows are pipelines.

A loop — propose, act, observe, revise — repeats until a stopping rule is met. Simulation calibration is a loop. So is hyperparameter search, and so is an agent that keeps querying a tool until it has an answer.

Loops are more powerful and much easier to get wrong, because you must supply the objective and the stopping rule. An optimizer with a badly chosen objective will happily find parameters that match your counts and violate the fundamental diagram. We build agentic loops on Oct 8; start with a pipeline.

Four worked workflows

Two from agency practice, two from research. Pick one and read the step table — the point is not the AI, it is how few of the steps are AI and how much of the work is knowing what the steps are.

Eight more, in one line each

  • Traveler advisory filtering. Pull the statewide FL511 event feed, keep only what matters for this vehicle's route and heading, speak it aloud. retrieval classification — a live DoTPilot branch.
  • Literature screening. 900 abstracts → 60 worth reading → an evidence table with the extracted design parameters. classification extraction
  • Public comment synthesis. 1,200 comments from a corridor study → themed summary with representative quotes and counts, for the planning report. classification generation
  • Signal request triage. Citizen complaints and 311 tickets → routed to the right district engineer, with the relevant timing plan and count history attached. classification retrieval
  • Work-zone plan review. A submitted TTC plan → check against the MUTCD clauses that govern it, flag the three items a reviewer should look at first. retrieval judgment stays human
  • Transit reliability reporting. AVL feed → on-time performance by route → a monthly narrative that says what changed and why. prediction generation
  • ADAS log triage. Hours of driving logs → the 40 seconds where lane keeping disengaged, clipped and labeled with context. classification extraction
  • EV charging siting support. Parcel, utility, and traffic layers → candidate sites with a written rationale a planner can defend in a public meeting. prediction generation

Any of these is a legitimate capstone. So is a workflow you bring from your own lab, your assistantship, or your employer — those are usually the best ones, because the baseline numbers already exist and someone actually wants the answer.

"Better" is a comparison. Better than what?

This is the question that decides your grade, and the one an advisory committee member will ask in the first ninety seconds. A workflow with no baseline is a demo, and a demo is worth less than a measured 12% improvement on something boring.

The four numbers to bring back from your partner

How long?Minutes per case, hours per week. Measure one real case end to end if nobody knows.
How often?Cases per week. A 90% time saving on something done twice a year is not a project.
How wrong, how often?Current error rate, and how errors are caught today. Often the answer is "they aren't" — that is a finding.
What does an error cost?A rework hour? A wasted truck roll? A miscoded fatal crash that skews HSIP rankings for five years?
If your partner does not measure these, say so and explain how you estimated them. A team that reports "the district does not track this; we timed four cases ourselves and got 22–31 minutes" is doing the work correctly. A team that reports a confident number it invented is not.

Choosing the comparison

Your baseline is the tuned conventional method, not "nothing" and not "a smaller neural network."

  • Beating a manual process → compare on time and agreement with the human's answer.
  • Beating a classical model → tune it properly first. A well-fit IDM, a calibrated HCM procedure, or a negative-binomial SPF is a serious opponent.
  • Beating a keyword search → it is a strong baseline for retrieval, and it is free.

In this course a tuned classical baseline is worth more to your grade than a second neural architecture. That is not a rhetorical position; it is how the rubric works.

Five ways an AI workflow fails

  • Silent failure. It returns a fluent, plausible, wrong answer with no error message. Classical code crashes; models improvise.
  • Distribution shift. Fine on the clear-day daytime footage you tested; useless at night, in rain, in a work zone, or on the district that paints its markings differently.
  • Automation bias. The human checkpoint becomes a rubber stamp by week three. Design against this: show the reviewer the evidence, not just the conclusion.
  • Cost at scale. Fine for 50 frames, unaffordable for 50,000. Get the cost per case early — this is a design constraint, not an afterthought.
  • Governance. Video of identifiable people, addresses, employee records. Ask what may leave the building before you build around a cloud API. Identifiable data means instructor approval and IRB consultation before any collection.

The two questions that end a weak project early

"And then what?" — Your workflow outputs a number, a label, a ranked list. Who receives it, and what do they do differently? If the honest answer is "nothing," the problem is the framing, not the model.

"How would you know if it got worse?" — Six months after you graduate, the camera is repositioned, the vendor updates the model, the reporting form changes. What in your workflow notices? If nothing notices, you have built a demonstration, not a workflow.

What you already know how to do

You may be the only person in the room who has not trained a neural network from scratch, and it is worth being clear about how little that matters here. The scarce skill in an AI workflow is not building the model. It is knowing which step is safe to automate, what the output should look like when it is right, and how to prove it. All three are transportation questions.

What your program gave youWhat it buys you in an AI workflow
Traffic flow theory
fundamental diagram, shockwaves, capacity, car-following		 You know what a physically impossible output looks like. Speed rising with density, flow above capacity, a 0.4 s headway held for a minute, a "predicted" queue that discharges faster than saturation flow — these are lie detectors you can code as automatic checks on any model's output. A generative or predictive model has no physics; you supply it. This is the single most transferable thing you own.
Statistics & econometrics
sampling, inference, confounding, model selection		 You know that accuracy on a test set is not evidence of impact, that the test set has to be split by site or by day and not at random, that a rare-event problem needs more than a confusion matrix, and that "we improved from 0.81 to 0.83" with n=60 is noise. You will design the evaluation while everyone else designs the model.
Travel demand modeling
four-step, calibration, validation targets		 You have already built, calibrated, and defended a multi-stage pipeline whose stages feed each other and whose errors compound. That is workflow engineering. You also know what a validation target is and what it costs to miss one.
Traffic simulation
SUMO, VISSIM, CORSIM, CARLA		 Two things nobody else brings: you can build the environment an agent loops against, and you can generate labeled data for events too rare or too dangerous to collect in the field. Half the "we have no labels" problems in this course are solvable in a simulator by someone who knows one.
Design standards & manuals
HCM, MUTCD, AASHTO Green Book, FDOT Design Manual, agency SOPs		 Domain rules become three concrete things: the specification the workflow must satisfy, the retrieval corpus a language model reads from, and the acceptance test that catches an answer which is fluent but out of policy. You can tell the difference between a plausible answer and a compliant one. A model cannot.
Safety analysis
SPFs, HSM, crash typing, countermeasure selection		 You understand asymmetric error costs and liability. That is precisely the reasoning that places the human checkpoint and defines "AI must not act alone here." You also know the difference between correlation in a crash database and a countermeasure that works.
Field data collection & sensors
counts, probes, GPS, CAN, video, lidar		 You know what dirty data looks like before a model does — dropouts, clock drift, unit errors, a detector stuck at 255, a GPS point in the Gulf of Mexico. You also know what personally identifiable information you are holding and what that obliges you to do.
ITS & TSMO operations
ATMS, work orders, districts, procurement		 You know the system of record, who signs, what a truck roll costs, and why a workflow that cannot write into the existing database will never be used no matter how good it is. Deployment reality is a competitive advantage in this room.
Programming, GIS, databases
Python, R, pandas, spatial joins, SQL		 The glue. Realistically, 70% of the code in your project is ingest, joins, schema wrangling, and plumbing between an API and a dataframe. This is the part AI coding assistants help with most — and the part where an assistant's silent mistake does the most damage, because it looks like it worked.

memos, TRB papers, public meetings		 The real output of most workflows is a document someone signs or presents. Knowing the format, the audience, and what a reviewer will attack is the difference between a result and a result that gets used.

How the two halves meet

The recurring failure in AI-for-transportation work is a technically excellent system solving a problem no agency has, evaluated against nothing, deployed nowhere. The mirror-image failure is a real problem attacked with a method the team does not understand well enough to know when it is lying.

You are being asked to hold both ends: bring the problem and the judgment, learn enough of the method to be dangerous, and never stop asking what the result is compared against. That combination is rarer than either half, and it is what the advisory committee is coming to evaluate on December 3.

From workflow to project

You will see a set of candidate topics in a moment, and you may modify one or propose your own. Either way, run the idea through these five questions before you commit. They take ten minutes and they save weeks.

Five questions

1Whose job is this? Name a person or a role — a district maintenance engineer, a signal timing engineer, a transit planner, a PhD student in your lab. If you cannot name anyone, you have a topic, not a workflow.

2Can you watch it happen once? An hour of watching someone do the work beats a week of assuming. Ask them to narrate. The pain point is almost never the step you guessed.

3Do you have the four baseline numbers? How long, how often, how wrong, what an error costs. If not, getting them is your first deliverable.

4Can you label every step? Extraction, classification, generation, retrieval, prediction, or judgment — and at least one marked "AI must not act alone here."

5Can you get the data before October? Data access is what kills projects, not modeling. Topics are confirmed Sept 17 and presentations are Dec 3. Confirm access before you commit, not after.

Scope tests

  • The two-step rule. Automate two steps well rather than eight badly. A workflow where AI helps with two of six steps, with evidence, beats a full pipeline nobody can check.
  • The ugly-case rule. Before you build, find the ten cases that will break it — night, rain, a form filled out wrong, a segment with no ID. If you cannot name ten, you do not understand the work yet.
  • The demo rule. A working narrow thing beats an ambitious broken thing. Every time.
  • The integration rule. Teams that split by component and never integrate discover the integration problems in the last week. Integrate early with placeholder pieces.

Your first artifact — do this before you write anything else

One page. Boxes and arrows. For every box: what comes in, what goes out, and who owns it today. Put a star ★ at the first irreversible action. Shade the boxes you believe a machine could do, and write one sentence under each shaded box saying how you would know it was wrong.

If you can draw that page for a real job that a real person does, you have a project. Everything else this semester is learning how to fill the shaded boxes — and learning to defend the ones you left unshaded.
