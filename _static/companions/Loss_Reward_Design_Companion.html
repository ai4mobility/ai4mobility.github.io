Loss and Reward Design — how you tell a model what you want

Backpropagation does not know or care what problem you are solving. The loss is the only place in the whole system where what you actually want enters — and the machinery will optimize exactly what you wrote there, including the parts you wrote by accident. Move the two knobs that matter (δ and the class weight), then watch what happens when the grader is no longer a formula you can write down at all. Every number on this page was computed in numpy by loss_reward_companion_data.py and asserted against the lecture slides before it was baked in — nothing here is estimated in your browser.

ρ_MSE(r) = ½ r² ψ(r) = r grows without limit ρ_MAE(r) = |r| ψ(r) = sign(r) every record pulls the same ρ_Huber(r) = ½r² if |r| ≤ δ ψ(r) = clip(r, ±δ) squared near zero, flat past δ δ(|r| − ½δ) otherwise

MSE is drawn as ½r² so all three pulls agree for a small residual; ½r² and r² have the same minimizer, so nothing about the fit changes.

Read the right panel, not the left. The penalty tells you what a record costs. The slope tells you what it does to the answer — and a squared penalty says a 25 mph outlier deserves 25× the pull of a 1 mph one, forever. That single line is why the next tab happens.
Going deeper — the pull is literally the fitting weight

Fitting any of these by iteratively reweighted least squares uses the weight w(r) = ψ(r)/r. For squared error that is a constant: every bin counts the same, which is the assumption. For absolute error it is 1/|r|: a bin 20 mph out counts a twentieth as much as one 1 mph out. Huber switches between the two at δ. So "which loss" and "which records do I believe" are the same question asked twice — and Huber answers it without you having to label the bad records in advance.

Set it to the measurement noise and it does what you want; set it to the size of the outliers and you have written squared error the long way round.

Going deeper — why accuracy stops being a metric here

At a 21.1% base rate the majority rule already scores 79%, so accuracy is mostly a measurement of the base rate rather than of the model. Balanced accuracy averages recall on each class, so the majority rule scores exactly 50% and the number becomes informative again. Neither replaces the operational pair a TMC actually argues about: breakdowns caught per week, and false alarms per day. Report all four.

The reward is a coin flip, and gradient descent finds the best phase anyway

Four phases. Serve one, and the queue either clears or it does not — the reward is 0 or 1, a Bernoulli draw, about as non-differentiable as a signal gets. The controller is not told the clearance probabilities and never estimates the environment. It only ever multiplies that 0 or 1 into the gradient of its own log-probability. Press play.

∇θ J(θ) = E[ r · ∇θ log πθ(a | s) ] J = average reward per decision · r = what the intersection handed back · π(a|s) = the probability the policy gave the action it took In words: r is never differentiated. It is a scalar that scales the gradient of the log-probability of the action you actually tried.
Why this works at all. With a deterministic controller a 0/1 reward gives a flat objective — zero gradient everywhere, nothing to descend. Randomizing the choice is what makes J(θ) smooth, because J is then a weighted average of rewards with the policy's own probabilities as the weights, and those are differentiable. The intersection stays as jagged as it likes.
And what it costs — count the decisions, not the spread. It took 800 tries to sort four options, and 40 seeds still land anywhere in —, recovering about — of the gap between choosing at random (504) and knowing the answer (760). A supervised learner with labels would need four. Nothing was biased; the direction was simply inferred from the correlation between "how likely was that action" and "how did it go" rather than read off a label. That is variance, and it is why RL burns millions of samples and why almost all of it happens in simulation.
Going deeper — why the reward never needs a derivative

Write the objective as an expectation over trajectories τ drawn from pθ and use ∇p = p ∇log p:

∇E[R(τ)] = ∫ ∇pθ(τ) R(τ) dτ = ∫ pθ(τ) ∇log pθ(τ) R(τ) dτ

Since pθ(τ) = p(s0) ∏ p(st+1|st,at) · πθ(at|st) and the transitions do not depend on θ, they vanish from the gradient, leaving only the sum of ∇log π. R survives as a plain multiplier, so it may be an indicator, a measurement, or a human thumbs-up.
