PCA is the simplest autoencoder

Each grey dot is one 5-minute reading from two detectors 0.73 mi apart on I-405 North (PeMS, weekdays of May and June 2012; every 6th of the 12,672 readings). The encoder keeps one number per reading, z: how far along the gold line its shadow falls. The decoder walks that far along the line to rebuild both speeds.

Turn the line with the angle slider. Drag the green probe anywhere in the plot. Move the z slider to decode any z you like.

The encoder and the decoder

encoder: z = u · (x − m)
decoder: y = m + z u

m is the mean reading and u is a unit vector along the line. That is the whole autoencoder.

z depends on the input

Drag the probe. Every input gets its own z: its shadow on the line. All points on one perpendicular share a shadow, so they share a z and rebuild to the same y. What sits off the line is lost.

Nearby z, nearby y

Any z decodes to a point on the line, even a z no reading has. Moving z by Δ moves y by exactly Δ mph along the line, everywhere. A linear decoder is a ruler.

What PCA chooses

Press Snap to PCA. PCA picks the angle with the smallest error: 45.8° here, keeping 95.9% of the variance with 4.41 mph RMS error per station. It cannot keep the gap between the stations. Put the probe at (70, 18) mph, when the back of a queue sits between the detectors, and see how far off the rebuild is.

  1. Turn the line to 135°. Why is the error so large, and what does z measure now?
  2. Two readings have the same z. Must they be the same traffic state? Use the probe to find a pair that are not.

Watch a two-layer network learn PCA

The same 2,112 readings, now fed to a network with one hidden unit: z = h(w₁·x + b₁) and y = w₂ z + b₂, with speeds scaled as x = (speed − 50)/25. Training is gradient descent (the Adam version) on the rebuild loss ½‖y − x‖². It starts from the weights used in the handout's step-by-step example.

Why each reading has its own z

z is computed from x with the current weights. Every training step changes w₁ and b₁, so every reading's code moves. Watch the histogram drift and reshape while the error falls. A code is not stored anywhere; it is recomputed from the input each time.

Same rebuild, different codes

Train once with tanh and once with no activation. Both end at PCA's error, and both decoder lines sit on PCA's line. Yet the codes end up on very different scales: in our runs, between −0.24 and 0.23 with tanh, and between −2.41 and 1.48 with no activation. Nothing in the loss decides where z should live: only the rebuild is scored.

Two hidden units copy

Give the network 2 hidden units, as many as inputs. The error falls almost to zero because it simply copies x. A near-perfect score that teaches nothing about the road.

The encoder paints a number on the plane

The background colour is the encoder at work: every spot gets a code z. Readings in the same colour band share a code, so they rebuild to the same point. Scrub the training: the bands start as straight stripes (almost PCA) and bend as the network learns the two branches.

The decoder draws one curve

Slide z and y moves along the curve. Nearby z give nearby y because the decoder is a smooth function. But the same Δz moves y much further where the curve is stretched: look at the lower chart near the peak.

Codes nobody uses

The codes the training readings use come in clumps with gaps between them (shaded in the histogram). The dashed parts of the curve decode codes in those gaps and beyond the ends. The decoder still returns something there, but nothing anchors it to real traffic: watch the dashed loops cut across the diagram. These are the holes that make sampling risky (tab 4).

The code is the network's own coordinate

Switch runs A, B and C. Each lays out z differently: a different range, a different direction, a different place for free flow. Their flow errors on held-out readings are 91, 59 and 46 veh/h. None of these codes is density.

Why a plain autoencoder cannot generate

Nothing in its loss says where z should live, so its codes have their own range and lumpy shape (tab 2 showed the scale is arbitrary). Draw from N(0, 1) and the samples crowd one part of the curve. Fit a bell to the codes and some draws fall where no code lives, so the decoder extrapolates off the diagram.

What the VAE changes

1. A soft code. The encoder outputs a centre μ(x) and a spread σ(x) for each reading: a small bell of plausible codes.

2. Noise in training. The decoder is trained on a random draw z = μ + σ·ε, with ε from N(0, 1). Every code near μ must rebuild well, so neighbouring codes decode to neighbouring states. Writing the draw this way is the reparameterization trick: the randomness sits in ε, so gradients still reach μ and σ.

3. A price for leaving the prior. The loss adds β times the KL divergence between N(μ, σ²) and N(0, 1). It grows when a code drifts far from 0 or shrinks to a point.

The prior is an assumption we choose

p(z) = N(0, 1) says: before seeing any traffic, a code is a standard bell draw. Training makes the codes agree with it. Generation is then two lines: draw z from N(0, 1), decode it.

loss = ½‖y − x‖²
+ β · ½(μ² + σ² − 1 − ln σ²)

What the numbers say

Run A's codes have a spread of 2.55, and only 37% fall within ±2. On 3,000 samples, its N(0, 1) draws miss the real mix of densities by 45%. That is the share of samples you would have to move to match the data's density histogram. Fitting a bell to its codes, 16% of samples land far from the curve. The VAE with β = 0.03 misses the mix by 5%, with 5% far away. It still under-produces free flow: 9% of its samples, against 18% of real readings.

Going deeper (optional): where the KL term comes from

A generative model would ideally make the real data as likely as possible: maximise log p(x). For a VAE that cannot be computed, but it has a lower bound, the evidence lower bound (ELBO): log p(x) ≥ E[log p(x | z)] − KL(q(z | x) ‖ p(z)). The first term becomes the rebuild error when the decoder is read as a bell curve around y. The second is the KL term above, written for a bell q(z | x) = N(μ, σ²) against the prior N(0, 1). β weighs the two; β = 1 is the textbook VAE, and other values give a β-VAE. Sources: Kingma and Welling (2014); Bishop and Bishop (2024), section 19.2.

β is a dial. At β = 0.003 the code centres spread wider than the bell (spread 1.13). At β = 0.1 they sit inside it, but the flow error grows to 118 veh/h, against 60 at β = 0.003.

Where the codes sit

Only 11.7% of the autoencoder's training codes fall inside the radius-2 circle, where 86.5% of bell draws land. For the VAE, 94.6% do. The autoencoder's codes also spread far wider: about 2.8 and 5.0 units along the two axes, against about 1 for the VAE.

So bell draws mean different things

Through the autoencoder, bell draws hit one small part of its cloud and decode to similar heatmaps: two draws differ by a median 12.6 mph. Through the VAE they spread over the cloud: 24.7 mph apart, close to the 28.5 mph between two real test heatmaps.

Nearby codes, nearby heatmaps

Walk between two codes. In both models small steps give small changes, because both decoders are smooth. But the autoencoder's path can cross space no training heatmap uses, so nothing guarantees those middle frames look like real traffic.

The price

The VAE rebuilds a little worse (7.68 against 7.29 mph RMS per cell on the test heatmaps), and its samples are slightly blurred: the decoder returns an average of the states that share a code. The VAE handout, section 8, measures this.

Recap

  1. An autoencoder squeezes x into a code z and rebuilds y. With a linear decoder, the best it can do is PCA (tabs 1 and 2).
  2. z is computed from the input with the current weights. Every reading gets its own code, and every training step moves all of them (tab 2).
  3. The decoder turns any z into a y. Nearby z give nearby y because the decoder is smooth, but how far y moves depends on where you are on the curve (tabs 1 and 3).
  4. Depth bends the line. Different runs lay out z differently, so a code is a coordinate the network chose, not a measurement (tab 3).
  5. A plain autoencoder never says where z should live, so you cannot safely draw new codes from it (tabs 2, 4 and 5).
  6. The VAE adds a prior p(z) = N(0, I), soft codes μ ± σ, noise in training, and a KL price. Its codes fill the bell, so a bell draw decodes to a plausible traffic state (tabs 4 and 5).
