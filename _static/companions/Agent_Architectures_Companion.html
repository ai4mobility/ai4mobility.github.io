Agent architectures: one loop, four questions

Blog posts list 8 or 11 “agent architectures,” and no two lists agree. Most of the named “architectures” answer one of four design questions about a single loop: how the agent picks its next step, what it can reach, how many agents work together, and where a person approves. This page shows the loop, sorts eight published lists onto the four questions, helps you choose the answers for a transportation task, shows what has been built in transportation and what agencies ask for, and measures what one choice changes on a small agent that retimes a signal in SUMO.

Every agent runs one loop

The model reads its context and writes the next step as text. The harness, the code around the model, runs any tool call and adds the result to the context. The loop repeats until the model writes a final answer or hits a limit. Every pattern on this page is a choice about some part of this loop.

Doing the sorting worksheet in class? Finish it before you open tabs 2 and 3: they show where the blog posts' labels land.
Terms used on this page
Token
A piece of a word. Models read and write text as tokens; context limits and cost are counted in tokens.
Context window
Everything the model reads in one call: the instructions, the tool descriptions, the task, and every earlier step and result. It has a size limit in tokens.
Harness
The ordinary code around the model. It sends the context to the model, runs the tool calls the model writes, enforces limits, and pastes the results back.
Schema
The description of a tool that the model reads: its name, what it does, and the names and types of its inputs.
MCP (Model Context Protocol)
An open standard for offering tools and data to any agent through a server. SUMO-MCP is an MCP server for SUMO.
TraCI
SUMO's Traffic Control Interface: a way for a program to read and change a running simulation, step by step.
LoRA
Low-rank adaptation: a cheap way to fine-tune a large model by training small added matrices instead of all its weights. LLMLight uses it.
UniTSA
A reinforcement-learning signal controller that LA-Light uses as a comparison and as one of its tools.
Leverage labels
The six labels from Section 5.1 for what AI adds to a step: extraction, classification, generation, retrieval, prediction, judgment. They appear on the pattern cards. Judgment is not a step you can hand over.

Look down the Q4 column. All six act only in simulation or on stored data, and none puts a
     person's approval in front of an action. The human steps are “ask when information is missing,” advice only, or
     stopping a learning loop. Agency rules (below) require a person in the process. An agent design with a real answer to Q4,
     tested with real operators, is an open research gap.

From agency documents and research projects found in November 2026.

  

    
FDOT: questions over the Standard Specifications

      
A research project (2025–2026) testing a language-model system that answers questions from FDOT's
      construction specifications. It measures how accurately the system cites its sources, how often it makes things up,
      and how it handles industry jargon. Q2: documents. TRB RiP

Humans are fully responsible for the work and product outputs involving AI.

      
We must disclose if the products are generated partially or fully with AI assistance.

      
Quoted in an article by FDOT's Chief Engineer of Operations; the policy text itself was not found online.

AI usage must engage humans throughout the process, with human involvement in reviews and decisions.

      
and a ban on using “AI applications not approved by the Department” on its systems or data.
      The summary notes that it does not reflect FDOT's concurrence.

TxDOT AI Strategic Plan, January 2026 update

      
AI-assisted workflows must include a mandatory validation of outputs. A “Human-in-the-Loop” (HITL) requirement ensures that professional judgment, ethical considerations, and institutional context always anchor AI-driven outcomes before they are finalized or implemented.

      
The same update lists chatbots over manuals and the state administrative code, a pilot of a vendor agent platform on the complaint
      system, and “Agentic and Multi-Agentic Systems” as a coming trend.
      TxDOT PDF

    
MassDOT: HEKA, the “Highway Engineer Knowledge Agent”

      
Launched November 2024 to let design engineers query department manuals; early testing reported a 78%
      time saving over manual search. Called an agent, built as retrieval over documents (Q2), which is what most agencies need first.
      Burnes Center

  

  
The pattern so far. Agencies use retrieval over their own documents and fixed workflows, with a person who validates
     the output. Agent platforms are starting to arrive through vendors, for back-office work such as complaints. In the documents found,
     no agency lets an open-ended or multi-agent system run traffic operations; that remains research.







  
Measured: three ways to pick the next step

  
Same small model (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct on a CPU), same synthetic intersection, same timing limits enforced by the
     harness, same limit of 10 model calls per run. Step by step and check-then-retry run Section 5.2's agent unchanged, with its three tools.
     Plan first needs more than a new instruction: it gets a fourth tool (write_plan), its own system prompt, and a
     harness that opens its first plan call. A timing that breaks a limit is rejected by the harness, not simulated. Each style ran with the same 10 model seeds (0–9).

  

    
Step by step
The model picks every tool call itself (Section 5.2's condition C).

    
Plan first
After looking up the intersection, the model lists up to five timings in one write_plan call. The harness simulates every timing within the limits and returns all the delays at once; then the model submits, or tests more.

  

  
Check, then retry
As step by step, plus an outside check at submission: the harness accepts only a timing that was simulated and beats the current one, and otherwise sends the agent back with the reason (Section 5.2's condition D).

“Better” means a lower delay than the current timing 50/30 (21.5 s/veh), on the same SUMO seed.

  
What got submitted. A timing better than the current one (21.5 s/veh): 4 of 10 runs step by step, 6 of 10 plan first, 4 of 10 check-then-retry. Runs that ended with nothing submitted: 0, 2 and 6.
How many timings got tested. Per run, on average: 1.1 step by step, 5.1 plan first, 2.7 check-then-retry. In 24 of 30 runs the model never simulated the current timing 50/30, the one it was asked to improve on; only the check-then-retry harness compares against it, and it measures 50/30 itself.
Timings that broke a limit. 2, 107 and 7 in total. The harness rejected each one without simulating it.
What it cost. Model calls per run: 3.4, 5.6 and 7.7. Tokens read per run: 2,774, 9,845 and 10,055. Wall time per run (median): 62, 158 and 207 s on two CPU cores. The machine ran other jobs at the same time, so wall time is rough; tokens are the steadier measure of cost.
What the check bought. Every timing it let through (4 of 10 runs) beat the current one, which is what the check requires. The price: 6 runs ended with nothing, after 29 rejected submissions in all.
Is the difference real? Plan first returned a better timing most often: 6 of 10, against the next best 4 of 10. If the styles were equally good, a split at least this uneven would still turn up with probability 0.66 (Fisher’s exact test). Ten runs per style cannot settle which style is better.

  
What the plan-first harness needed. On its first try the model wrote an empty list, then lists cut off mid-way, and never
     simulated anything. The harness now writes the opening of the first list (as it already writes “Thought:”) and reads every complete
     timing in it. A pattern is only as good as the harness that runs it, and small models write lists badly.

Before you trust these results.
    What is the baseline? The current timing, and a plain grid search with no model that tested 315 timings. The grid is coarse (steps of 4 s north-south, 3 s east-west): 2 runs submitted 35/21 at 18.4, a timing between its points, 0.1 s/veh below its best. On one SUMO seed, a gap that small may not be real. Missing: Webster's method,
    the classical formula an engineer would try first (TrafficGPT gives its agent a Webster tool).
    How was the data split, and why is that honest? There is no split. One synthetic intersection was used both to build the plan-first
    harness (after its first attempt failed) and to score all three styles, with one SUMO seed and 10 model seeds per style, in one
    software environment. So this compares the styles on one task; it says nothing about other intersections, other models, or a city.
    The runs in Section 5.2 were recorded in another environment, so single runs differ.
    What does it do on the ugly cases? Count the squares and the dots on or right of the red line: runs that submitted nothing, or a timing no better than the one already in the field.







  
Check yourself

  
Judgment questions. Answer each one in two or three sentences before you open it.

  
1. A district wants engineers to ask questions about its Standard Specifications and get answers with the page cited. Workflow or agent? Which Q2 answer, and where is the check?
Start with a workflow: retrieve, answer, cite. Q2 is documents (RAG). The check is the citation: an engineer can open the page. Move to an agent only if questions need several lookups or other tools, and test the retriever on its own first.
2. Your plan-first agent writes five test plans before it has looked at the intersection. What goes wrong, and what is the fix?
The plan is written blind, so it can miss the limits and the volumes. Look up first, then plan (as the plan arm in tab 6 does), and let the harness reject plans that break a limit.
3. A lab mate proposes a reflection loop in which the model rereads its own crash-narrative summary and fixes the errors. What do you ask?
What is the outside check? A model grading itself gains little or loses (Huang et al., 2024). Grade against a labeled sample or an analyst instead, and count how often the revision changes a right answer into a wrong one.
4. When does splitting a four-corridor retiming study across four corridor agents pay off, and when does it not?
It pays off when the corridors do not interact and can run in parallel. It does not when queues spill from one corridor into another, because each worker sees only its piece. Price it too: multi-agent systems used about 15× the tokens of a chat in Anthropic's report.
5. Post P2 says an Autonomous Loop lets you set a goal and stop watching. What must change before it touches a TMC's systems?
Read-only access by default; a gate before every write (sign, timing, work order); a step and budget limit; a log of every action. Then it is no longer “stop watching”: Q4 has a real answer.
6. Read the measured results in tab 6. Which control style would you pick for this retiming task, and what evidence is still missing?
Pick from the numbers on the page, and say which row decided it: better plans, fewer failed runs, or lower cost. Then name what this test cannot tell you. It used one synthetic intersection, one SUMO seed and one small model, and it has no train/test split: the same intersection was used to design the harness and to score it. Missing: other demands and intersections, a second SUMO seed (is a 0.4 s gain real?), other models, and the classical baseline an engineer would try first, Webster's method.
