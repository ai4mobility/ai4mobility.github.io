Learning traffic dynamics: Newell's shift and the LWR shockwave as convolutions

Module 7 keeps asking one question: can a learned model stand in for a simulator? This page answers it for the two simplest simulators we have — Newell's car-following rule and the cell transmission model — by showing that each one already is a convolution. Everything here runs live in your browser, including a real trained network with 1,217 weights.

A car-following model is a picture you can slide

Newell's simplified car-following model says something almost embarrassingly simple: in congestion, a follower's trajectory is its leader's trajectory, moved — later in time by a reaction-and-response time τ, and back in space by a jam spacing δ. In words: copy the leader's trajectory, shift it right by τ and down by δ, and cap it at free-flow speed. That is the whole model. Two parameters per driver.

Why this tab exists. Hold on to the word shift. A shift applied identically at every point of a picture, using the same small set of numbers everywhere, is the exact thing a convolution layer does — and it is the reason the rest of this page works.

Newell is one convolution tap plus a bias

Write the follower's position as a weighted sum of the leader's recent positions — a causal 1-D convolution over the leader's trajectory. Newell's model is not approximated by that layer. It is one specific setting of its weights: a single 1 at the tap that equals τ, zeros everywhere else, and a bias of −δ. Fit the layer to a simulated platoon below and watch it find them.

a causal 1-D convolution over the leader's trajectory, K taps of Δt each x̂ₙ(t) = Σₖ₌₀..ₖ₋₁ wₖ·xₙ₋₁(t − k·Δt) + b Newell is the setting wₖ = 1 at k = τ/Δt, 0 elsewhere b = −δ and the free-flow cap is the activation, because min(a, b) = a − ReLU(a − b)
Read the kernel as a clock, not as a filter. The position of the weight is the reaction time. The height of the weight is how much of the leader's motion is passed through — 1.0 means all of it. The bias is the space you keep. A student who can read those three things off a plot can read any 1-D convolution in this course.

Offline check, run once and recorded. With identical drivers (τ = 1.2 s, δ = 7 m, Δt = 0.1 s) an exact least-squares fit of the same 30-tap layer returns w₁₂ = 1.0000, every other tap summing to 1.9 × 10⁻¹¹, and bias −7.000 m. With 30 heterogeneous drivers sharing one kernel, the trained layer recovers a mean lag of 1.169 s against a true 1.170 s, an sd of 0.299 against 0.295, and a bias of −7.191 m against a true mean δ of 7.190 m. The reproducer is exp1_newell_conv.py.
The trap, and it is worth your time. Fit those 30 taps by unconstrained least squares and you get nonsense — taps of −0.78, +1.66, −1.03 sitting next to each other, and ±1.4 at the far end. Nothing is wrong with the optimiser. A vehicle's position at adjacent lags is almost the same number, so the columns of the design matrix are nearly collinear. Constraining the kernel to be a distribution over lags — non-negative, summing to one — makes it readable, and then the honest accounting is this: across all 36 combinations of the sliders above, the fitted kernel recovers the mean lag to within 0.003 s and the bias to within 0.1 ft, while its spread is not trustworthy — it reports 0.07–0.11 s of width even when every driver is identical, and lands anywhere from 0.07 to 0.33 s when the true spread is 0.26 s. The mean and the intercept are identified by this data; the shape of the distribution is not. That is a calibration result about car-following data, and it applies to any model you fit to trajectories — neural or not.
Going deeper — why the sum of the taps must be 1, and what the bias absorbs

Put a leader travelling at a constant speed v into the layer: xn−1(t − kΔt) = xn−1(t) − v·kΔt. The output is (Σk wk)·xn−1(t) − v·Δt·Σk k·wk + b. For the follower to travel at the same speed rather than drift away, the first coefficient must be exactly 1. So Σ wk = 1 is not a regulariser you chose, it is the steady-state condition of a car-following model, and enforcing it costs nothing. The second term is then v·τ̄ where τ̄ = Σ k·wk·Δt is the kernel's mean lag, so the equilibrium spacing is v·τ̄ + δ — the linear spacing–speed relation whose inverse is the congested branch of a triangular fundamental diagram. The bias absorbs δ alone because it is the only term that does not scale with speed.

Newell, G. F. (2002). A simplified car-following theory: a lower order model. Transportation Research Part B, 36(3), 195–205.

The same argument, one dimension up: the CTM step is a residual conv block

Go from trajectories to the density field and the shift argument survives intact. The cell transmission model updates every cell from itself and its two neighbours, with the same rule everywhere. Local, plus position-independent, is precisely the pair of assumptions a convolution layer encodes.

CTM (Daganzo 1994) — one Godunov step on a triangular fundamental diagram sending Sᵢ = min( u·kᵢ , Qmax ) receiving Rᵢ = min( Qmax , w·(Kj − kᵢ) ) interface yᵢ = min( Sᵢ₋₁ , Rᵢ ) = min( u·kᵢ₋₁ , Qmax , w·(Kj − kᵢ) ) update kᵢ(n+1) = kᵢ(n) + (Δt/Δx)·( yᵢ − yᵢ₊₁ ) every quantity on the right comes from cells i−1, i, i+1 → a 3-tap convolution in space a min of three affine functions is two nested ReLUs → the nonlinearity the k(n) carried through → the skip connection

The block that represents it exactly

k(n) ──┬─ conv3 (1→32) ─ ReLU ─ conv1 ─ ReLU ─ conv1 ─┐ │ + ─→ k(n+1) └────────────────────────────────────────┘

This is not an analogy. min(a,b) = a − ReLU(a−b), applied twice, turns the three-way min into two ReLU layers; the 3-tap convolution supplies ki−1, ki, ki+1; and the skip connection carries the conservation law. Any exact CTM step can be written in this block, and the block trained below finds it from data.

The skip connection is mass conservation. Predicting the change in density rather than the density itself is not an optimisation trick here — it is the statement that vehicles are neither created nor destroyed inside a cell, and everything the network has to learn is the flux across two boundaries.

In the time–space image, the rule is a shift

Where the value at a cell comes from one step earlier. In free flow it is copied from upstream at u = 75 mi/hr, which at this discretisation is exactly one cell per step. In congestion it is copied from downstream at w = 15 mi/hr — 0.2 cells per step, one cell every five. Which of the two applies is decided by the state itself, and that decision is the min.

The shock speed is arithmetic, not simulation. s = (q2 − q1)/(k2 − k1) — the slope of the chord between the two states on the fundamental diagram. Any model that claims to predict queue growth has to reproduce this number, and it is the first thing to check on a learned one.

What "measure the wave speeds" does. It puts a small density bump on an otherwise uniform road, rolls the network forward twice — once with the bump and once without — and tracks the centroid of the difference. That difference is the perturbation, isolated. On a free-flow background the trained block moves it at +74.7 mi/hr against the model's +75.0; on a congested background, at −15.4 against −15.0. Nobody told it either number. It read both off the data, and they are the two characteristic speeds of the fundamental diagram.
Do the honest version of this measurement. Tracking the bump's centroid against the nominal background instead of against a second rollout gave +127 mi/hr in congestion — not because the network was wrong, but because 0.4 veh/mi of harmless background drift spread over 80 cells outweighs a 15 veh·cell bump. A difference of two rollouts cancels the drift exactly. Most "the model learned the physics" claims are one careless baseline away from this.

Switch the boundary to "frozen" and watch what happens

With the boundary cells supplied each step, the rollout holds 0.20 veh/mi while the exit is blocked and 1.38 after it clears — 0.85 over the whole run — and its queue tail tracks the same cells as the truth, at −11.45 mi/hr. Now set the boundary to frozen. The error stays at 0.35 veh/mi right up to 7.6 minutes, the moment the exit clears in the truth, and then jumps to 35.6. The network keeps propagating the queue upstream at very nearly the right speed (−12.11 against −11.45). It simply has no way to know the incident ended, because nothing in the interior of a corridor carries that information.

This is the single most useful thing on this page. A learned traffic model is an operator: give it a state and boundary conditions and it will step them forward faithfully. It is not a forecast. Any corridor forecast you build is only as good as the demand and capacity forecast you feed it, and a model that looks superb on historical replay — where the boundaries are known — can be useless in real time, where they are not. Ask any vendor of a predictive traffic product which of the two they benchmarked.

Where it breaks

Three failure modes, in the order you will meet them on a real project. None of them is a bug; each one is a property of the assumption that makes convolution work in the first place.

1 · Change the road and the weights are wrong

The block was trained at w = 15 mi/hr. The wave speed is not an input to it — it is baked into the weights, the same way a calibrated Wiedemann parameter set is baked into a VISSIM model. Move the trained network to a corridor with different driver behaviour and it degrades hard, while the CTM simply takes the new parameter.

Units veh/mi/ln. A 3 mi/hr change in wave speed costs a factor of 13 in RMSE. Note also that at w = 20 the tail speed is recovered but the field is not: a single headline metric can look fine while the estimate is bad, which is why two of these columns exist.

Which is the better deal? The CTM has three parameters you can measure from a week of detector data. The network has 1,217 you must retrain per corridor. The network buys you the ability to learn dynamics you cannot write down — lane changing, bounded acceleration, capacity drop, driver heterogeneity — and it buys you speed at inference. If the dynamics you need are already in the CTM, the CTM wins on every axis that matters.

2 · Translation invariance is exactly what a bottleneck violates

A convolution applies the same weights at every location. That is why it needs so few parameters, and it is true of car-following and of the LWR update on a homogeneous segment. It is false the moment the road stops being the same everywhere: a lane drop, a merge, an upgrade, a work zone, a signal. The pattern "a shockwave" is position-independent; the pattern "a bottleneck at milepost 12.3" is not.

The fix is a channel, not a bigger network. Feed geometry in as extra input channels aligned to the same grid — lanes, capacity, grade, ramp presence, signal state — so the convolution keeps sharing weights over space while the input tells it where the road changes. This is exactly how a CNN handles a colour image: same kernel, three channels. A network that must memorise where the bottlenecks are has lost the property you chose it for.

3 · The receptive field has to cover the domain of dependence

Information in LWR travels along characteristics: downstream at u in free flow, upstream at w in congestion. So the state at a point 12 minutes from now depends on a wedge of the current field — at this discretisation, 150 cells (15 miles) of road upstream and 30 cells (3 miles) downstream. If your network's receptive field is smaller than that wedge, no amount of training fixes it; the information is not in the input.

a stack of 3-tap convolutions reaches 1 cell per layer reach after L layers = L cells need 150 cells upstream → 150 layers the recurrent form gets it for free one 3-tap block, applied 150 times = reach 150, and 1,217 weights total

This is the argument for rolling one small block forward in time rather than building one deep feed-forward CNN that maps the present to the future in a single shot. It is also the argument for anisotropic kernels: the wedge is not square, so a square 3×3 stack spends most of its parameters on space–time locations that carry no information. Thodi et al. restrict the kernel's support to two bands, one along each characteristic, and the parameter count then grows linearly rather than quadratically with kernel width.

Before you trust this result

QuestionAnswer for the result on tab 4
What is the baseline?The CTM itself, with three parameters, which is exact by construction. The network's job was never to beat it — it was to show that the operator is learnable from data. Against a "persistence" baseline (hold the field fixed) the rollout is far better; against the CTM it is worse, and the honest headline is 0.85 veh/mi of error where the correct answer is 0.
How was the data split, and why is that honest?Trained on 400 randomly generated scenarios; tested on one scenario built by hand — uniform free-flow start, a 35% capacity drop at a fixed time — that is not in the training distribution's typical range. The split is by scenario, not by time step, because splitting a single simulation into train and test time steps would leak the answer: neighbouring steps of one run are nearly the same field.
What does it do on the ugly cases?It fails in three named ways, all on this tab: a different wave speed costs a factor of 13; a fixed bottleneck violates its core assumption; and a frozen boundary makes the 12-minute error jump from 0.36 to 36 veh/mi. The data are synthetic throughout — no measurement noise, no lane changing, no capacity drop, no heterogeneous drivers. On real detector data all four would be present, and none of them is in this evaluation.

Fit card, applications, and the literature this connects to

Fit card — convolutional surrogate for traffic dynamics

Idea in one sentenceBecause a car-following rule and the LWR update apply the same local rule at every point of the time–space plane, both are convolutions, and a small residual conv block can learn the update operator from simulated or measured fields.
Why it works (the logic)Locality (the next state depends on a small neighbourhood) plus position independence (the same rule everywhere) are exactly the two inductive biases a convolution encodes; the skip connection encodes conservation, and the ReLUs encode the min() that switches between free-flow and congested regimes.
Fits these tasksTraffic state estimation and reconstruction from sparse probes and detectors; fast surrogates for a calibrated microsimulator inside an optimisation or digital-twin loop; short-horizon corridor prediction when boundary demand is known. Leverage labels: prediction, extraction.
Needs this dataSpace–time fields on a regular grid — detector speeds/flows at fixed stations, probe trajectories rasterised, or simulator output. Enough scenarios to cover the regimes you will meet (not enough time steps of one scenario). No labels beyond the field itself. Agencies already own the ingredients: ATSPM and ATMS logs, RITIS/probe speeds, permanent count stations.
Breaks whenThe corridor's fundamental diagram differs from the training one (factor of 13 in RMSE for a 3 mi/hr change in w); geometry varies along the road, breaking translation invariance; the receptive field is smaller than the domain of dependence; and — always — when boundary demand and capacity are not known over the forecast horizon.
Keep the classical method whenThe dynamics you need are already inside the CTM or IDM. Three measurable parameters beat 1,217 fitted ones on interpretability, on transferability to a new corridor, on data hunger, and on your ability to defend the result to a review panel. Reach for the learned version when you need dynamics you cannot write down, or inference fast enough to sit inside a loop.

The literature this connects to

Everything above is a rediscovery, in modern vocabulary, of a line of work that predates deep learning in transportation. Read at least the first of these.

  • Treiber, M., & Helbing, D. (2002). Reconstructing the spatio-temporal traffic dynamics from stationary detector data. The adaptive smoothing method: two exponential kernels, φ(x,t) = exp(−|x|/σ − |t − x/c|/τ), one tilted along cfree = 80 km/h and one along ccong = −15 km/h, blended by w = ½[1 + tanh((Vc − V*)/ΔV)] with Vc = 60 km/h. Read it as a hand-designed two-filter CNN with a gate, because that is what it is. See also Treiber, Kesting & Wilson (2011), CACAIE 26(6), 408–419.
  • Thodi, B. T., Khan, Z. S., Jabari, S. E., & Menéndez, M. (2022). Incorporating kinematic wave theory into a deep learning method for high-resolution traffic speed estimation. IEEE T-ITS 23(10). Anisotropic traffic kernels whose support is restricted to a free-flow band and a congested band, on a 10 m × 1 s grid at 5% probe penetration, trained in VISSIM and transferred to NGSIM and HighD. Parameter count linear, not quadratic, in kernel width.
  • Yang, C., Ramana, A. S. V., & Jabari, S. E. (2023). Generalized adaptive smoothing based neural network architecture for traffic state estimation. ASM rewritten explicitly as a three-layer network whose six parameters are the ASM's own.
  • Rempe, F., Franeck, P., & Bogenberger, K. (2022). On the estimation of traffic speeds with deep convolutional neural networks given probe data. Transportation Research Part C, 134.
  • Newell, G. F. (2002) and Daganzo, C. F. (1994) for the two models this page starts from.
