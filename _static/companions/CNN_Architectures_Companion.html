From LeNet to ResNet: eighteen years of learning to see

You already know what a convolution does — a small window of weights, slid over an image, reused at every position. This page is about what happened next: the five architectures that turned that one operation into a working vision system, what each one actually changed, and what it cost. The point is not the history. The point is that when a vendor quotes you “a ResNet-50 backbone” or a Jetson spec sheet quotes you “MobileNet at 30 fps,” you know exactly what has been traded away. Every number on this page was measured in the container that built it, on the real checkpoints, with the reproducer shipped alongside; published competition results are labelled and cited.

Five architectures, five ideas

Read this as a sequence of problems, not a sequence of names. Each architecture is the answer to whatever was blocking the previous one — and each answer is still in the code you will download this semester.

The five, in one line each

ArchitectureThe problem it was answeringThe idea it added
LeNet-5 1998 Read handwritten digits on bank cheques without hand-designing a feature for every stroke. Convolution + pooling + a trainable stack. The whole template — conv, subsample, conv, subsample, dense, classify — is already here, at parameters.
AlexNet 2012 The same template on 1.2 million photographs and 1,000 classes, which nobody had successfully trained. Scale, ReLU, dropout, and two GPUs. Not a new operation — a demonstration that the old one works if you make it 1,000× bigger and stop using saturating activations.
VGG-16 2014 AlexNet's kernel sizes (11×11, then 5×5, then 3×3) were hand-tuned and nobody knew which choice mattered. One kernel size, more layers. Every convolution is 3×3. Depth is the only knob. The result is beautifully simple and parameters heavy.
GoogLeNet 2014 VGG's cost. Same competition, same year, opposite philosophy. 1×1 bottlenecks and parallel branches, and no large fully-connected head. Beat VGG on error with fewer parameters.
ResNet 2015 Past ~20 layers, deeper networks got worse — and not from overfitting. Training error rose too. The skip connection. Each block learns a correction to its input rather than a replacement for it. Depth stops being dangerous; 152 layers becomes routine. This is the one that is still everywhere.
MobileNetV3 2019
(the coda)		 None of the above fits in a roadside cabinet or a vehicle ECU. Depthwise-separable convolution and an architecture searched against measured latency. of VGG-16's compute at higher ImageNet accuracy.
The through-line worth remembering. Between 1998 and 2015 the operation never changed. A 3×3 convolution in ResNet-50 does exactly what C1 does in LeNet-5 — nine weights, slid, reused. What changed was how the operations are wired to each other. Every one of the five ideas above is a wiring decision, and every one of them is a decision about cost.

Two things the diagram makes obvious

The funnel is the architecture

Every one of these nets trades where for what. The map starts large and specific to position (224×224, 3 colours) and ends small and specific to meaning (7×7, 512 or 2048 channels). The receptive field readout is the same story from the other side: by the last convolution, one output value is looking at a window across the input. That is why a deep net can say “queue” and a 3×3 kernel can only say “edge.”

Parameters and compute are not the same resource

Switch the colouring above. In AlexNet, of the parameters sit in the three dense layers at the end — but they do only of the arithmetic. VGG-16 is worse: of parameters, of the compute. GoogLeNet and ResNet delete that head entirely and replace it with global average pooling, which is why ResNet-152 has fewer parameters than VGG-16 while being nine times deeper. If your constraint is memory, look at the head. If your constraint is latency, look at the early convolutions.

Going deeper — how the receptive field is computed

Walking forward through the stack, each layer with kernel size k and stride s updates the receptive field r and the cumulative stride (“jump”) j:

rout = rin + (k − 1) · jin     how much wider the window gets jout = jin · s            how far apart neighbouring outputs are, in input pixels

In words: a kernel does not widen the window by k pixels, it widens it by k−1 steps of whatever the current stride is. So a 3×3 convolution after four pooling layers adds 32 input pixels, not 2. Stacking is cheap; pooling is what makes stacking powerful. Following Bishop, Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts (2024), §10.2.

The four moves that mattered

Each of these is a specific, checkable piece of arithmetic. If you understand these four, you can read any modern vision architecture diagram, including the ones that came after ResNet.

Move 1 — LeNet → AlexNet: nothing new, everything bigger

AlexNet's contribution is unusual in that it is mostly negative results cleared away. Same convolution, same pooling, same dense head. What changed:

  • ReLU instead of tanh. You measured this in the activations companion: tanh saturates hard — at |z| = 3 its gradient is 0.0099. Stack eight of those and the gradient reaching layer 1 is gone. ReLU passes gradient 1 wherever the unit is on.
  • Dropout in the dense head, because of the parameters live there and they will otherwise memorise the training set.
  • Two GPUs and 1.2 million labelled images. The architecture is larger than LeNet-5.
Why this matters to you. The lesson agencies keep re-learning is the one AlexNet taught: the bottleneck was labelled data and compute, not cleverness. When someone proposes a novel architecture for your crash-prediction problem and you have forty crashes, the architecture is not your bottleneck either.

Move 2 — AlexNet → VGG: replace one big kernel with a stack of small ones

Two 3×3 convolutions in a row see the same 5×5 window as one 5×5 convolution; three see the same 7×7 window. But they use fewer weights and put a non-linearity between them. Pick a width and check:

one big kernel — weights
stack of 3×3 — weights
fewer weights
non-linearities gained
Two wins for one change. Fewer parameters and more non-linearity for the same field of view. This is why every architecture after 2014 is built from 3×3 convolutions, and why you should be suspicious of a design that uses 7×7 anywhere except the very first layer (where the input has only 3 channels, so a big kernel is cheap). Simonyan & Zisserman (2015), §2.3.

Move 3 — VGG → GoogLeNet / ResNet-50: the 1×1 bottleneck

A 1×1 convolution has no spatial extent at all. It looks at one pixel, across all channels, and mixes them — a learned change of basis on the channel axis. Sandwich the expensive 3×3 between a 1×1 that narrows the channels and a 1×1 that widens them back, and the 3×3 is doing its work on a quarter of the channels:

two 3×3 — weights
1×1–3×3–1×1 — weights
cheaper, per block

Move 4 — the skip connection, and the problem it actually solves

This is the one worth getting right, because the usual explanation is only half true. Two experiments, both run on a 128×128 patch of our own Florida stop-bar frame pushed through stacks of plain 3×3 blocks, with and without a skip.

Experiment A — gradient reaching layer 1, no normalisation

The plain stack's gradient collapses, the residual stack's explodes. With normalisation switched off in both arms, the gradient arriving at layer 1 of a plain stack falls from at 8 conv layers to at 56 — a factor of . The residual stack goes the other way and blows up, because each skip adds its input straight back and nothing rescales the sum. Neither is usable. Real ResNets put BatchNorm in every block for exactly this reason. Keep both halves of this result: the skip fixes the vanishing direction and creates the exploding one.

Experiment B — with BatchNorm on, the deeper problem is still there

He et al. noticed something normalisation does not fix. Suppose the best thing a block could do is nothing — pass its input through unchanged. A plain block has to learn that identity map out of random weights. A residual block, with its branch initialised at zero, starts there. Both stacks below have BatchNorm; both are trained to reproduce their own input for steps.

The plain stack never gets back to where the residual stack begins. Not at any depth we tested, not after steps of training. And here is the part that should bother you: the deeper plain stack ends up worse. After the same training on the same task, 8 conv layers reach and 56 conv layers reach — the error for seven times the depth. That is the degradation He et al. reported: deeper plain networks with higher training error, which overfitting cannot explain, because overfitting lowers training error. The skip connection does not make the network more expressive. It makes “do nothing” free, so adding depth can never hurt.
Going deeper — why “learn a correction” is the right reading

Write the block's job as the mapping H(x) you wish it would compute. A plain block parameterises H directly. A residual block parameterises the difference:

plain:    y = H(x) residual: y = x + F(x)    so F(x) = H(x) − x

In words: both can represent the same functions — this is not about capacity. But if the useful H is close to the identity, then F is close to zero, and pushing a stack of weights toward zero is something gradient descent does easily and reliably. In transportation terms: a residual block is a correction term on an estimate that is already roughly right, which is the same reason we write v = vfree − Δv(k) rather than fitting speed from scratch. He et al. (2016), §3.1.

What this actually shows

1. ImageNet has no word for the thing you care about

Searching all ImageNet-1k class names for person, pedestrian, human, child and cyclist returns nothing. There is no pedestrian class. On the night-crossing frame — a frame whose entire subject is a person in the road — the best any of these models can say is traffic light or spotlight. The label space, not the architecture, is what makes a model useful to you.

2. The ImageNet ranking does not survive the trip

ResNet-152 is the most accurate model here by the published benchmark ( top-1, against ResNet-18's ). On our stop-bar frame ResNet-18 answers sports car and ResNet-152 answers . One frame is not an evaluation — but it is a warning that a two-point benchmark gap tells you very little about your corridor.

So what are these models for? Not for classifying your frames. You use them as a backbone — you throw away the 1,000-class head, keep the convolution stack that learned edges, textures and object parts from 1.2 million photographs, and train a small new head on your label space with your few hundred labelled frames. That is transfer learning, it is next week's topic, and it is the reason the architecture question is worth caring about at all: the backbone you pick sets the cost floor for everything you build on top of it.

Provenance: the stop-bar and departure frames are real Florida arterial dashcam frames from the course clip. The queued-arterial, night-crossing and work-zone stills are not US scenes — the queued arterial has a toll gantry with Malay signage and the night crossing is a UK street. They are here because they are hard, not because they are representative.

Which one do I use?

Answer four questions about the mobility problem in front of you. The recommendation below is assembled from the measured numbers on this page — it will show you its reasoning, and you should argue with it.

Fit cards

ResNet-18 / ResNet-50 — the default backbone

Idea in one sentence
A deep stack of 3×3 convolutions in which every block learns a correction to its input rather than a replacement, so depth is safe.
Why it works
The skip connection makes the identity map free, so adding layers can only add capacity — it cannot destroy the signal already flowing through.
Fits these tasks
Anything where you will fine-tune a pretrained backbone on a few hundred to a few thousand of your own labelled frames — vehicle and pedestrian detection from roadside or dashboard cameras, sign and pavement-condition inventory, incident detection. Leverage labels: classification, extraction.
Needs this data
An ImageNet checkpoint (free, ) plus your own labelled frames. Budget roughly a minute per frame to label a detection task by hand — you measured that in the object-detection lab.
Breaks when
Your imagery is far from photographs of objects — thermal, radar range–Doppler, lidar range images, top-down occupancy grids. The pretrained features are worth much less there, and sometimes nothing.
Keep the classical method when
The camera is fixed, the lighting is controlled, and you want a vehicle count. Frame differencing against a median background needs no labels, no GPU, and no checkpoint — and you measured that it works, right up until the queue stops moving.

MobileNetV3 (and the small detectors built on it) — the edge backbone

Idea in one sentence
Split every convolution into a per-channel spatial filter plus a 1×1 channel mixer, and let an architecture search pick the layer sizes against measured latency on a real phone.
Why it works
The spatial part and the channel-mixing part of a convolution are the expensive parts, and doing them separately costs far less than doing them jointly — multiply–adds per image against ResNet-50's .
Fits these tasks
Anything that has to run in a cabinet, on a pole, or in a vehicle: roadside detection on a Jetson or Pi, in-vehicle driver-state or forward-scene monitoring, battery-powered work-zone counters. Leverage labels: classification, extraction.
Needs this data
Same as above, plus a real latency measurement on the target device before you commit to a frame rate.
Breaks when
The objects are small or far away. You measured this in the detection lab: on the departure frame, boxes 40 px and larger averaged 0.70 confidence and boxes below 40 px averaged 0.36 — and the small model returned nothing at all on the night crossing and the work zone. A smaller backbone loses the far half of the scene first.
Keep the classical method when
You need a count and you have a loop or a radar detector already in the ground. It reports occupancy in the rain, at night, at 100% recall, for a few hundred dollars.

VGG-16 and AlexNet — historical, and you should know why

Idea in one sentence
Depth by repetition (VGG) and scale-plus-ReLU (AlexNet), both with a very large dense head bolted on the end.
Why it works
It does work — VGG-16 is still within a few points of ResNet-50 on ImageNet. It just costs the compute to get there.
Fits these tasks
Reproducing a published result that used them; reading older transportation papers, where a VGG-16 backbone was standard from about 2015 to 2018.
Needs this data
A checkpoint, which is by itself a reason not to put it on an edge device.
Breaks when
You have a memory or latency budget of any kind.
Keep the classical method when
Always, relative to these two — there is no mobility task in 2026 for which AlexNet or VGG-16 is the right answer over a ResNet at a comparable cost.

Check yourself

  1. A district wants to inventory every regulatory sign on 400 centreline miles from existing fleet dashcam video, processed overnight in a server room.   2. The same district wants a pedestrian-presence alert at one uncontrolled mid-block crossing, running on a solar-powered pole cabinet, reporting to the signal controller within 200 ms. Which backbone, and which of the two false positives from the detection lab worries you more here — the wing mirror or the banner flags?
  3. Your advisor suggests ResNet-152 because it has the best ImageNet accuracy in the table. Using two specific numbers from this page, make the counter-argument.
  4. A vendor's spec sheet says “3.8 GFLOPs, 50 layers, 76% accuracy.” Name one thing each of those three numbers does not tell you about how the system will behave on your corridor at 3 a.m. in the rain.
