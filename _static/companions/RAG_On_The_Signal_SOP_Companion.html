RAG on the signal SOP: how an agent looks things up

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets a model answer from a document it was never trained on. This page builds RAG one step at a time on a document District 7 actually uses: the FDOT D7 Traffic Signals SOP for hurricanes (v1.5, June 2025), pages 5–11. You split the SOP into pieces, turn the pieces into numbers, search them with your own questions, build the prompt, check the answer that comes back, and see where it breaks. Every number about the SOP is computed from its text; everything else is cited at the bottom of the page.

RAG: let the model look it up

A general AI model has not read District 7's signal SOP, and it cannot point you to a page it never saw. RAG keeps the SOP outside the model. For each question it finds the few pieces of the SOP that matter, puts them in front of the model together with the question, and asks the model to answer from those pieces only and to say which piece each sentence came from.

You already work this way. When the TMC supervisor asks “when is the first generator refuel?”, you don't answer from memory. You open the SOP binder at the post-storm tab, find item 3.2.1 on page 8, and answer “six hours after deployment, page 8.” RAG does the same three things: open the binder at the right tab (search), read that item (put it in the prompt), and answer with the page (cite).

Two places knowledge can live

In the weights: what the model learned in training. Hard to update, cannot point to a page, and sometimes made up.

In the index: the SOP as searchable pieces. When v1.6 comes out you rebuild the index, not the model, and every answer can point to its item and page.

Lewis et al. (2020) called these the parametric and non-parametric memory. Section 5.4 of the notes covers that paper; this page is the hands-on version.

Step 1 — Split the SOP into pieces

Search hands the model whole pieces, so a piece is what the model reads. Too small, and a rule loses the heading that says when it applies. Too big, and the model reads half a page to find one line. Cut in the wrong place, and a rule arrives in two halves. Move the sliders and watch the red marks.

The transportation lever: split the way the authors wrote it. The SOP is numbered (phase → time window → item → sub-item). One piece per item keeps every rule whole, and its title is its citation: “Funding > 0-1 Month Post Storm, item 2 (p. 10)”. Manuals have the same kind of structure: MUTCD sections with their Standard, Guidance and Option paragraphs; HSM chapters and CMF table rows; signal warrant criteria. Use it.
Bigger has another cost. MiniLM, the embedding model in tab 3, reads at most word pieces. With 200-word windows, of the 15 pieces are longer than that, so only their first words are turned into numbers; the last words are never seen by meaning search. Keyword search still reads them. Check your embedding model's input limit before you choose a piece size.
Going deeper (optional): why overlap stops cuts

Windows of S words start every S − O words, where O is the overlap. Take any rule and the last window that starts at or before its first word: that window starts at most S − O − 1 words before the rule, so it still has at least O + 1 words left. Any rule of O + 1 words or fewer therefore fits whole in some window.

The longest rule in pages 5–11 is words, so any overlap of words or more guarantees no cuts (try it). The price: every piece repeats O words of its neighbor, so the index grows and the model reads some text twice.

Step 2 — Turn each piece into 384 numbers

An embedding model reads a piece of text and returns a fixed list of numbers: a point in space. Pieces that mean similar things land near each other, even when they share no words. You met this idea with word2vec (Module 1) and BERT (Module 2); here one point stands for a whole SOP item. The model is all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a small six-layer BERT-style model trained on more than a billion pairs of related sentences.

Pre-StormPost-StormFundingTask Work Orders (TWO)✕ the question  —  lines to its 3 nearest items

How close is close? One number per pair

similarity(q, p) = q · p = q₁p₁ + q₂p₂ + … + q₃₈₄p₃₈₄
qthe question's 384 numbers, scaled to length 1
pone SOP piece's 384 numbers, scaled to length 1
q · pmultiply the two lists number by number and add up. Because both have length 1, this is the cosine of the angle between them.

In words: the more the two lists rise and fall together, the higher the score. Near 1 means the same direction (same meaning); near 0 means unrelated.

You have seen this dot product before. In attention (Module 2), a query scores every key to decide what to mix in. Here a question scores every SOP piece to decide what to read. Same arithmetic, used to search a binder instead of a sentence.
Going deeper (optional): where the 384 numbers come from

MiniLM runs the piece through six layers of self-attention and returns 384 numbers for every word piece. Mean pooling averages them into one list for the whole piece, and dividing by its length makes the dot product a cosine.

Training was contrastive, like CLIP in Module 1: for pairs of texts that belong together (a question and its answer, a title and its body), the model learns to score the true partner above the other texts in the batch. It never saw this SOP. That is why it handles “power” and “generator” better than keyword search does, and why it does not know that a TWO is a District 7 task work order.

Sources: Reimers and Gurevych (2019), Sentence-BERT, EMNLP; the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model card (Hugging Face): 384 numbers, inputs longer than 256 word pieces are cut off, fine-tuned on more than 1 billion sentence pairs.

Step 3 — Search: match words, match meaning, or both

For each question, the retriever scores every piece and hands the best k to the model. Keyword search counts the question's words in each piece. Meaning search compares the 384 numbers from tab 3. “Both” merges the two rankings. Pick a question, or type your own the way you would ask it on day 3 after a storm.

Meaning search on your own question needs the embedding model in your browser (about 45 MB, downloaded once from the internet). The listed questions work without it. In tests, its rankings matched this page's precomputed ones on all listed questions (one piece per item). An 8-bit copy would be half the download, but it changed the top 3 for 13 of 31 test questions: compressing a model changes what it retrieves.

The three scores, in words

  • Keyword (BM25, the classical method from 1990s information retrieval). A piece scores for every question word it contains. A word found in few pieces (DDIR, refuel) counts more than one found in many (signal). Long pieces are discounted, so they don't win just by being long. Common words (the, when) are ignored.
  • Meaning (MiniLM). The similarity from tab 3: question and piece as 384 numbers each, multiplied and added.
  • Both (reciprocal-rank fusion). Each piece gets 1/(60 + its rank) from each list, and the two are added. A piece near the top of either list rises.
Try it. Pick “Our backup power units went out to the field this morning…”. With one piece per item and keyword search, the refuel rule is at rank : the SOP says generator, refueling and deployed, the question says none of them. Meaning search does better (rank ) but still misses the top 3. Now type the SOP's words: “When is the first refueling after a generator is deployed?” Keyword search puts the rule first. Rewriting a field question into the document's words is a step an agent can do before it searches.
Going deeper (optional): the BM25 and fusion formulas
BM25(q, p) = Σ(over question words t) idf(t) · f(t,p)·(k₁+1) / ( f(t,p) + k₁·(1 − b + b·|p| / avg|p|) ) idf(t) = ln( 1 + (N − n(t) + 0.5) / (n(t) + 0.5) ) k₁ = 1.2, b = 0.75 on this page RRF(p) = 1/(60 + rank_keyword(p)) + 1/(60 + rank_meaning(p))

f(t,p): how many times word t appears in piece p; |p|: words in the piece; N: pieces in the index; n(t): pieces containing t. Words are lower-cased, common words dropped, and simple endings (-s, -ed, -ing) removed, so refueled and refueling match.

Sources: Robertson et al. (1995), Okapi at TREC-3; Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher (2009), Reciprocal rank fusion, SIGIR.

Where it breaks

  
Six ways RAG fails on this SOP. Each one is measured on the SOP or shown live, with one piece per item and
     meaning search unless the card says otherwise.

  

    
1The question uses field words, not the SOP's words

      
Ten questions about real SOP rules, written the way a crew lead or operator might ask
        them (“backup power units” for generators, “federal damage paperwork” for a DDIR). Where does the rule that answers each one rank?

      

      
Fixes: let the agent rewrite the question into the SOP's terms before searching, and search with both phrasings;
        build a glossary of field and SOP terms with the people who use both; write test questions the way crews actually talk.
        Tab 8 measures a glossary on questions it never saw: it helped less than these ten suggest.


    
2A rule is cut in half

      

      
Fix: split at the SOP's own items, or overlap the windows by more than the longest rule (tab 2).


    
3Two rules disagree

      
Funding 0-1 Month item 1 says repairs “with a repair cost of $5000 or greater” need a DDIR, and 0-1 Week
        6.1.1 sorts them as “< $5,000 or => $5,000”. Funding 0-1 Month 2.1 groups repairs “if the total cost exceeds $5,000”.
        For a group totaling exactly $5,000 they give different answers. Did search fetch both wordings for “”?

      

      
Fix: retrieval usually fetches both here; the risk is that the model quotes one. The prompt in tab 5 says
        “if two passages disagree, quote both”, and the SOP's known conflicts belong in a list the agent reads and a person settles
        (here: ask the DDIR team).


    
4The answer is not in the SOP

      
Search always returns k pieces, even when none of them answers. Each dot is one question;
        its position is the similarity of the best piece found.

      

      

      
Fix: no score cutoff will do it. The prompt must allow “the passages do not say”, and the SOP's known gaps
        (Appendix B zone maps and Appendix C responsibility matrix are blank pages; there is no Priority #1 list) belong in a note
        the agent reads, so it can say what is missing and whom to ask.


    
5The piece is longer than the embedding model reads

      
MiniLM reads at most  word pieces and silently drops the rest.
        With 200-word windows,  of 15 pieces are over the limit and lose their last  words.
        On the 22 test questions this caused no miss, but nothing warns you when it does.

      
Fix: keep pieces under the model's limit (one piece per item: longest is  GPT-4o tokens), or use a model with a longer input.


    
6The SOP changes and the index does not

      
Version 1.5 is dated June 2025. When District 7 issues v1.6, an index that still holds v1.5 will
        quote the old rule, with a citation, confidently. This happens to national manuals too: FHWA adopted Revision 1 of the
        MUTCD 11th Edition in the Federal Register on March 5, 2026, so an index built on the December 2023 text now quotes
        superseded wording wherever Revision 1 changed it. In the RAG paper, answering questions about world leaders with
        the index of the right year gave 70% and 68% correct; with the wrong year's index, 12% and 4%.

      
Fix: rebuild the index on every revision, remove or mark the old version, put the version date in every
        piece's title, and rerun the test questions, including one whose answer changed.

  







  
Beyond lookup: what RAG cannot do

  
Every question so far asked for one rule that sits in one place. Many real questions don't: a sequence of steps,
     a plan, a complete list, a check across every party, something that is missing, a judgment about the whole SOP. RAG hands
     the model the k pieces that look most like the question. When the answer needs fifteen pieces spread over four sections,
     or needs the model to notice what is not written, the model never sees what it needs, and it still writes a confident answer.

What to expect when you give a document to a chat assistant

    

      
A short document that fits (like this SOP, about 3,200 tokens). The assistant reads all of it: Claude projects use in-context
        processing when the files fit, and ChatGPT Enterprise includes the full text of uploads under 110,000 tokens. Holistic questions are possible because
        everything is in front of the model. Still: long lists come back with items missing, gaps get filled with generic best practice without saying so,
        and nothing about how the SOP works in practice is in the file.

      
A long document or a library (manuals, years of AARs). The assistant switches to search: Claude projects switch to a project
        knowledge search tool as the files approach the context limit, and ChatGPT brings in the chunks its search index finds relevant. Lookups still work.
        Plans, lists and whole-document questions are now built from a few fragments, as on this page, and they still sound complete. Nothing in the
        answer tells you how much was read.

      Fit card, applications, check yourself
      
  
      
    
      Fit card — RAG over agency documents
      
    
      
      
      Idea in one sentence
      Keep the documents outside the model; for each question, fetch the few pieces that match and have the model answer from them, citing each one.
      
      
      Why it works
      The model answers from what is in front of it. An index can be rebuilt when the document changes, and every sentence can be traced to an item and a page; weights can do neither.
      
      
      Fits these tasks
      Questions over long or changing text: SOPs, manuals (MUTCD, FDOT Design Manual), after-action reports, past studies, crash-report codebooks. retrieval extraction
      
      
      Needs this data
      The documents as clean text that keeps their structure (sections, item numbers, pages, version date); 20 or more test questions with known answer rules; people who know both the field's words and the document's.
      
      
      Breaks when
      The question is not a lookup: a plan, a sequence of steps, a complete list, something missing or a judgment about the whole document needs more pieces than k (tab 7). Field words differ from the document's; a rule is cut in half; two clauses disagree and the answer quotes one; the answer is not in the document (search still returns something); a piece is longer than the embedding model reads; the index is stale.
      
      
      Keep the classical method when
      The document fits in the prompt (this SOP: about 3,200 tokens), so send all of it or make it a skill; the question uses the document's own terms, where keyword search matched or beat meaning search here; or the answer is a number in a table or database, so query it with a tool. For complete lists and missing items, extract a table from the document once and filter it with code.

      From RAG to an agent that reads
      
  
      RAG takes one look at the document, chosen by similarity before the model has read anything. An engineer reading the SOP or
     the MUTCD takes many looks, and each depends on the last: open the right section, follow a reference, look up a table, check that nothing
     is missing. An agent hands those looks to the model, inside a harness you build. This tab shows the ladder from one to the other, lets you
     play the agent on the SOP, and lays out where transportation knowledge goes in the harness.
      

  
      
    
      The ladder: who picks what the model reads
      
    
      
    
      Measured on the 72-hour plan question. The agent rung is the scripted trace you can replay below: its calls were written by
      hand, and every result and token count was computed from the SOP. It is the same idea as fixed-time, actuated and adaptive signal control.
      Each rung costs more to build, run and test, so climb only when the rung below fails your test questions.

      Pick a question, then call the tools the way an agent would. Every call adds text to what the model has
      read, counted in tokens. The last number, needed items in hand, is the teacher's view: an agent cannot see it, so your harness needs a
      check that stands in for it.

      Read the MUTCD like an engineer
      
    
      One lane-closure question, read the way an engineer reads the 11th Edition's Part 6. The index points to a typical
      application; its figure uses letter codes for sign distances, so the next look is Table 6B-1; the taper length comes from Tables 6B-3 and
      6B-4; Sections 6B.03 to 6B.07 give the four areas a zone must have; and Section 1A.04 leaves the decision to an engineer. You cannot know
      the third look until the second one has been read. One similarity search, made before reading anything, cannot plan that path.
      
    
      
    
      Steps 3 to 6 are not searches: a table lookup, a computation, a checklist and a person. In a harness each becomes a tool,
      a check in code, or a checkpoint. Use the edition and revision your state has adopted, and its standard plans.

      Before you trust these results.
    What is the baseline? RAG's top 3 and the whole SOP, on the same six questions frozen before any search ran on them.
    How was the data split, and why is that honest? The “sections that hold the items” row opens exactly the sections that contain the needed
    items, a best case that an agent has to find for itself. The scripted harness was written by hand to show what a harness allows; only its
    results are computed.
    The ugly cases: a model may open the wrong sections, stop early or loop, so run an agent at least 3 times and count those runs. Tokens here
    count the text added to what the model has read; a real agent re-sends its growing context at every step, so its cost is higher.
      

  
      
    
      Openings for research and practice
      
    
        
      
      • A manual as an environment. Typed actions (open, follow, look up, compute) over the MUTCD or the HCM; a test set of questions whose
        evidence spans sections; scores for evidence reached, correctness, steps and tokens, over repeated runs. CrossTraffic (Tamaru and Ran, 2026)
        already connects a model to HCM methods through validated tools.
        • 
      
      • Knowledge ablations. The same model and loop with and without each part of the harness: the glossary, the checklist, the
        Standard/Guidance check. This is how a paper shows that transportation knowledge, not the model, did the work.
        • 
      
      • Checking documents against manuals. An agent that reads a TTC plan or an SOP and lists, with citations, where it departs from the
        MUTCD or leaves a step without an owner, for an engineer to review.
        • 
      
      • Keeping up with revisions. An agent that compares MUTCD Revision 1 with the December 2023 text and lists which of a district's
        documents cite sections that changed.
        • 
    
      
    
      Related general work: a state DOT assistant that loops retrieval, evaluation and query rewriting (Developing an AI Assistant for
      Knowledge Management and Workforce Training in State DOTs, 2026); ReadAgent (Lee et al., ICML 2024), which keeps a short summary of every page
      and lets the model choose which pages to reopen.
      
  
      

  
      
    
      Check yourself
      
    
      
      
      1. The workflow and the scripted harness both put all 15 plan items in front of the model. What would make an agent worth its
        extra cost in your district?
      
      
      Notes
      Questions nobody wrote a step for, references that change the path (a missing list, a cross-reference),
        and gaps it is asked to find. If most questions fall into a few known types, write those workflows and keep the agent for the rest.
      
      
      2. check_owner flagged two steps. Its list of role words (FDOT, SAT, DAT, CEI, TMC, contractor and others) is
        transportation knowledge. What goes wrong if a role is missing from the list, and how would you test the check?
      
      
      Notes
      Steps owned by that role are flagged as having no owner. Test the check on items whose owners you know,
        including every role the district uses, before it runs inside an agent.
      
      
      3. For the MUTCD lane closure, which steps would you give the model as tools, and which would you run as code every time?
      
      
      Notes
      Tools: list and open sections, look up a table row, compute a taper length. Always-on code: the four-area
        checklist, citation checks, and the Standard-behind-every-“must” check. The engineer approves the result.
