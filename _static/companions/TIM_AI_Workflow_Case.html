Traffic Incident Management as an AI Workflow

TIM is the best first case in this course, because it is entirely a problem about time. Every stage has a timestamp, every timestamp is already collected, and Florida has a written policy target to beat. That means you can measure the baseline before you build anything — which is exactly what a workflow project demands. This case walks the clock, rebuilds the workflow with AI in it, and is honest about which of the promised gains have actually been demonstrated in the field.

TIM is a stopwatch problem

A crash happens. Some interval later, somebody in authority knows. Some interval after that, it is confirmed, resources are sent, they arrive, lanes reopen, the scene empties, and traffic returns to normal. Every improvement in traffic incident management is a reduction in one of those intervals — and every AI question you can ask about TIM reduces to which interval does this shorten, and by how much?

The incident timeline — click a segment

Roadway Clearance Time (RCT) T1 → all lanes available
Incident Clearance Time (ICT) T1 → last responder leaves

Both measures start at first recordable awareness — so the detection interval, the one an AI workflow can shrink most, sits outside both headline performance measures. So does recovery. What gets measured shapes what gets managed.

Note on notation: FHWA's performance-measures page lays the timeline out as eight points (T0–T7); the National TIM Responder Training handout compresses it to T0–T6. Both define RCT and ICT identically. Diagrams vary by source — say which one you are using.

The first thing to notice is a gap, not a number. FDOT's own 2026 TIM Strategic Plan does not publish a current statewide RCT/ICT figure — it points to a future reporting product. The FY2019/20 numbers above are the most recent statewide figures traceable to a published FDOT source. If your project needs a current number, getting it is part of the project, and you will get it from a district, not from a document.

Nationally the measurement problem is worse. In the 2018 TIM Capability Maturity Self-Assessment, roughly 61% of programs reported measuring roadway clearance time and 54% measured incident clearance time. Slightly more than half the country measures the primary outcome of its own program. Before you propose a model, notice that a large share of the available improvement is in measurement, which needs no AI at all.

Why the clock matters: secondary crashes

An incident that stays on the roadway generates a queue, and the back of that queue is where the next crash happens. That is the safety argument for clearing fast, and it is worth getting the numbers right because the commonly quoted one is old.

The stat everyone repeats: the likelihood of a secondary crash increases about 2.8% per minute the primary incident remains a hazard. That comes from Karlaftis et al. (1999), on a single Indiana dataset, and it is now more than 25 years old.

The better current citation: a multi-state re-analysis using 2022–2024 crash data from Florida, Maryland, Tennessee and Virginia finds the effect is not constant — it is front-loaded. Roughly +13–21% odds per minute in the first ten minutes, about +3%/min from 10–30, about +1.5%/min from 30–60, and under 1%/min after an hour.

Read what that implies for your workflow. If the risk is concentrated in the first ten minutes, then minutes shaved off detection and verification are worth several times as much as minutes shaved off the tail of clearance. That single finding should shape which interval you attack — and it is the opposite of where most TIM investment goes.

How common are secondary crashes, really?

FHWA's 2023 multi-state analysis started from 5.46 million crashes across ten states, of which 51,856 (0.94%) had been flagged as secondary. After spatial-temporal verification, only 15,448 — 0.28% — were confirmed. Under a third of the flags held up.

Florida specifically: a 67.1% verification rate (second-highest of the ten states) and confirmed secondary crashes at 0.13% of all Florida crashes. Of the confirmed ones, roughly 30% occur simultaneously with the primary and about half within twenty minutes.

An unresolved conflict, and you should know about it. A widely circulated infographic states "up to 1 in 13 crashes are secondary" — roughly 7.7%, which is more than an order of magnitude above the FHWA verified rate. The two use different denominators and methods, and the discrepancy is not reconciled in the sources. If you cite a secondary-crash rate in front of practitioners, say which one and why.

This is a live example of a course theme: the number that circulates is rarely the number that survives verification, and knowing the difference is the transportation engineer's job, not the model's.

Road Rangers

Free service patrol, contracted by FDOT and partners. Motorists reach them at *347. Coverage is 2,100+ centerline miles across 105+ contracted beats spanning FDOT districts, the Turnpike, and three expressway authorities. 489,000+ assists in FY2021/22, and a reported benefit-cost ratio of $12.60 returned per $1 spent.

Note the number that matters for an AI project: Road Rangers are already patrolling the network continuously. Every beat vehicle is a sensor platform that is already paid for.

RISC

Rapid Incident Scene Clearance — pre-contracted heavy recovery with an incentive clock. From FHP's Notice to Proceed: one heavy wrecker on scene within 45 minutes, all equipment within 60, roadway open within 90 → a $2,500 bonus. Past 150 minutes, a $10-per-minute penalty.

Statewide FY2022/23: 499 activations, $1,470,300 in bonus payments. Turnpike two-year averages: 48-minute response, 50-minute clearance, over 95% success against the 90-minute goal.

RISC is where duration prediction would pay. The decision to activate is currently made after someone sees the scene.

The systems of record

SunGuide is FDOT's ATMS, running at every district RTMC — Tampa Bay's since 2006. It holds the event log, integrates FHP computer-aided-dispatch data, detectors, cameras and Road Ranger data, and computes RCT, ICT and secondary-crash counts.

FL511 is the public face, aggregating FDOT sensors and cameras through SunGuide plus FHP, Road Ranger and local law-enforcement reports, Waze crowdsourced data, and NOAA weather — roughly 81,000 information requests a day.

Any workflow that cannot write into SunGuide will not be used, no matter how good it is.

Local detail worth knowing

The Tampa Bay Regional TMC operates around the clock across 12 jurisdictions — five counties (Hillsborough, Pinellas, Pasco, Hernando, Citrus) and seven cities — under a five-year, $31 million operations contract confirmed in April 2026. Nothing in the public contract materials describes an AI capability.

And a detail that surprises people: the Severe Incident Response Vehicle (SIRV) program — a dedicated FDOT incident-response unit that puts a trained responder on scene with equipment and authority — operates in District 4 only (Broward, plus the I-95 Express Lanes in Miami-Dade). It is not in District 7. FDOT's 2026 TIM Strategic Plan lists exploring scalable replication to other districts as a one-to-three-year action item.

That is a real, current, local gap in a written state plan. A project that asks "what would SIRV-equivalent capability look like in District 7, and could part of it be delivered by better information rather than another vehicle?" is a project with a live audience.

The AI did not take over incident management — it took over searching, typing, notifying, and estimating, so that the people can spend their attention on deciding. That is what a well-built AI workflow looks like, and it is much less dramatic than the vendor version.

Eight things that actually change

This is the answer to "how is it different from the traditional way?" Not "it uses AI" — these eight structural differences. Six are speed-ups of something that already happens. Two are capabilities the traditional workflow does not have at all, and those two are where the interesting projects live.

1Detection stops waiting to be told
Traditional

The clock from occurrence to awareness belongs to nobody. It ends when a motorist calls, a trooper drives up, or an operator happens to glance at the right monitor. It is luck, and because it is luck it is rarely measured.

AI workflow

Many weak signals — a probe-speed drop, detector occupancy, a cluster of Waze reports, hard-braking events, camera analytics — are fused into one alert with a confidence score. No single signal has to be conclusive, which is precisely what single-source automatic incident detection could never do. The system proposes an incident before anyone reports it, and the interval becomes a measurable, ownable quantity.

2Verification becomes a confirmation, not a search
Traditional

The operator must work out which camera sees the reported location, slew it, interpret what is on screen, and then type it into the event record. Under load, across many simultaneous events, this is where the queue forms.

AI workflow

The alert arrives with the cameras already pointed and a draft description attached to the frame it came from. The operator's job collapses to confirm, edit, or dismiss. The step is not automated away — it is pre-loaded. This is the single most realistic near-term gain, and it is also the checkpoint, so it is safe to make it fast.

3Escalation moves from discovery to prediction
Traditional

Response is scaled from a coarse incident type, then corrected once someone sees the scene. A heavy-recovery need discovered at minute 40 costs those 40 minutes plus the 45-minute RISC response window that then starts.

AI workflow

Duration and resource requirements are estimated at verification, so RISC candidacy is a decision available at minute three. This is the largest single time lever in the whole workflow — and the shakiest model. Incident duration is heavy-tailed; published models predict the middle well and the tail badly, and the tail is exactly what RISC exists for. Any honest version of this reports accuracy separately for short, medium and long incidents.

4The shape changes from a chain to a fan
Traditional

One operator does things in sequence: type the event, then post the signs, then update 511, then make calls. Downstream actions are serialized behind one person's keyboard, and the busier the incident, the longer the chain.

AI workflow

One verified record triggers everything at once — signs, 511, navigation feeds, arterial signal plans, Road Ranger notification. This is a workflow-architecture change, not a model. It is also the cheapest thing on this list to build, and the easiest to overlook because there is nothing clever in it.

5Traveler information addresses drivers, not signs
Traditional

"CRASH AHEAD — EXPECT DELAYS" on a dynamic message sign, seen identically by everyone who passes it, including the drivers exiting before the incident.

AI workflow

An advisory filtered by the vehicle's route and heading, carrying a predicted clearance time and a diversion worth taking. The unit of information changes from a sign to a driver. You can build this now: the DoTPilot v2x-traffic-advisor-f511 branch already filters the FL511 feed on-device by GPS, heading and route.

6A new output: where the back of the queue is right now no traditional equivalent
Traditional

Nobody knows the queue tail location in real time. It is inferred from experience and from whichever detector last showed a speed drop. The tail is where secondary crashes happen, and it moves.

AI workflow

Estimate the tail continuously from probe and detector data and push a warning to the signs and the vehicles approaching it. This is not an automation of an existing step — the traditional workflow has no such step. And recall the secondary-crash finding: risk is concentrated in the first minutes, which is exactly when the tail is growing fastest and least predictable. Of everything on this page, this is the most valuable and the least built.

7Measurement becomes a by-product instead of a project
Traditional

RCT and ICT are reconstructed from timestamps a busy operator typed while the incident was still running. Nationally, only about 61% of programs measure RCT at all and 54% measure ICT. The data quality problem is structural, not lazy.

AI workflow

The system's own trace is the record. Timestamps are machine-accurate, the lane-blockage timeline is continuous rather than a handful of entries, and the after-action summary drafts itself. This is why the "record" step exists in the workflow anatomy — without it there is no honest evaluation and no next version.

8The loop closes no traditional equivalent
Traditional

After-action review happens for major incidents. The other several hundred thousand events a year teach the system nothing, because nothing is comparing what was expected to what happened.

AI workflow

Every incident yields labels: predicted duration versus actual, recommended resources versus what was needed, alerts that were real versus phantom. Traditional TIM has no learning loop; this one does — and that is the difference that compounds. It only exists if feature 7 was built first, which is why measurement is not the boring part of the project.

The pattern underneath all eight

Six of these are the machine taking over searching, typing, notifying, and estimating. Two — the queue tail and the learning loop — are genuinely new capability. Not one of them is the machine taking over a decision.

If you can state your project's difference in that form — "today this interval is owned by nobody and measured by no one; here is the signal that would shorten it; here is the person who still decides" — you have a workflow project. If the only difference you can name is "we use a neural network," you have a method looking for a problem.

What must not change

Round 1 requires you to mark at least one step "AI must not act alone here" with a reason. TIM has several, and the reasons are not squeamishness — they are authority, liability, and the structure of emergency response.

Four steps that stay human

1Declaring all lanes open. This stops the RCT clock and puts traffic back through a scene. The person who declares it is on the ground, can see what a camera cannot, and carries the liability. No confidence score substitutes for that.

2Dispatching emergency responders. A model can rank, recommend, and pre-fill. Sending people into a live roadway is a dispatcher's decision inside an established chain of command.

3Activating RISC. It spends money on a contract with a bonus-and-penalty clock, triggered by FHP's Notice to Proceed. Prediction informs it; a person authorizes it.

4Anything involving injury, fatality, hazmat, or a criminal scene. Investigation requirements, medical examiner control, and evidence preservation govern the scene, and they are not trade-offs to optimize.

Two structural constraints

Incident command is human by doctrine. TIM is multi-agency by definition — FHP, fire, EMS, FDOT, towing, sometimes the medical examiner — coordinated under unified command. A workflow that only works inside the TMC has optimized the one agency whose actions least determine clearance time. The responders on scene determine it.

The 90-minute goal is a policy, not an objective function. The Open Roads Policy states the goal and explicitly excepts weather and safety closures. An optimizer handed "minimize clearance time" will eventually recommend clearing a scene that should not yet be cleared. The trade-off between reopening fast and investigating a fatal crash properly is a judgment a state has already made in writing — and your workflow must encode that policy as a constraint, not rediscover it as a cost.

Automation bias is the failure mode to design against. An operator who confirms three hundred machine proposals a shift stops reading them. Show the evidence next to the proposal — the frame, the signals that fired, the confidence — so that confirming stays a real act. A checkpoint that has become a reflex is not a checkpoint.

And a privacy line

TIM data touches identifiable people: camera imagery of a crash scene, CAD records, and anything about injuries. For your project, video, imagery, or other data about identifiable people requires instructor approval and IRB consultation before any collection begins — and that conversation starts when you pick the topic, not when you are ready to collect. Agency-held incident data usually comes with its own conditions on what may leave the building; find out before you design around a cloud service.

Why automatic incident detection failed the first time

Loop-detector AID algorithms — the California algorithm, McMaster, and a decade of successors — were deployed widely and then quietly abandoned. The reasons are worth knowing, because they are the reasons your version could fail too.

  • The trilemma is structural. Detection rate, time-to-detect and false-alarm rate trade against each other, and no calibration optimizes all three. A Texas DOT-funded evaluation concluded that site-specific calibration procedures for some deployed systems were never developed at all.
  • Weather broke them. McMaster improved on California but misread storm-induced speed drops as incidents.
  • And so operators turned them off. The same evaluation reports the blunt outcome: "most implementations of ATMS operate with the incident detection algorithm turned off."
What is different now is the number of independent signals, not the cleverness of the algorithm. One detector station could never be sure. Probe speed plus Waze plus camera plus connected-vehicle events can be, because their errors are uncorrelated. If your project's answer to "why won't this fail like the last one" is "better model," it will fail like the last one.

Where Florida actually is

As of August 2026 there is no publicly reported operational AI or computer-vision incident detection deployment in Florida TIM. FDOT's own 2026 TIM Strategic Plan lists emerging technologies — photogrammetry and robotic surveying for crash investigation, connected LED flares, automated debris removal, third-party navigation integration — and AI-based video incident detection is not among them.

Read that as an opportunity with a warning attached. The opportunity is obvious. The warning is that a state with one of the most mature TIM programs in the country, which has been running SunGuide since the mid-2000s and measures its own clearance times, has not adopted this. Find out why before you assume nobody thought of it. That question is a good first email to a district TIM engineer, and the answer is worth more to your project than another week of modeling.

The honest possibilities include: procurement cycles, the false-alarm history above, the fact that Road Rangers already provide dense human coverage on the corridors that matter, or that nobody has shown a benefit-cost ratio that beats $12.60 to $1.

The hardest technical problem, stated honestly

Incident duration prediction is heavy-tailed and nobody has solved it. A systematic overview of the literature finds the duration distribution has a long tail that models fail to capture, with no consistent distributional fit across studies — log-normal, log-logistic, Weibull and generalized-F all reported as best by different authors. In one five-method comparison, only the neural-network model could predict incidents longer than 90 minutes at all. Hazard-based models handle central tendency and struggle at the extremes.

Read what that means operationally. The incidents where duration prediction would create the most value — the ones that justify RISC activation, the ones that generate the queues where secondary crashes happen — are exactly the ones the models predict worst. A duration model reported as a single MAPE across all incidents is hiding this. Report performance separately for short, medium and long incidents, or you have not reported it.

TIM as a capstone project

Four scoped versions, ordered by how likely they are to finish in eleven weeks. Every one of them is a real project. The first involves no machine learning whatsoever and is the one most likely to produce something an agency uses.

Track A · lowest risk, high value

Measure where the time actually goes

Reconstruct the incident timeline for one Tampa Bay corridor from archived event records. Break RCT and ICT into their component intervals. Find which interval carries the most variance, not the most minutes — variance is where the manageable losses hide. Segment by time of day, incident type, and whether a Road Ranger was first on scene.

Deliverable: the current-state measurement FDOT's own 2026 plan says is not yet published, for one corridor. Method: data work and statistics. AI content: optional, and honestly not needed.

Do not underrate this one. It answers a question a district can act on, it is finishable, and every other track needs its output anyway.

Track B · the honest version of automatic detection

Could we have known sooner?

Offline replay on historical data. Take probe speeds, detector data, and Waze or FL511 event records for a corridor and a period, and build a fusion detector. Then compare its alert time against the recorded first-awareness timestamp for each real incident.

The metric is the whole project: minutes earlier, against false alarms per beat per week. Report both or neither. A detector that fires four minutes earlier and cries wolf twice a shift is worse than nothing, and you should be the one to say so.

Why this scopes well: you are competing against a recorded timestamp, so the baseline is unambiguous and already in the data. No live system, no deployment, no IRB complication if the data is aggregate.

Track C · high ceiling, needs discipline

Duration prediction with an honest tail

Predict roadway clearance time from attributes known at verification — not attributes known afterward, which is the leak that makes published models look better than they are. Then do the thing the literature mostly does not: report accuracy separately for under 30 minutes, 30 to 90, and over 90.

The deliverable is not the model. It is a decision rule: at what predicted duration and what confidence should RISC activation be considered, given a $2,500 bonus, a $10-per-minute penalty past 150 minutes, and the cost of activating for an incident that would have cleared anyway? That question is answerable, it is economic rather than architectural, and it is the one a practitioner will ask you.

Track D · hardware-integrated

Queue-tail warning on the vehicle

Estimate the back-of-queue location from probe or detector data during an incident and deliver a targeted warning to an approaching vehicle. Fork the DoTPilot v2x-traffic-advisor-f511 branch, which already filters the FL511 feed on-device by GPS, heading and route, and add tail estimation and an approach warning.

Why it is worth the risk: this is feature 6 — the capability the traditional workflow does not have — aimed at the interval where the secondary-crash evidence says the risk is concentrated. Scope it to one corridor and one direction and demonstrate on recorded data before attempting anything live.

The constraint that decides all four

Data access, not modeling. SunGuide event records and FHP computer-aided-dispatch data sit inside agency systems with their own agreements. Start that conversation the week you choose the topic — topics are confirmed Sept 17 and presentations are Dec 3. A team still negotiating access in October has already lost.

Tracks that survive a slow data agreement: Track B on any state's open incident archive, or Track C on published datasets, with Florida framing. Have that fallback identified before you commit.

Round 1, for any of these tracks: the workflow in one picture, the baseline in real numbers, the pain point, the data on hand and its governance, and every step labeled — with at least one marked "AI must not act alone here." Tab 2 of this page is most of your first slide already.
