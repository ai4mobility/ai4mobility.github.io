After the storm: what the TMC knows, SAT teams and the generator ledger

The post-storm steps of the FDOT District 7 signal SOP, run on simulated storms on the Tampa network. The dispatcher no longer sees the truth, only the signal system, eSTORM reports and the outage map. Example numbers, not FDOT figures.

What the runs say

    1. What the TMC can see

    The earlier pages let the dispatcher know every signal's true state. The SOP describes something else: three sources, each with a blind spot, and Situational Awareness Teams (SAT) that drive the corridors to confirm.

    2. Does the picture matter?

    The same storms and the same trucks in every row. Only what the dispatcher knows changes. A wasted trip is a truck that arrives and finds nothing to do: power already back, damage a generator cannot fix, or no commercial power where it came to pick up a generator.

    3. How many SAT teams, and how good must the reports be?

    District 7 staff told us that SAT teams are partly temporary workers who are new to signals, so late and wrong reports are expected. The base case assumes 15% of reports wrong and 30 minutes on average before a finding is entered.

    4. Three choices the SOP leaves open

    An agent that carries out the SOP reaches these points and finds no instruction. Each row changes one choice; the first row is the base.

    5. Routes for generator teams

    The SOP gives every team a route. Our best guess for a route is the zone's corridors in order, those with Priority 1 signals first. The rows compare a team that follows that list with a team that picks its next stop by score.

    6. The generator ledger

    SOP 3.1 asks for team, generator NH # and time at every deployment. A generator that is connected but not written down gets no fuel and no pickup until someone finds it.
