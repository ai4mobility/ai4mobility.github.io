Tokens: the unit a language model actually reads

Before a transformer can do anything with the Signal Timing Manual, a crash narrative, or a trajectory, something has to decide what one symbol is. That decision is tokenization, and it is made once, before training, by an algorithm that has never heard of transportation. This page shows you the real thing: the tokenizer that today's frontier models use is running in your browser, on whatever text you type. Every count on this page was produced by that tokenizer or by tiktoken on a real document — nothing here is illustrative.

The mobility problem

You want a model to answer questions about the FHWA Traffic Signal Timing Manual (FHWA-HOP-08-024) — 274 pages, 711,331 characters. A transformer cannot read characters and it cannot read words. It reads a sequence of integers. Someone has to decide which integers, and that decision is frozen into the model forever.

The picture: what happens to a sentence before the model sees it

The bridge: you already do this — you call it discretization

Choosing a token is the same decision you make when you choose a cell length Δx in the cell transmission model, a 5-minute versus 15-minute aggregation bin for detector counts, a link versus a lane as the unit of a network model, or an HCM level-of-service letter instead of a delay in seconds. In every case:

  • Everything below the unit becomes invisible. A 30-second shockwave does not exist in 15-minute bins. A 4 mph deceleration does not exist in 10 mph speed bins. The characters inside a token do not exist to a language model.
  • Everything above the unit must be built out of units. A queue longer than one CTM cell is represented as several cells; a word longer than one token is represented as several tokens.
  • Finer units cost sequence length; coarser units cost fidelity. That trade is the whole design problem, and it is the same trade in both fields.
The one sentence to carry: a token is the smallest symbol the model can see or emit. It is a modelling choice, not a property of language — and in transportation it is a choice you will have to make yourself, because nobody has made it for trajectories, signal logs, or detector streams the way OpenAI made it for text.

The key equation, and its reading

tokenizer: text → (i1, i2, …, iT), it ∈ {0, 1, …, V−1} embedding: xt = E[it, :] E ∈ ℝV×d
SymbolMeaning, in transportation terms
itThe token ID at position t — an integer, like a detector station ID or a link ID. It carries no meaning by itself.
TSequence length: how many symbols the document became. This is what you pay for — attention cost grows with T².
VVocabulary size: how many distinct symbols exist. Fixed forever when the model is built. o200k_base = 200,019.
dEmbedding dimension — the same d from Sept 10 (Word2Vec 300, GPT-2 768).
EThe embedding table: one learned row per vocabulary entry. GPT-2's is 50,257 × 768 = 38,597,376 parameters, spent before any reasoning happens.

In words: tokenization turns a document into a list of row numbers; the embedding table turns each row number into a vector. Everything the model will ever know about a piece of text has to survive that lookup.

Going deeper — why V is a budget, not a free parameter (optional)
V appears twice and pulls in opposite directions. At the input it costs V·d parameters in E; at the output the model must produce a probability over all V tokens, which is a softmax of the same width — another V·d matrix and an O(V·d) multiply at every generated step. Doubling V therefore buys shorter sequences (attention cost falls roughly with T²) at the price of a bigger, slower, and more thinly-trained vocabulary — rare entries get few gradient updates. Going from r50k_base (V = 50,257) to o200k_base (V = 200,019) shortened the Signal Timing Manual by 7.6% (157,196 → 145,220 tokens) for a 4× larger table. The BPE merge rule itself is a greedy compression algorithm: Gage (1994), applied to text by Sennrich, Haddow & Birch (2016), applied at the byte level by Radford et al. (2019).

Across the whole Signal Timing Manual — 103,673 whitespace-separated words — the ratio settles at 1.401 tokens per word, or 4.90 characters per token. That number is the one to remember when someone asks "will this document fit?"

2 · The space belongs to the token

The same five letters cost one token mid-sentence and two at the start of a line, because ·lidar earned its own vocabulary entry and lidar did not. A leading space is not whitespace to be stripped — it is part of the symbol.

3 · Case is a different symbol

Your report writes LiDAR, the vendor spec writes LIDAR, the paper writes lidar. To the model these are three unrelated symbol sequences costing 2, 3, and 1 token. Nothing in the architecture knows they are the same instrument — that has to be learned, from data, at some cost.

The practical consequence: a 20-character row of detector data, 07:00,1842,34.6,18.2, costs 14 tokens — 1.44 characters per token. A paragraph of prose costs 5.66 characters per token. Numeric data is about four times more expensive per character than English. Pasting a day of 5-minute detector counts into a chat window is not a cheap way to analyze it.

5 · Your jargon is fragmented; ordinary English is not

The vocabulary is a frequency snapshot of a general web corpus. Words common on the internet get one symbol; words common in a signal timing office do not.

One token

Two or more

Measured on the manual. Of its 5,131 distinct alphabetic word types, 4,038 (78.7%) are a single token; those cover 96.0% of all word occurrences. The 21.3% that fragment are not random — they are ring-and-barrier, protected-permissive, semi-actuated, lead-lag, volume-to-capacity, time-of-day. The tokenizer shatters precisely the terms that carry the domain.

6 · There is no such thing as an unknown word — and that is the real break from Word2Vec

Word2Vec (Sept 10, Lab 2) had a fixed list of word types and an UNK bucket for everything else — a new vendor name, a misspelling in a crash narrative, a road you had not seen. Byte-level BPE has single-byte entries for every possible byte, so every string encodes. Nothing is ever rejected.

What you gain. No out-of-vocabulary failures. V2X, TSM&O, a garbled OCR line, a Spanish street name, a Python snippet — all encode without special handling.
What you pay. Failure becomes silent and gradual instead of loud. An unfamiliar term is not rejected; it is shredded into pieces and costs more tokens, and you only notice in the bill and the accuracy.
And the classic failure this explains: ask a model how many characters are in I-275 and it is working from three symbols — I, -, 275 — not five characters. Character-level tasks (counting letters, reversing a string, checking a station-ID checksum, spotting a typo in a route number) are hard for reasons that have nothing to do with intelligence and everything to do with the unit.

Two generations of the same idea

r50k_base (V = 50,257) is GPT-2's tokenizer, 2019 — the one in this course's gpt2_tokens_and_embeddings.py demo. o200k_base (V = 200,019) is the current OpenAI generation. Four times the vocabulary buys about 7.6% fewer tokens on the Signal Timing Manual (157,196 → 145,220). Try k = 45 veh/mi/ln, v = 32 mph, q = 1440 veh/h/ln: the newer tokenizer is worse there (25 tokens vs 22), because its pre-token rule refuses to attach a space to a digit run. Bigger and newer is not uniformly better on engineering text — measure it on your corpus.

One honest limitation of this page. To keep the file small it ships the ASCII portion of each merge table — 128,803 of the 199,998 o200k_base merges and 49,353 of the 50,256 r50k_base merges. For ASCII input the result is exactly what the real tokenizer produces, because merges involving non-ASCII bytes can never apply to ASCII bytes. Paste an em-dash, a curly quote, an accented name or a CJK character and the page will tell you it cannot answer — the real tokenizer can, and would encode them as multi-byte sequences.

Where the vocabulary came from

Nobody wrote those 200,019 entries by hand. They were learned, by an algorithm with one rule, run over a very large corpus. The rule is simple enough to run here on 280 words of the Signal Timing Manual and watch it build a signal-timing vocabulary from scratch.

Byte-Pair Encoding, the whole algorithm

start: every word is a sequence of single characters, the first carrying its leading space repeat: find the most frequent adjacent pair in the corpus → merge it everywhere → record it stop: after V merges

In words: whatever two symbols sit next to each other most often become one symbol. Repeat 200,000 times. Frequency is the only criterion — not meaning, not morphology, not whether an engineer would call the result a word.

Going deeper — what the real training run changes, and what it does not (optional)
Three differences from the toy above, none of which change the logic. (a) Bytes, not characters: production BPE starts from the 256 possible bytes, so the vocabulary can represent any string and an UNK token is never needed. (b) A pre-token regex first: merges are only allowed within chunks produced by a fixed regular expression, which is what forbids a merge across a space or across a digit boundary — and why o200k_base cuts every number into runs of at most three digits. (c) Scale: hundreds of billions of characters instead of 280 words, so the frequency estimate is stable. What does not change: the tokenizer is fit once, before the network exists, has no learnable parameters, and is never updated by training. If your domain was rare in that corpus, you inherit the penalty for the life of the model. The fix is not to retrain the tokenizer — it is to measure your corpus's tokens-per-word and budget for it, or to choose a model whose tokenizer was fit on text like yours. What a token is when the data is not text

Transformers do not require language. They require a sequence of symbols from a finite vocabulary. So the transportation question is not "how do I tokenize my text" — it is "what is one symbol in my data, and who decided?" Real mobility systems answer it very differently, and the answers are published.

Fit card, failure modes, and what to check

Fit card — Sub-word tokenization (byte-level BPE)

Idea in one sentenceLearn a fixed vocabulary of frequent byte strings from a corpus, then represent any input as a sequence of integers indexing that vocabulary.
Why it works (the logic)Frequent things get short codes and rare things get built from pieces, so a fixed-size vocabulary covers an unbounded language with no unknown-word case.
Fits these tasksAny transformer over text or over any signal you are willing to discretize — extraction, classification, generation, retrieval, prediction.
Needs this dataTo use: nothing, the vocabulary ships with the model. To judge: a sample of your own corpus, to measure tokens per word and per record. Anyone with a document archive has this.
Breaks whenThe corpus is numeric (1.44 chars/token), heavy in domain jargon, non-English, or OCR'd badly; and on any task that needs to see inside a token — counting characters, checksums, spelling.
Keep the classical method whenThe values are already numeric and structured. A detector archive belongs in a dataframe and a Kalman filter, not in a context window — you would be paying 14 tokens to transmit 20 characters that float64 stores in 8 bytes.

Where it breaks, in transportation terms

  • Distribution shift, in the vocabulary. A tokenizer fit on the open web meets a corpus of FDOT plan sheets. Nothing errors; your token bill rises and long documents stop fitting.
  • Silent cost blowups. The manual is 145,220 tokens; the same page count of scanned tables with OCR noise can be far more. Measure before you promise a client a price.
  • Chunk boundaries are drawn in tokens. A RAG index that splits every 512 tokens will cut a timing table in half. The retrieval failure looks like a model failure.
  • Character-level tasks. Route-number typos, station-ID checksums, counting occurrences of a letter — all sit below the unit and fail for structural reasons.
  • Numeric reasoning. 11 | , | 247 is three unrelated symbols. Do not ask an LLM to do the arithmetic you would put in a spreadsheet.
  • For your own tokens: a quantization you chose is a modelling assumption you are obliged to defend. If a 4 mph deceleration cannot be represented, your model cannot predict rear-end conflicts, no matter how it scores.

Evaluation close — the three questions, every time

  • What is the baseline? For the tokenizer comparison on this page, the baseline is r50k_base (2019). o200k is 7.6% cheaper on the manual's prose and more expensive on the units line — so "newer tokenizer" is not by itself an improvement claim. For a trajectory tokenization, the baseline is the continuous model: a Kalman filter that never quantizes at all.
  • How was the data split, and why is that honest? There is no fitting on this page, so there is no split to defend — the counts are a census of one public document (FHWA-HOP-08-024, 274 pages) with the text extracted by pdftotext, and the browser tokenizer was checked against tiktoken on 3,084 strings including 60 passages of that manual. The honesty risk here is the extraction, not the split: different PDF extractors give different character counts, so the tokens-per-word ratio is the transferable number, not the absolute total.
  • What does it do on the ugly cases? Numeric tables (1.44 chars/token), hyphenated domain compounds (Ring|-and|-bar|rier), all-caps acronyms (L|ID|AR), and anything non-ASCII, which this page refuses outright rather than guessing.
