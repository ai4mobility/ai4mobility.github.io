What the backbone owes the head

Nearly every classifier and regressor you have met so far ends the same way: a large backbone, then one linear layer that reads the answer off (the detection and segmentation heads of 1.4 are the exception, and they are not linear). ResNet-50 keeps 92% of its weights in the backbone; BERT ships with no head at all and gets a 1,538-parameter one bolted on. This page asks the question that structure raises. A linear head can only draw flat cuts — so what must the backbone hand it? Which shape of embedding makes a flat cut enough, how would you measure whether you have it, and what does that shape cost you later? Every number here is computed by the reproducer next to this page, on a -parameter network with a -parameter head, trained from scratch on real NHTSA vehicle-owner complaints.

The head is linear. That is a demand on the backbone, not a limitation of the head.

You saw in the anatomy companion that the exit of a deep network changes with the task while the body stays the same, and in the backbone-and-head slides that the exit can be as small as 4,098 numbers on top of 23.5 million. The picture below is why that works — and it is the whole page in one figure.

A linear head can only cut with flat surfaces. The backbone's job is to arrange the data so that flat cuts are enough — and when the two are trained together, the backbone goes further than "enough": it builds a very specific shape for the head. The rest of this page measures that sentence: what flat cuts can do (tab 1), how many points they can cope with (tab 2), where along a real backbone the task becomes flat-cuttable (tab 3), what shape training converges to (tab 4), and what that shape costs you when you want to reuse the backbone (tab 5).

A linear head draws hyperplanes; K of them carve convex regions

Before asking what the backbone should produce, pin down what the head can consume. It is less than it looks and more than it looks, and both halves matter.

the head: one score per class, linear in the embedding sk = wk · z + bk class = argmaxk sk the region the head assigns to class k Rk = { z : (wk − wj) · z + (bk − bj) ≥ 0 for every other class j }
zthe embedding the backbone hands over — here numbers per complaint wk, bkthe head's weight row and bias for class k — in discrete-choice terms, the taste weights and the alternative-specific constant of alternative k skthe logit — the "utility" of class k; softmax turns the K of them into probabilities, exactly as the MNL does RkK − 1 linear inequalities at once: an intersection of half-spaces, which is why it is convex

In words: the head is a multinomial logit whose utilities are linear in z. Everything you know about a linear-in-parameters utility applies — including what it cannot express. For a regression head the demand is even plainer: ŷ = w · z + b, so the target has to be an (almost) affine function of the embedding; the surfaces of constant target in z must be parallel hyperplanes. Lab 8's probes are exactly that test: the VAE latent read traffic density back at R² ≈ 0.86–0.91 (affine enough) and inflow at 0.06 (not there at all).

Three things a flat cut cannot do — try the first one

1 · A rule that is "different from" — wrong-way driving

A wrong-way event is a vehicle whose heading disagrees with the lane's designated direction. Code both as 0 = east, 1 = west: the four possible cases are the corners of a square, and the two wrong-way corners are the diagonal. Move the line with the sliders and try to put both wrong-way cases on one side and both normal cases on the other. Checked by brute force over lines: the best any line does is of 4. The information is all there — the label is a deterministic function of the two inputs — and a linear head still cannot reach it. Present is not the same as linearly accessible.

The two wrong-way cases, E heading in a W lane and W heading in an E lane, land on the same point — the origin — and the two normal cases are pushed out along different axes. The layer did not "add information"; it threw away the one distinction the head did not need (which direction was wrong) and kept the one it did. That is invariance, and it is the whole idea of a representation: make the cases the head should treat alike indistinguishable, and the cases it should separate far apart. Now a single line gets 4 of 4. (By hand you can do the same with z₁ = ReLU(h − l), z₂ = ReLU(l − h); then the two normal cases coincide instead.)

2 · A class in the middle of one axis

Put stop-and-go traffic between jam and free flow on a speed axis, and ask a two-class head to flag "unstable" from speed alone. 3 · Any class that is not convex

A ring, a crescent, two separate blobs of the same class with another class between them — anything whose region cannot be an intersection of half-spaces. Anomaly detection framed as "normal in the middle, anomalous around it" is the textbook case, and it is why one-class methods do not end in a softmax. The backbone's answer is always the same: move the points, not the cut. Fold the ring open, pull the two blobs together, straighten the crescent — and the head's flat cut is enough again.

Convex-region reasoning also tells you what a head cannot be blamed for. If a linear probe fails on a frozen encoder, the encoder either discarded the information or left it tangled. Tab 3 shows how to tell those apart.

Going deeper — why the regions are convex, and how much a hyperplane can memorise

Rk is the set of z satisfying K − 1 inequalities of the form a · z + c ≥ 0. Each inequality is a half-space; a half-space is convex; an intersection of convex sets is convex. So no softmax head — whatever its weights — can own a non-convex region, and with K = 2 the single inequality is one hyperplane. The same argument says nothing about what the head can do once the points have been moved, which is the backbone's whole job. The classical capacity statement is that hyperplanes in d dimensions have VC dimension d + 1: there exist d + 1 points they can label every possible way, and no set of d + 2. Tab 2 turns that into the number that matters for probes. Vapnik & Chervonenkis 1971; Bishop, Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts, ch. 4 (linear discriminants).

Capacity has a number: about two points per dimension

Tab 1 said what shapes a flat cut can separate. This tab says how many points it can separate regardless of shape — and why that number decides whether a "linear probe" measures anything at all.

Cover (1965): N points in general position in d dimensions, a hyperplane with a bias number of labellings it can realise C(N, d+1) = 2 · Σk=0..d C(N−1, k) share of all 2N labellings P(N, d) = C(N, d+1) / 2N the two landmarks N ≤ d + 1 → P = 1 every labelling, however random, fits perfectly N = 2(d + 1) → P = 1/2 and it falls to zero within a few more points
Nhow many points the head is asked to fit — the complaints in a probe's training fold dthe embedding's dimension — here , so every labelling fits up to N = and half of them at N = general positionno d + 1 of the points lie on one hyperplane — true of real features unless some dimensions are dead (see below)

In words: a linear head in d dimensions can memorise any labelling of about 2d points. It does not need the labels to mean anything. That cuts both ways: it is why a wide embedding makes nearly any task look linearly separable, and it is why a probe fit on too few points proves nothing.

The trap this sets — the same head, two feature spaces

TF-IDF space: features for complaints

The classical pipeline is itself a lift into a very high-dimensional space — one coordinate per word and word pair. There are points per dimension, far below Cover's limit of 2. The rule for anyone reporting a linear probe. The probe's dimension is part of the instrument. Report N / (d + 1) alongside the score; below about 2, a probe's training-fold accuracy is a statement about Cover's theorem, not about the representation. Score on a held-out fold, always — and when N / (d + 1) is small, run the control from Lab 8: fit the same probe on shuffled labels (or a control task) and subtract. The Sept 17 crash-narrative companion is a live case: 399 narratives in a 384-dimensional MiniLM space, so each five-fold training fold of ≈ 320 narratives sits below d + 1 = 385 and can fit any labelling exactly. Its cross-validated average-precision numbers are the only ones that counted, which is why those were the ones it reported.
Going deeper — where the count comes from, and what "capacity" means for the head you actually train

Add one point in general position to a set of N. Every hyperplane that separated the old points either lands the new point on a definite side (the old labelling survives once) or can be rotated about the old points to put it on either side (the old labelling survives twice). Counting those cases gives the recursion C(N+1, m) = C(N, m) + C(N, m−1), and solving it gives the sum above. Two consequences worth keeping. First, the transition is sharp: for large d the share of realisable labellings drops from ≈ 1 to ≈ 0 over a band of a few dozen points around N = 2(d+1). Second, the head you train jointly with the backbone always sees N ≫ 2d — points per dimension here — so it cannot memorise; every bit of its training accuracy had to come from the backbone arranging the points. That is the sense in which the head's weakness is a feature: it forces the backbone to do the work. Cover, T. M. (1965). The floor: what an untrained backbone already gives you

The deeper random layers scramble that: z at initialisation reads (MLP ), close to chance. This is the random-encoder floor Lab 8 insists on, and it says two things: the credit for L0 belongs mostly to the words, and the credit for z belongs entirely to training.

Before you trust this result

So the answer to "what does the head need?" has a precise endpoint: six tight clusters, equally far from the centre, and a head whose rows are the arrows to them. Once that shape exists, the 390-parameter head is not doing classification in any interesting sense — it is looking up the nearest centre. All the intelligence is in where the backbone put the points. This is also why the head can be so small: a nearest-centre rule needs one vector per class, and that is exactly what a linear layer stores.

Going deeper — why cross-entropy plus weight decay pushes toward the simplex, and where the picture bends

Treat the last-layer features as free variables (the "unconstrained features" model). Cross-entropy wants every logit margin large, which pulls each class's points toward a common direction and pushes the K directions apart; weight decay caps the total length of features and head rows. The configuration that maximises the minimum margin under a length budget is the simplex equiangular tight frame with the head rows aligned to it — the global minimiser of that idealised problem (Mixon, Parshall & Pi 2020; Fang, He, Long & Su 2021). Real backbones approach it in the terminal phase to the extent the data and the optimiser keep pushing — and on this specimen a hundredfold stronger weight decay changed nothing, so the plateau is the specimen's, not the optimiser's.

Where it bends. Imbalance: with a minority class the minority means merge ("minority collapse") — with 116 crashes in 4,200 complaints, a crash-vs-not head would not get a clean simplex. Held-out data: collapse is a property of the training set; on new complaints the clusters are looser (NC1 versus here) and the gap between the two is the generalisation gap in geometric form. Semantics: nothing in the objective rewards keeping Engine near Power train, and nothing forbids it either — at finite training the angles keep what the data suggested. Papyan, Han & Donoho (2020), PNAS 117(40); Fang, He, Long & Su (2021), PNAS 118(43); Mixon, Parshall & Pi (2020), arXiv:2011.11619.

The ideal z for the head you trained is a poor z for the head you have not trained yet

Collapse means throwing away within-class variation. Sometimes that variation is exactly what the next task needs. NHTSA's file carries a second field the backbone never saw: whether the complaint involved a crash ( of ). Freeze the component-trained backbone at every checkpoint, fit a linear probe for the crash flag at every tap, and score it on the held-out complaints ( crashes among them — too few to trust the test split alone, so validation and test are pooled and the probe's penalty is chosen by cross-validation inside the training split).

AUC = probability that a random crash complaint scores above a random non-crash one; 0.5 is chance. Confidence intervals are bootstraps over the held-out complaints.

The words know. TF-IDF features — no backbone, no label of any kind used to build them — read the crash flag at AUC . The information is in the text.

The trained embedding mostly does not. After epochs on the component task, z reads the crash flag at (), and the logits at . It drained as training went on. z read the crash flag best at epoch (), when the component task itself was only at held-out — the backbone was still learning what words mean rather than which six answers exist — and it lost of that by the end (paired bootstrap of the difference: ). Everything after epoch 12 — the terminal phase that tab 4 showed tightening the six clusters — bought nothing for the component task on held-out complaints and cost a little more for the crash task.

Depth is not the cure. Every convolution block, not just z, was trained by the component loss alone, and every one of them reads the crash flag at 0.67–0.72 at the end. The tap that keeps the most is the one closest to the words: the word-vector mean at (paired difference against z: ). Taking "one layer earlier" helps only if that layer had some other reason to keep the information — here none of them did.

The regex control says the same, louder. Whether a complaint mentions a highway, freeway or interstate is a fact any word-count model reads almost perfectly (TF-IDF: ). From z it reads at — against from the component label alone. Indistinguishable: the collapsed embedding kept the label and nothing else about the road.

The same lesson from the label-free side, in Lab 8. The VAE encoder there was trained on no label at all. Its hidden layer read "bottleneck present" better than the raw pixels (0.753 against 0.653) while its 2-D latent lost inflow entirely (R² 0.06, and no MLP could bring it back). A label-free objective keeps things no head asked for — up to the point where its own bottleneck discards them. A supervised backbone keeps what its label needed, and the tighter it fits, the less else survives. Kornblith et al. (2021) measured the same trade across ImageNet models: the training objectives that separate the classes most cleanly in the penultimate layer transfer worst to new tasks.

Design rules that follow

  1. One task, one head: let it collapse. The tight-cluster geometry is what makes a 390-parameter head enough. Nothing is wrong with it.
  2. Reuse in mind: give the backbone a reason to keep more. Train it with an objective that has no single label to collapse onto — masking or reconstruction (BERT, the VAE), contrastive pairs (CLIP) — or with several heads at once. "General-purpose" is a property of the training signal, not of depth.
  3. Adapting a pretrained backbone: probe first, then fine-tune. Fit the new linear head on frozen features before unfreezing anything (linear probing, then fine-tuning). A randomly initialised head fine-tuned end-to-end rewrites the features on its first steps and can destroy exactly what you wanted to reuse (Kumar et al. 2022).
  4. Stop when held-out accuracy stops. The terminal phase tightens the training geometry and drains everything else; it did not add a point of held-out accuracy here.
  5. Audit with three probes, not one. Linear, MLP and a floor (random encoder or shuffled labels), on held-out data at a known N / (d + 1). If the MLP cannot beat the linear probe, the ceiling is upstream — go back to the objective, not the head.

Check yourself

  1. A vendor's report shows a linear probe reaching 97% for "work-zone present" on their frozen 1,024-dimensional video encoder, fit on 900 clips. What one number do you ask for before you read further, and what control would settle it?
  2. You fine-tuned an ImageNet backbone hard on 40,000 labelled dashcam frames for "vehicle vs. not", to 99.6% training accuracy, and now want a second head for "wet pavement" from the same features. Predict what the linear probe will show, say why, and name two things you would change in the training recipe.
  3. Two representations of detector data reach the same held-out accuracy for regime classification. In one, k-NN matches the linear probe; in the other, k-NN is 12 points lower. Which would you rather build a nearest-neighbour "find similar days" tool on, and what does the gap tell you about the geometry?
