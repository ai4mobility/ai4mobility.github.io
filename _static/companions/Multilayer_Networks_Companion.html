What it means for the basis functions to be learnable

Bishop §6.2: "the key idea behind neural networks is to choose basis functions φj(x) that themselves have learnable parameters." You have been choosing basis functions your whole career — the interaction terms in a mode-choice utility, the breakpoint in a triangular fundamental diagram, the knots in a spline. This page makes that step visible, then hands it to the optimizer and measures exactly what you gain and what you lose. Some of the results below argue against neural networks. Everything runs locally in your browser.

The specification step is a fit you did by eye

A linear basis-function model is y(x,w) = Σ wj φj(x). It is linear in w, the error surface is a bowl, and one matrix solve gives you the global optimum. That is a real and underrated advantage. But it only holds after somebody has written down the φ's — and that somebody was you.

The specification step feels like modeling, not fitting, which is why it is easy to miss. But you did choose those functions, you chose them before seeing the residuals, and nothing in the estimation loop could tell you that you chose wrong. Bishop's sentence is about closing that loop.

Now let the knots move

Same data, same basis family — hinges, max(0, a·x + b) — so this is a clean apples-to-apples test. The only difference is whether the knot positions are placed by you on a grid or learned by gradient descent along with the output weights. This is exactly Bishop's (6.7)–(6.8) in one dimension, and a ReLU network in one dimension is a free-knot linear spline.

fixed knots y = w₀ + Σj wj · max(0, x − κj) κj on a grid — 1 free parameter per basis function free knots y = w₀ + Σj wj · max(0, ajx + bj) knot at −bj/aj — 3 free parameters per basis function

Read the second line as a hidden layer: aj is the weight, bj the bias, wj the output weight. The bias is the location of the kink and the weight is its steepness. That is what a learnable parameter inside φ physically means here: where the breakpoint goes.

What the right-hand plot shows. The knots start evenly spread. Within a few hundred steps most of them stampede to the queue front at 0.45 and stack up there, because that is where the error is. Nobody told the model where the shockwave was. That migration is Bishop's sentence, animated: the basis functions have parameters, and the parameters are being adjusted by the same gradient that fits the output weights.
And now the honest part. Press match parameter counts. At 25 parameters each, the fixed grid wins — by a wide margin. This is not a bug in the training; run it from several starts and the answer holds. The arithmetic is the reason: in one dimension a fixed basis function costs 1 parameter and a learnable one costs 3. You are paying triple for the privilege of placing each knot, and on a single axis you did not need to place them well — you could just cover the whole line. In one dimension, learnable basis functions are a bad deal.
Keep that result. Anyone who tells you neural networks are better function approximators has not run this experiment. The next tab is where the arithmetic reverses, and it reverses for a reason you can compute on paper.

Where it flips: dimension

A fixed basis has to cover the input space before it knows where the function varies. Covering a line is cheap. Covering a 10-dimensional space is not. A learnable basis function costs more each, but you need vastly fewer of them — provided the function has structure worth aligning to. That trade has an exact crossover, and it is much earlier than most people guess.

With five knots per axis and eight hidden units the crossover is at D = 3. Three inputs. That is a car-following state vector with the lead-vehicle terms dropped. Every real mobility problem you care about — a detector vector, a trajectory window, an image patch — is far to the right of the crossing, where the gap is not a factor of two but a factor of 105.

Does it actually work? A breakdown pocket on a diagonal

The target: risk peaks when one particular weighted combination of D conditions sits in a narrow band — y = exp(−4(u − ½)²) with u = a·x, and the direction a unknown to every model. Physically: breakdown risk that depends on a combined index of flow, truck share, rain, grade and ramp spacing, not on any single one. 500 training samples, whatever D you pick.

Three contenders, and the fixed bases are given every advantage — bandwidth and ridge penalty tuned by grid search on the test set, which is cheating in their favour:

Read the right-hand plot first. Those are 32-dimensional points. Plotted against the single direction the network discovered, they collapse onto a clean one-dimensional curve. The network did not memorize a 32-D function — it found the one combination that mattered and then had an easy 1-D problem left. Learning wj is learning which direction in input space matters.
The additive grid fails for a structural reason worth naming in class: it has a term for every input separately and no term for any combination, so it cannot represent a ridge that runs diagonally. This is the failure mode of every model where you specified the main effects and forgot an interaction — and in D dimensions there are 2D possible interactions to forget.

Why jointly is the load-bearing word

Bishop says the basis-function parameters are adjusted "along with the coefficients {wj}, during training." You could instead do it in two stages: compress the inputs first with PCA, then fit a model on the components. People do this constantly with detector data. Here is what it costs.

Ten detectors, one predictive combination

Ten correlated detector readings. Nine directions carry the big daily volume swings. One direction — a small differential between neighbouring stations — carries almost no variance and all of the predictive signal for the outcome. Both pipelines below end in the same 8-unit network with the same training budget. The only difference is whether the projection into a k-dimensional space is chosen by variance or by the loss.

Slide k from 1 to 9 and the two-stage pipeline sits at or slightly worse than the predict-the-mean baseline, no matter how much variance it keeps — 99.8% of the variance at k = 9 buys nothing. It only recovers at k = 10 — that is, when it throws nothing away and the "compression" is just a rotation. PCA is not bad at compression; it is bad at knowing what to keep, because the criterion it optimizes (variance in x) has no connection to the thing you are predicting.
Joint training is what removes that disconnect. The gradient arriving at wj(1) carries one message: move this basis function so that the prediction error goes down. The features become task-relevant because the task defined them. That is the whole content of the word "jointly", and it is why the last layer of a network is still an ordinary linear model — everything before it is a machine for manufacturing the regressors that linear model needs.
For your own work: the same argument applies to any unsupervised preprocessing you bolt on the front — PCA on detector matrices, k-means on trajectory clusters, hand-rolled congestion indices. None of them can see the label. If the target depends on something those steps discard, no downstream model can recover it.

What learnable basis functions cost

Bishop's next sentence is the price tag: "we then optimize the whole model by minimizing an error function using gradient-based optimization methods." The moment φ has parameters, the model stops being linear in its parameters. Three consequences, all measurable.

1 · The parameters mean nothing individually

Relabel hidden unit 3 as unit 7 and unit 7 as unit 3, carrying their weights with them. Different parameter vector, bit-for-bit identical function. With M = 8 units there are 8! = 40,320 relabellings of every solution, and sign flips on top of that.

–
This is why a hidden weight is not a β. In a logit model, a coefficient has a unit, a sign, and a confidence interval you can defend to a review panel. In a network, the individual φj are not identified at all — only the composed function is. If a vendor shows you "the feature the model learned for pedestrian risk", ask them what happens to that story under a relabelling.

2 · Two runs of the same code give two different models

Six free-knot fits to the corridor data, identical except for the random seed. Look at the knot sets: different counts of active hinges, different placements, some units driven clean out of the domain and left dead. Then look at the curves.

Non-convexity is not a footnote you can cite and move past. It means "the model" is not a well-defined object — it is whatever this run of this optimizer landed on. Report the seed, run more than one, and never present a single training run as if it were an estimate.

3 · It needs enough data to find the structure, and below that it is worse than nothing

Back to the diagonal breakdown pocket, now at D = 12, sweeping the training-set size with three seeds each. Watch for a threshold rather than a gentle curve.

There is no gentle improvement. Below the threshold the learned basis is no better than predicting the mean, and usually worse — it has enough freedom to chase noise and not enough data to locate the real direction. Right at the threshold the seeds scatter: some runs find the direction, some do not, from identical code. Above it the error falls by nearly two orders of magnitude in a single doubling of the sample size. The fixed basis, meanwhile, improves smoothly and never gets anywhere, at any sample size on the plot.
The practical reading. "We tried a neural network and it did not beat our regression" is ambiguous evidence. It can mean the problem has no learnable structure — or it can mean you were on the left side of that cliff. The two look identical in a results table and are fixed by completely different things.

And remember tab 2

On the one-dimensional corridor, at matched parameter counts, the hand-placed grid beat the learned basis. Small data, a low-dimensional input, and a functional form you actually know: that is the regime where the classical model is not the baseline you have to beat — it is the right answer. Learnable φ earns its cost when the input space is too big to tile and the function has structure to align to and you have the samples to find it. Three conditions, not one.

Two things students get wrong about this section

Misconception 1 · "Universal approximation is why neural networks work"

A two-layer network with enough hidden units can approximate any continuous function to any accuracy. True — and not the reason. A fixed Gaussian grid is also a universal approximator on a bounded interval. Watch a fixed basis walk down to the noise floor on the corridor data with no learnable parameters anywhere in sight.

Fixed Gaussian gridParametersTest RMSE

RMSE is measured against the noise-free truth, so it can and does fall below the 1.5 mph observation noise. Adding basis functions past M = 60 stops helping — at that point the error is set by the 120 samples, not by the basis.

Universality is cheap. Both families have it; it cannot be what separates them. What separates them is how many basis functions you need to get there in D dimensions — which is the counting argument from tab 3, not an approximation theorem. Teaching the theorem as the punchline leaves students unable to explain why anyone abandoned splines.

Misconception 2 · "Depth is what adds the nonlinearity"

Bishop notes that h(·) must be a differentiable nonlinear function, and that the construction extends recursively. Both conditions are load-bearing. Set h to the identity and stack as many layers as you like — the composition collapses to a single matrix.

With the identity, the discrepancy is at machine precision — the eight-layer network and the one-layer linear model are the same function, and all those extra parameters are pure redundancy. Switch to tanh and the two curves separate immediately. Nonlinearity and learnability only pay off together: learnable parameters inside a linear φ buy you nothing at all.
The classroom test, worth keeping for the rest of the course: strip every nonlinearity out of an architecture; if it still does the job, you did not need the network.

The whole section in five lines

The ideaA network is a linear model whose regressors are also being fitted. The last layer is still ordinary least squares; everything before it manufactures the features.
The mechanismEach φj = h(wj·x + wj0) has a direction (which combination of inputs it responds to) and an offset (where along that direction it switches). Both are trained.
The reasonNot expressiveness — parameter efficiency in high dimensions. Fixed grid KD vs learned M(D+2)+1; they cross at about D = 3.
The requirementJoint training against the task loss, and a nonlinear differentiable h. Drop either one and the idea evaporates.
The billNo closed form, no convexity, no identifiability, no single answer, and a sample-size threshold below which it is worse than a constant.
