What a neural network is, and how it learns

Two questions, one page. First: what are the parts — the weights, the connections, the bias, the activation function — and what does each one physically do to a prediction? Second: when we say the network "learns," what exactly moves, and who decides which direction? Everything here runs on two transportation problems you can hold in your head: a loop detector deciding whether a freeway segment is congested, and the same detector deciding whether it is about to break down. Every number on this page is computed live in your browser from a fixed seed — nothing is a stock illustration, and nothing is asserted that you cannot re-derive by dragging a slider.

A neuron is three decisions and one squash

One detector station on a freeway reports two things every 5 minutes: density k (veh/mi/lane) and demand-to-capacity ratio v/c. We want one number out: the probability that this segment is congested — defined here as mean speed below 45 mph. That is a whole neuron's job. It has exactly three tunable numbers and one fixed shape.

the two inputs, rescaled to 0–1 so the weights are comparable x₁ = k / 90 x₂ = (v/c − 0.30) / 0.75 weighted sum + bias (this is the whole "linear" part) z = w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + b activation: squash z into a probability ŷ = σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)

Three learnable numbers: w₁, w₂, b. If you have ever estimated a binary logit mode-choice model, you have already fitted this exact object — the scale-times-utility term is z, the alternative-specific constant is b. What is different in a neural network is not the equation. It is that these numbers are found by gradient descent instead of maximum likelihood, and that thousands of them get stacked.

What each part is actually responsible for

PartWhat it does geometricallyWhat it means for this detector
Weight w₁, w₂Sets the tilt of the decision line and how fast the answer changes as you move. The vector (w₁,w₂) points straight uphill, perpendicular to the line. How strongly each measurement counts, and in which direction. Raise w₁ and density starts dominating; make it negative and more density means less congestion, which is exactly the kind of sign error a fit will catch for you.
Bias bSlides the line without rotating it. Without a bias the line is nailed to the origin — it must pass through k = 0, v/c = 0.30. The threshold. It is the answer to "how loaded does this segment have to be before I call it congested?" — and it is the single parameter people most often forget a model needs.
Activation σBends the straight-line score into the 0–1 range with a smooth S. Far from the line it saturates; near the line it is steep. Turns a score into a probability you can threshold, price, or feed to a downstream decision — and, critically, gives a gradient, which the hard step function does not.
ConnectionSimply the fact that xi has a wi at all. "Is this detector wired into this decision?" A weight of exactly zero is a missing wire. In bigger networks the wiring pattern is the architecture.
Try the activation menu. Switch to step: the picture barely changes, but the surface is now flat everywhere — every derivative is zero, so no gradient method can tell you which way to move. That is precisely why the 1958 perceptron needed its own bespoke update rule and why it could not be stacked. Switch to linear: the model happily predicts probabilities of −1.4 and +2.3. The squash is not decoration.
Press "Refit with the bias frozen at 0." Same data, same two weights, one parameter removed. Accuracy falls from 94.0% to 71.8% and the log-loss rises from 0.116 to 0.528. The bias is not a nuisance term; it is where the threshold lives.
A weight is not "importance." The fit lands on w = (26.95, 8.47), b = −16.91. Translated back into engineering units that boundary is 0.299·k + 11.29·(v/c) = 20.30, so the model has recovered a demand-to-density trade-off ratio of 37.7 where the truth used to generate the data is 33.3 — close, and the comparison is only meaningful because both inputs were rescaled to 0–1 first. Feed a network raw k in the hundreds alongside v/c near 1.0 and the weight magnitudes tell you about your units, not your physics.
Watch the fit past step 50. Accuracy reaches 94.0% at about step 50 and then never moves again — but the weights keep growing, from a magnitude of about 7.4 to about 28. Gradient descent has stopped changing the decision and is now only sharpening the confidence, because log-loss always pays a little more for being more certain about points it already gets right. That gap between "accuracy stopped improving" and "the loss is still falling" is the reason we watch both.

Same detector, harder question — and the line runs out

Keep the station. Change the question from "is it congested right now" to "will flow break down in the next 15 minutes." Breakdown is not monotone in density. Below the critical range there is nothing to break down; above it the facility is already in queue discharge, so the event has come and gone. The risk lives in a band of density, and only when demand is high enough to push through it. That single change — from a threshold to a band — is enough to end what one neuron can do, and it is the reason the rest of this page exists.

"Fine — I'll stack more layers"

The instinct is right and the first attempt fails in a specific, checkable way. Stack as many layers as you like, but leave out the activation function, and every layer is a matrix multiply followed by an add. Composing those gives you… a matrix multiply followed by an add. With the activation set to identity: an 8-layer, 5-unit-wide stack holds 231 parameters, and every one of them is spent computing a function that 3 numbers reproduce to within 1.3 × 10−15 — floating-point noise. Switch the menu to tanh and the deviation jumps by fourteen orders of magnitude, to about 0.13. Depth without nonlinearity is not a deep model; it is an expensive line.
So the activation function has two jobs, not one. On tab 1 it turned a score into a probability. Here it does something structurally different: it is the only reason stacking layers creates a function that a single layer could not have expressed. Remove it and depth collapses. That is why "which activation" is a real engineering question and "should there be one" is not.

Connections: one neuron becomes a layer

A layer is not a new idea. It is H copies of the neuron from tab 1, each with its own weights and its own bias, all reading the same two inputs — and then one more neuron on top that reads their outputs instead of the raw detector. What one hidden unit contributes

Click any hidden unit above. Each one draws its own soft half-plane over the detector space — a tilted, blurred edge, positioned by its bias and oriented by its weights. It is a feature detector: "is density above roughly 34?", "is demand high?" The output neuron then takes a weighted vote of those answers. The network's expressiveness comes entirely from combining edges.

The published solution scores 0.780 balanced accuracy on held-out data. Gradient descent on the same architecture reaches 0.797. You can set a neural network's weights by hand, if you happen to know the answer, and here you do — you were told the labels came from three gates. Hold on to that: the weights are not magic, they are just the answer written down. Training is what you do when nobody told you the answer.
But look at the log-loss. Your hand-built version: 0.547. The trained version: 0.334. Same accuracy, far worse probabilities — because you set the gates to be nearly hard, so the model announces 0.99 and 0.01 where the honest answer is 0.7. On a screen that only shows accuracy these two models look identical. If anything downstream consumes the probability — a ramp meter, a dispatch threshold, an expected-delay calculation — they are not remotely the same model.
One more thing worth knowing before tab 5. We built the truth from three gates and gave the network exactly three units, and it did not recover our three gates. Trained from a random start it finds one rising density edge crossing at k ≈ 34, one falling density edge crossing at k ≈ 57, and a third unit that sits saturated across the whole domain acting as an offset; demand enters through a mild positive tilt shared by all three, not as a gate of its own. Click the units above and read their weights — the two density edges are the ones with the large ±w on density. It gets the same answer by a different decomposition. Hidden units are not guaranteed to be the factors you had in mind, and reading meaning into individual units is a claim that needs evidence, not a free interpretation.

Backpropagation: one sample, nine numbers, nothing hidden

Here is the whole learning problem. The network below has nine tunable numbers. A detector interval comes in, the network produces a probability, the probability is wrong by some amount, and now somebody has to answer nine separate questions: should this particular number go up or down, and by how much? Backpropagation is the answer, and it is not a learning algorithm — it is a derivative calculator. Gradient descent is what actually does the learning; backprop just tells it which way is downhill, for every parameter at once.

the loss for one sample — a single number that says how wrong we were L = −[ t·log ŷ + (1−t)·log(1−ŷ) ] what we want, for all nine parameters at once ∂L/∂w — the slope of the loss in the direction of each weight what we then do with it (this part is gradient descent, not backprop) w ← w − η · ∂L/∂w

You could get every one of those derivatives without any theory at all: nudge a weight up by ε, run the network, nudge it down by ε, run it again, take the difference. Two forward passes per parameter. For these nine that is 18 passes — annoying. For a network with 7 billion parameters it is 14 billion passes per training example, which is why nobody did deep learning that way. Backprop computes all of them in roughly the cost of one extra pass, by noticing that the same intermediate quantities keep reappearing. Everything below is checked against the slow way, so you can see it is the same answer.

Real training averages the gradient over many samples precisely so that no single interval gets to move the weights this far.

The four things worth taking away

1 · The output error term is embarrassingly simple. Work through the algebra of ∂L/∂z⁽²⁾ for cross-entropy loss composed with a sigmoid output and you get exactly ŷ − t. The σ′ factor from the activation and the 1/ŷ factor from the logarithm cancel each other perfectly. That cancellation is not luck; it is why cross-entropy is the loss we pair with a sigmoid. Swap in squared error and the σ′ survives — and then a confidently wrong prediction, the case that most needs a big correction, generates almost no gradient at all.
2 · The backward pass reuses the forward pass's own wires. To send blame from the output back to hidden unit j, backprop multiplies by vj — the very same weight the forward pass used to send information the other way. That is the whole trick, and it is why the cost of the backward pass is about the cost of the forward pass, no matter how many parameters there are.
3 · Select "saturated hidden units" in the menu. Nothing is broken: the forward pass runs, ŷ = 0.931, the loss is small and sensible. But the hidden units sit at σ′(z) = 6.4 × 10−5, so every gradient reaching the first layer is multiplied by essentially zero. The largest hidden-layer gradient is 0.14% of the largest output-layer gradient: the output layer will keep learning and the first layer is frozen. This is the vanishing gradient, visible in a network with one hidden layer. Stack forty of these and the multiplication happens forty times — which is the story the activation-functions companion tells in full.
4 · Every gradient here is a product of local quantities. Look at ∂L/∂w11 = δ1 · x₁ — the blame arriving at that unit, times the input that flowed along that wire. Nothing in that expression knows about the rest of the network. That locality is what makes automatic differentiation possible, and it is why you will never implement backprop by hand again after this page: PyTorch's loss.backward() is doing exactly what you just stepped through, in the same order, for a few million more wires.

With the sensible settings, look at where the two curves separate. Held-out log-loss bottoms out at 0.318 around step 350 and then climbs steadily to 0.3475 by step 6,000 — while the training loss is still politely falling the whole time, 0.272 → 0.255. Nothing announces this. The training curve, on its own, says the run is going well right up to the end. Roughly 94% of that run was spent making the model worse at the only job it has. This is what "early stopping" means, and it is why a held-out set is not a formality you add before publication.
Press "every weight starts at zero." This is the single most instructive failure on the page. Every hidden unit gets an identical gradient, so every hidden unit takes an identical step, so they stay identical forever — after 6,000 steps the maximum difference between any two hidden units is exactly 0, not approximately zero. All the network can still learn is its output bias, which converges to σ(b) = 0.2075 — the base rate, to four decimals. You have built a 25-parameter neural network that is an expensive way to compute the fraction of intervals containing a breakdown. Random initialization is not a heuristic; it is what breaks the symmetry.
The learning-rate presets, measured. η = 0.02: after 6,000 steps the training loss has only reached 0.338 and balanced accuracy 0.675 — still crawling toward a solution it would eventually find, just far too slowly to be useful. η = 2: 0.255. η = 8: 0.659, with balanced accuracy back at 0.50 — the steps overshoot the valley and the network gives up and predicts the base rate. And the edge between those is not a gentle slope: η = 5 gives 0.261, η = 6 gives 0.284, η = 7 gives 0.332, and η = 8 collapses. Two learning rates 15% apart, two completely different runs. There is no formula for the right value. There is a sweep, and a held-out set to judge it with.
Push η past about 15 and something worth knowing happens. Balanced accuracy stays pinned at 0.50, but the number stops being reproducible: at η = 20 this page reports a training log-loss somewhere between about 5 and 20, and which one you get depends on your browser. The arithmetic is deterministic; the trajectory is not. Once the steps are large enough to bounce out of the valley, a difference in the last bit of a floating-point tanh grows, over 6,000 steps, into an entirely different run. If you have ever had a training run that would not reproduce across two machines and blamed the framework, this is usually the reason — and it is a signal that the learning rate is outside the regime where any of your results mean anything.
Before you believe any result on this page — the three questions.
What is the baseline? Always answering "no breakdown" is 79.25% accurate here. Any model must beat that, and on this problem accuracy alone cannot show it; balanced accuracy and log-loss can.
How was the data split, and is that honest? Two independently drawn sets of 400 intervals, and the held-out set never touches a gradient. Split real detector data by time, not at random — 5-minute intervals from the same afternoon peak are not independent samples, and a random split will quietly leak the answer across it.
What does it do on the ugly cases? Breakdown is genuinely stochastic — the same detector state sometimes breaks down and sometimes does not — so there is an irreducible floor, and here we can compute it, because we built the process. A rule that knows the true probability exactly and calls it at 0.5 scores held-out log-loss 0.308 and accuracy 0.855. This network's best is 0.318: within 0.01 of the process that generated the data. There is almost nothing left to win, and a model reporting much better than that has been handed the answer somehow. One caution on the balanced-accuracy column: it depends on where you put the threshold, so a model can score above the 0.783 the true rule gets at 0.5 just by being differently calibrated — always report the threshold with the number. Then go and look at the intervals nearest the band edges one at a time, before you report any mean at all.

The same machinery, pointed at a different question

Everything so far predicted a probability. Most transportation problems do not: you want an acceleration, a travel time, a queue length — a number. Here is a car-following problem. The follower is travelling at 20 m/s; given the gap to the leader and the closing speed Δv, predict the acceleration. The data come from an IDM-style rule with 0.25 m/s² of sensor and driver noise on top. Exactly two things change from tab 5, and backpropagation does not change at all.

The three baselines are the point of this tab. Predicting the average acceleration for every situation, scored on held-out data: RMSE 2.33 m/s². Fitting a plane — linear regression on gap and closing speed: 1.30. A six-unit network, 25 parameters: 0.29. The measurement noise we deliberately added is 0.250, so the network sits about 16% above the best score anyone or anything could achieve on this data; against the noise-free rule it is off by roughly 0.15 m/s². Report the third number in isolation and it means nothing. Report all four and a reader can see two things at once: the nonlinearity was necessary, and there is almost nothing left to win.
Why the plane fails is physical, not statistical. Acceleration is roughly flat and mildly positive across most of the gap range and then falls off a cliff as the gap closes with a positive Δv — the IDM desired-gap term enters as (s*/s)², so the response is strongly curved and the two inputs interact. A plane has to average that cliff against the flat region and gets both wrong. This is what a hidden layer buys you: the freedom to be flat in one region and steep in another.
And the same warning as everywhere else. At η = 0.3 the held-out RMSE is 0.294; nudge it to η = 0.5 and it is 0.387; at η = 0.8 the run goes to infinity within a few thousand steps. That ceiling is nearly seven times lower than the η = 2 that worked well on tab 5, because the targets here span roughly −6 to +1 m/s² and squared error on large targets produces large gradients. Nothing about the network changed; only the scale of the numbers you fed it did. Standardizing your targets is not housekeeping, it is what makes a learning rate transferable between two problems.
