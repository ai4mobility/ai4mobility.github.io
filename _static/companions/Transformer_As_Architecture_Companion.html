AI for Mobility · Module 2 · Transformers

Your own tokens

The last two pages used a transformer the way it was built — on language. This one takes the language out. The encoder stack does not know what a word is; it knows how to let a set of vectors exchange information. Decide what your vectors are, and the same machine works on detector stations, sensor streams, or substations. Everything measured here runs on METR-LA: 207 real loop detectors on Los Angeles freeways, 5-minute speeds, 1 March – 27 June 2012.

Only the bottom and the top of BERT are about language

A transformer encoder is a stack of blocks, and a block does two things: let every token look at every other token and take a weighted average (attention), then push each token through the same small network on its own (the feed-forward layer). Neither step contains anything linguistic. The language lives in two removable pieces — the wordpiece tokenizer and embedding table that turn text into vectors at the bottom, and the fill-in-the-blank head at the top.

The one sentence to carry out of this page. A transformer is a learned, input-dependent adjacency matrix over whatever you decide to call a token. Everything else — the tokenizer, the position signal, the head — is you telling it what your problem is.

The same three questions, every time

Applying a transformer to a new problem is not an architecture decision. It is three modelling decisions, and only you can make them.

What is a token?

The unit that gets its own vector and its own row in the attention matrix. Too fine and the sequence explodes; too coarse and there is nothing left to attend over.

What may attend to what?

All-to-all is the default and is rarely what you want. A mask is how you put a causality constraint, a geographic cut-off, or a propagation delay into the model.

What does “position” mean here?

Attention alone cannot tell token 1 from token 5. In language, position is an index. In your problem it is a mile marker, a network distance, a time of day — see tab 4.

You already build adjacency matrices. This one is learned.

When you write a network model — a cell transmission model, a graph neural network, a spatial econometric model — you declare who talks to whom, and the declaration is fixed for the whole run. Attention computes it instead, from the content of the tokens, fresh for every input. That is the whole difference, and both the gain and the risk follow from it.

Aij = softmaxj( qi · kj / √d ) outi = Σj Aij vj
Aijhow much station i listens to station j, this input. Rows sum to 1 — it is a row-normalised, non-negative, asymmetric adjacency matrix.
qi, kjtwo learned projections of the token vectors. The dot product is the similarity that decides the weight.
√dkeeps the dot products from growing with the vector length and saturating the softmax.
vjwhat station j actually contributes once it has been listened to.

In words: every token scores every other token, the scores are turned into weights that sum to one, and each token replaces itself with that weighted average of the others. Set A to your road network's adjacency matrix and you have message passing on a graph. Let the model compute A from the data and you have attention.

48.4% — and the measured neighbourhood is not even stable. Between the morning peak and the evening peak, fewer than half of a detector's top-five partners stay the same. No single fixed adjacency matrix can be right at both 8am and 5pm. That is the argument for computing it from the input.
Going deeper — why an asymmetric, row-normalised matrix is the right object

Traffic influence is directional: a queue at a downstream bottleneck propagates upstream, while free-flow perturbations travel downstream, and the two travel at different speeds. A symmetric adjacency (which is what you get from a distance kernel, and what a plain graph convolution uses) cannot represent that; Aij ≠ Aji can. Row-normalisation also means attention can only redistribute attention, never manufacture more of it: if station i starts listening harder to an upstream queue it must listen less to something else. That is a real inductive bias, and occasionally the wrong one — which is why architectures that need a token to attend to nothing add a null token for the attention to dump weight on.
Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need, NeurIPS 2017; Li et al., DCRNN, ICLR 2018.

Three research problems, three definitions of a token

The architecture is the same in all three. What changes is what you cut the world into, what you let attend to what, and — the part that decides whether any of it works — what you are competing against. Pick one.

A token is one detector at one five-minute bin

What is one token
One detector station at one 5-minute bin. Its vector is the speed (or flow, or occupancy) at that cell, projected up to d, plus the position signals below. STAEformer uses d = 152.
What is the sequence
A (207 × 12) grid: one hour of history across the whole network, predicting the next hour. Both axes are attended over, usually separately.
What attends to what
Along time, unrestricted. Along space, either unrestricted (STAEformer, GMAN) or masked — PDFormer uses two masks, one for detectors within a road-network distance threshold and one for detectors with similar traffic profiles matched by dynamic time warping.
Where position comes from
Time of day and day of week (both essential — see tab 4), plus a per-detector learned identity vector. GMAN gets its spatial position from node2vec on the road graph; T-Graphormer uses shortest-path distance on the road graph as a bias on the attention score.
Data you need
Months of network-wide loop, radar or probe data at 5-minute resolution, plus the road-network distance matrix. METR-LA is 207 detectors × 34,272 bins, 8.1% missing. FDOT's RITIS/ClearGuide feeds and any District's Bluetooth or radar archive are the Florida equivalent.
Classical baseline you must beat
A weekly historical average and last-value persistence, then ARIMA or a tuned LSTM, then a graph model. Tab 5 has the numbers, and they are not flattering to the architecture story.
Breaks when
The incident is the thing you care about — these models are trained and scored on ordinary days, and a weekly average already explains most of the variance. Also when you move the model to a corridor it was not fitted on: the per-detector identity vectors do not transfer, because they are literally indexed by detector.

STAEformer: Liu et al., CIKM '23, arXiv:2308.10425. GMAN: Zheng et al., AAAI 2020, arXiv:1911.08415. PDFormer: Jiang et al., AAAI 2023, arXiv:2301.07945. T-Graphormer: arXiv:2501.13274.

A token is one reading from one stream

What is one token
One reading from one stream at one instant: a radar return, a CAN frame, an image patch, a gaze sample. Each carries a learned embedding of which stream it came from alongside its value.
What is the sequence
All readings from all streams inside a window — typically 5–10 s of history. Unequal rates are fine; the sequence is a set with timestamps, not a matrix.
What attends to what
Cross-attention: the stream you are predicting from queries the others. This is the one place where the fusion is only indirectly supervised — nothing tells the model that the radar return and the image patch are the same vehicle. Lab 7 is built entirely around checking whether that binding actually emerges.
Where position comes from
Continuous time, not an index. Encode the timestamp directly (sinusoidal or learned on the real-valued offset) so that a 40 ms gap and a 400 ms gap are different, and add the stream identity.
Data you need
Instrumented-vehicle logs with time-synchronised streams. FollowNet assembles 80,908 car-following events from HighD, NGSIM, SPMD, Waymo and Lyft; TransFollower uses 112,597 events from the Shanghai Naturalistic Driving Study. A comma 3X on the course fleet logs camera, CAN and radar together.
Classical baseline you must beat
IDM, Gipps or Newell with properly calibrated parameters — and a Kalman filter for the fusion itself. Do not skip this. In the FollowNet benchmark, deep deterministic policy gradient loses to IDM on four of six datasets, and on HighD it is 54% worse.
Breaks when
The error metric hides the failure. In FollowNet the learned models win on spacing MSE and then drive into the lead vehicle: NN collides on up to 116 of every 1,000 events, LSTM on 110, while IDM, GHR and DDPG collide zero times on every dataset. A model can be more accurate on average and unusable.

The table that should change how you read a car-following paper

Chen et al., FollowNet: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Car-Following Behavior Modeling, Scientific Data 2023, Table II (arXiv:2306.05381). MSE of spacing, lower is better; collision rate per 1,000 events.

Read the two halves together. The learned models own the MSE column and the classical models own the collision column. Whichever half your paper reports is the half your reviewer will not believe. Report both.
An honest gap worth knowing about before you propose a thesis on it. The literature splits into two camps that do not cite each other. Transportation-venue car-following papers (FollowNet, TransFollower, PIT-IDM) use only kinematic streams — speed, spacing, relative speed — and do benchmark against IDM. Computer-vision and robotics papers (TransFuser, Wayformer, MTR, Scene Transformer) genuinely fuse camera, lidar and map, and never compare against IDM, Gipps, Newell or a Kalman filter. A model that fuses camera + radar + CAN + driver state for longitudinal behaviour and reports an IDM baseline appears not to exist yet. That is an opening, not a warning.

A token is one facility — and there is no sequence at all

What is one token
One facility or asset. Its vector holds attributes of mixed type — asset class, age, elevation, served population, criticality — each embedded and concatenated. Different facility types can share one sequence precisely because each token carries its own type embedding.
What is the sequence
The whole system, or a neighbourhood of it. There is no time axis unless you add one; the usual setup is one token set per event (one storm, one outage) and a graph-level or node-level prediction.
What attends to what
All-to-all is the point: you are trying to discover the couplings, not encode them. If you already know the dependency graph, use it as a mask or an attention bias and you have a graph attention network — the sensible starting point.
Where position comes from
There is no index to use, so structure must be supplied explicitly: shortest-path distance on the known network as a bias on the attention score (Graphormer's spatial encoding), node degree, or Laplacian eigenvectors of the network as node features. Geographic coordinates and elevation are position too.
Data you need
Event-level records with outcomes: outage durations, restoration times, failure sequences. The strongest real-data example found — Duan & Ji's graph attention model of hurricane outage duration — uses 501 counties across 8 states and four hurricanes (Florence, Irma, Laura, Michael), roughly 130 samples per event.
Classical baseline you must beat
Network-flow or input–output interdependency models, engineering-judgment dependency graphs, and graph centrality heuristics (electrical betweenness, PageRank). Also plain XGBoost on facility attributes with no graph at all — in Duan & Ji the graph model beats non-graph models by 9–15%, which is real but is the whole benefit of the graph.
Breaks when
The class you actually need is the rare one. Duan & Ji reach ~93% average accuracy but only 0.71 on long-duration outages — the category that drives mutual-aid staging and emergency-management decisions. Their own paper says the plain part out loud: the number of historical extreme hurricanes is limited, so they deliberately shrink the model.

Duan & Ji, Graph Attention Network for Predicting Duration of Large-Scale Power Outages Induced by Natural Disasters, IEEE PES 2026, arXiv:2511.10898 — real PowerOutage.com records, HURDAT2 wind swaths, Census and CDC social-vulnerability data. Graphormer spatial encoding: Ying et al., NeurIPS 2021, arXiv:2106.05234.

Do not let a review paper tell you this field is mature. A 2026 review of AI for critical-infrastructure restoration concludes that “evidence maturity remains uneven, with many AI applications still simulation-based, sector-specific, or weakly validated in real restoration settings,” and that the central bottleneck is interoperability and data quality. A systematic review of ML for interdependent-infrastructure resilience names the same cause: “the lack of data, the diversity of system components, and the varying levels of uncertainty.” If your contribution is a new attention variant, you are working on the part that is not the bottleneck. Gergov et al., Sustainability 18(11):5297, 2026; Alkhaleel, Int. J. Critical Infrastructure Protection 44, 2024.

Attention cannot tell where anything is. You have to tell it.

Self-attention treats its input as a set. Shuffle the tokens and the outputs come back in the same shuffled order, unchanged — it is permutation-equivariant, and that is a theorem, not a quirk. For language the fix is a position index. For your problem “position” is a modelling decision, and it is the decision where your domain knowledge actually enters the model.

How much does position matter? The cleanest ablation in the literature

Dosovitskiy et al., An Image is Worth 16×16 Words, ICLR 2021, Appendix D.4 (arXiv:2010.11929). ViT-B/16, ImageNet 5-shot linear probe.

Read the two gaps. Removing position costs 2.8 points. Choosing between 1-D, 2-D and relative position costs 0.2. For images on a uniform pixel grid, that you encode position matters enormously and how barely matters at all — which is why ViT just uses a 1-D index. On a road network, where the metric is not uniform and not Euclidean, there is no reason to expect the second gap to stay small. That is the open question in your problem.

Going deeper — the two ways position gets in, and why the second is better for networks

Additive: add a position vector to the token vector before the first block, h₀ = x + p. Cheap, and what language models and ViT do. It puts position into the content, so the model has to re-derive relative position from a difference of absolutes.

As an attention bias: leave the tokens alone and add a learned scalar to the score, indexed by a relation between the pair:
Aij = (hiWQ)(hjWK)ᵀ / √d + bφ(i,j) where φ(i,j) is shortest-path distance on your network. This is Graphormer's spatial encoding, and it is the better shape for infrastructure and road networks: relative structure is expressed directly, unseen node counts are handled, and setting b to −∞ beyond a threshold recovers a masked, local model — so the fixed adjacency you would have declared is a special case of the thing you are learning.
Ying et al., NeurIPS 2021; Dwivedi & Bresson, DLG-AAAI 2021; Rampášek et al., NeurIPS 2022 — “PE gives a notion of distance, while SE gives a notion of structural similarity.”

The permutation-equivariance result is Property 1 of Lee et al., Set Transformer, ICML 2019 — proved for exactly this object, a transformer encoder block with the positional encoding removed.

A one-layer linear model beat every transformer they tested

Zeng et al. put a single linear layer (plus a moving-average trend split) against the transformer forecasters of the day on nine datasets. Their finding, verbatim: LTSF-Linear “surprisingly outperforms existing sophisticated Transformer-based LTSF models in all cases, and often by a large margin” — by 20–50% over the then-best on multivariate forecasting. On the Traffic dataset (862 Bay Area sensors, hourly), at a 96-step horizon: DLinear MSE 0.410 against FEDformer 0.587, Autoformer 0.613 and Informer 0.719.

The mechanism they blame is the one from tab 4: “the nature of the permutation-invariant self-attention mechanism inevitably results in temporal information loss.”

And the honest counterweight, from the same paper: naive repetition is not universally good either. It loses badly on Traffic and Electricity, and wins by about 45% on Exchange-Rate. Baselines are problem-specific. Run yours.

Zeng, Chen, Zhang, Xu, AAAI 2023, arXiv:2205.13504.

Measured here: this benchmark is mostly a temporal problem

A detector's own speed an hour ago correlates 0.53 with its speed now. Its road-network neighbours correlate 0.44, and a random other detector 0.20. The temporal signal dominates the spatial signal at every lag — which is why an MLP with identity embeddings is competitive with graph models here, and why a 2026 audit of these benchmarks concludes that they show “much weaker spatial than temporal correlation” and that spatially-unaware linear models are “a stronger competitor than previously reported.”

Correlations computed on the METR-LA training period, zeros masked. Audit: Martin et al., A Critical Audit of Spatiotemporal Forecasting Benchmark Datasets and Baselines, arXiv:2608.20980, 2026.

The evaluation close, for this method

The baseline
Weekly historical average and last-value persistence, both computed on your own data before you fit anything. Then a non-attention learned model — an MLP with identity and time-of-day embeddings is a genuinely hard baseline, not a straw man. Report all of them.
The split, and why it is honest
Chronological, never random: a random split lets 08:05 on a Tuesday train the model that predicts 08:00 on the same Tuesday. Build the input–output windows after partitioning, not before — windowing first leaks the boundary across the split. Hold out whole days, and if you claim transfer, hold out whole corridors.
The ugly cases
Score incidents, holidays, rain and sensor dropouts separately. An aggregate MAE on METR-LA is dominated by ordinary free-flow bins where a weekly average is already almost right; the 20% headline improvement can hide no improvement at all on the hours you built the system for. And for anything that controls a vehicle, report a safety metric next to the error metric — see the FollowNet collision column in tab 3.

When to reach for it, and when not to

Fit card — Transformer over non-language tokens
Idea in one sentence
Cut your problem into tokens, and let the model learn a fresh, input-dependent, directional adjacency matrix over them instead of declaring one.
Why it works (the logic)
Attention is a weighted average whose weights are computed from the tokens themselves, so the coupling structure can differ at 8am and 5pm, and can differ per incident — which a fixed adjacency, by construction, cannot.
Fits these tasks
Network-wide short-horizon forecasting; fusing asynchronous heterogeneous sensor streams; relational reasoning over an unordered set of assets. prediction classification extraction
Needs this data
Months of network-wide series with a distance matrix (traffic: RITIS, PeMS, a District's radar archive), or time-synchronised multi-stream vehicle logs (comma 3X, HighD, Waymo), or event-level records with outcomes (outage and restoration logs). Labels come free for forecasting, and are the binding constraint everywhere else. Volume is the gate: an N×N learned adjacency needs far more events than most infrastructure datasets contain.
Breaks when
Events are rare relative to N²; the tokens carry no identity, so the model cannot tell two detectors apart (97.4% of one-hour windows do not identify their detector); the benchmark is dominated by ordinary conditions, so a weekly average already scores well; or the error metric you optimise is not the behaviour you need — lower spacing MSE, more collisions.
Keep the classical method when
The coupling structure is already known and physically constrained — a corridor's conservation law, a known dependency graph — because then you are spending data to learn something you could have written down. Keep IDM, Gipps or Newell when you need a guaranteed collision-free, interpretable, three-parameter model you can calibrate on one afternoon of data and defend to an agency. Keep the weekly historical average when the horizon is long: at 60 minutes it is within 0.85 mph of the 2023 state of the art, and it costs nothing.

Check yourself

Four judgment questions. None of them has a derivation for an answer.

  1. You have three years of 5-minute speeds from 40 radar detectors on a Tampa corridor, and your advisor wants a transformer for 30-minute prediction. What two numbers do you compute before writing any model code, and what result would tell you to stop and do something else instead?
  2. You are fusing a comma 3X's camera, radar and CAN streams to predict the following vehicle's acceleration. The three streams arrive at 20, 20 and 100 Hz and the radar drops out in heavy rain. Give your token definition, say what carries “position”, and name the one baseline a reviewer will ask for that is not another neural network.
  3. A county has 140 lift stations, 22 substations and 310 signalised intersections, plus outage records from three hurricanes. Roughly how many labelled events is that, and how does that number compare to the free parameters in a learned all-to-all adjacency over those facilities? What would you build instead, and what would you have to observe before the attention version became defensible?
  4. A paper reports a 4% MAE improvement over Graph WaveNet on METR-LA and calls it a new state of the art. Using the table in tab 5, say what is wrong with the framing — and name the two additional rows you would want to see in that table before you believed the contribution mattered.
