The anatomy of a deep network

Eight real networks, opened up — not eight diagrams copied from eight papers, but eight architectures built in PyTorch and measured, so every parameter count and every tensor shape here came out of the code rather than out of a caption. Read them in order and one fact keeps repeating: the exit changes every time, and the body never does. The body is between 92% and 100% of every network on this page. Which means the question that decides whether any of this is useful to you is not which head to bolt on — it is where a good body comes from. That question has a name, and the last tab is the door to it.

How to read the picture. Height is how much of the input's layout survives at that point — the size of the feature map, or the length of the token sequence — on a compressed scale so that a 256×256 map and a single vector both fit on one screen. Width is channels: how many numbers describe each position. This is the same encoding used in the Which network, and what it costs companion, so the two pages can be read together.

Height falling off a cliff means the layout was thrown away and a head follows. Height coming back up means a decoder is rebuilding it. Height flat and low with the width pinching means a fully connected bottleneck. Parameter counts are in the row underneath and on every block you click — they are deliberately not in the drawing, because size and shape are different questions.

What actually decides the shape

Not the model family. Not the year. Sort the eight by how big their output is and the design pattern falls out in order:

NetworkWhat comes out Numbers outSo the exit is…
The rule. If what has to come out is a handful of numbers, you need a head — one or two layers, and the representation gets collapsed on the way in. If what has to come out is as big and as structured as what went in, you need a decoder — and it will cost about as much as the encoder did. If what has to come out is the representation, you need neither. The body is the same in all three cases — which is the whole reason the next two tabs exist. If the body is what every one of these has in common, and it is nearly all of the parameters, then it is the thing worth understanding.

Three names for the same block, and when each is right

WordMeansSafe to say when…
Backbonethe stack that turns raw input into a representation always — it names a role, so it never collides
Encoderthe same stack, when something decodes afterwards there is an actual decoder (U-Net, VAE, translation). Otherwise it invites the reader to look for a decoder that isn't there
Feature extractorthe same stack, frozen you are not training it
The word that will trip you. “Encoder” also names one specific half of a transformer — and GPT is called decoder-only even though nothing in it decodes anything back to the input space. Its exit is a single linear layer: a head, run in a loop. When the name and the shape disagree, trust the shape.
Going deeper — why the decoder costs what it costs

A head maps d → k, so it holds d·k + k weights, and k is small — 2 damage classes, 1000 ImageNet classes. A decoder has to rebuild a signal of the same order as the input, so it needs roughly the encoder's capacity run backwards. In the U-Net measured here that is against an encoder-plus-bottleneck of — the same order, as expected.

The exception on this page is GPT-2, whose exit maps 768 → 50,257 and so should cost 38,597,376 weights. It costs zero, because those weights are the token embedding table read backwards — the same matrix used twice. Weight tying is worth knowing by name: it is why GPT-2 small is 124,439,808 parameters and not 163,037,184.

Swap the task

You have a trained network. A different question arrives. What survives? Pick a starting point and a new job — every number in the answer is computed from the measured architectures, not estimated.

Two rules doing all the work here

  1. The body is indexed by the input. Change the modality and nothing transfers — not one layer. A network that has only ever seen photographs has no opinion about a crash narrative.
  2. The exit is indexed by the output. Change what has to come out and you rebuild the exit — cheap if the new output is small, expensive if it is a map.

Where the body comes from

Every tab so far has been about shape. This one is about training — and it is the door into the rest of today. All eight bodies were learned. None of them was learned on the job you actually want done. So what taught them?

Sort those bodies by size and something falls out

Look at what the label-free three were asked to do. Nobody sat down and annotated anything for them. They manufactured their own supervision out of the structure of the data — and that, not the architecture, is why they are the biggest bodies here.

Trick 1 — hide part of the data and predict it

The answer key was in the data the whole time; you just cover it up. BERT blanks out 15% of the tokens and predicts them from both sides. GPT-2 hides everything after the current word. The VAE squeezes the input through a two-number waist and asks for it back.

The transportation version. Blank out one detector station and predict it from its neighbours. Hide the next fifteen minutes and predict them from the last hour. Mask a segment of a trajectory and reconstruct it. Your detector archive is its own answer key — and unlike crash labels, you already have ten years of it.

Trick 2 — use pairs that already occur together

If two things reliably show up together, that co-occurrence is a free training signal. CLIP learns that an image and its own caption belong together and every other caption does not. Nobody wrote those captions for CLIP; they were already on the page.

The transportation version. A road link and the links that follow it in a trajectory. A signal phase and the phase that follows it. A ramp and the mainline segment that backs up when it does. Nobody labelled those either — the trajectories already exist, and co-occurrence is sitting in them.

That is representation learning

Learn a body on a task you do not care about, so you can use it on the task you do. The throwaway task is called a pretext task, and the two tricks above are how you build one when nobody will pay for labels.

Which is exactly where the rest of today goes:

  • Word2vec — trick 1, applied to words: hide a word, predict it from its neighbours. Then we run the same algorithm on a road network instead of a corpus, and see what the geometry knows.
  • Contrastive learning — trick 2, stated in general: pull things that belong together closer, push everything else apart.
  • CLIP — trick 2 across two modalities at once, which is how the picture space and the sentence space become one space.
Why this matters more in transportation than in computer vision. The reason CV could afford ImageNet is that a person can label a photo in seconds. Nobody can label a crash in seconds, and there are only ever forty of them at your intersection. Label-free objectives are not an elegance here — they are frequently the only way a model gets built at all.
