JEPA world models: predict the description, not the picture

A world model that predicts pixels has to draw glare, raindrops and moving leaves that nobody can predict. A JEPA (Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture) predicts only a compact description of what comes next. On this page you hide parts of a traffic scene the way I-JEPA and V-JEPA do, train a small JEPA on a car-following sensor in your browser, watch it collapse when it is left unprotected, and then plan with it the way V-JEPA 2 plans robot actions.

Companion to the handout JEPA and V-JEPA 2: World Models That Predict in Latent Space, further reading for 4.12 World models. Paper numbers come from the papers. Tabs 2 and 3 run a smaller, live version of the handout's synthetic worked example, so your numbers will differ slightly from the handout's.

Predict a description, not a picture

All three families below learn from data nobody labeled. They differ only in what the loss compares. A JEPA compares a predicted embedding of the hidden part with its actual embedding, so it never has to draw pixels.

Three ways to learn from unlabeled data · read each panel bottom-up: inputs, encoders, then what the loss compares

Takeaway: the JEPA panel is the generative panel with the decoder replaced by a predictor that works on embeddings. Δ_y tells the predictor where (or when) the hidden part is. The target encoder is a slow copy of the context encoder and gets no gradient.

The best possible predictor knows the gap and closing speed exactly, yet it still misses everything the glare does, because the glare is redrawn every frame. A pixel loss charges a model for that miss forever. A JEPA's target encoder is free to leave the glare out of the embedding, so the target becomes predictable.

Hide and predict: how I-JEPA and V-JEPA choose what to hide

A JEPA learns by predicting the embeddings of hidden patches. What you hide decides what it learns: hide a few specks and the neighbors give them away; hide a whole vehicle and the model must know what a vehicle is and where it is going.

Train a JEPA on a car-following sensor, live

Each frame is 64 numbers from a toy forward sensor. Two slow causes are mixed into it, the gap to the lead vehicle and the closing speed, together with six fast nuisance causes that are redrawn every half second. Every encoder below turns 64 numbers into 2. Which 2 does each one keep?

The key equation

minθ, φ  ‖ Pφ( Eθ(xt) ) − sg( Eθ̄(xt+1) ) ‖1
In words: change the encoder and the predictor so that the predicted embedding of the next frame matches the actual embedding of the next frame. sg means the target is held fixed during each update.
Eθ
the encoder: 64 sensor values in, 2 latents out (here a single linear layer)
Eθ̄
the target encoder; in the EMA version a slow copy, θ̄ ← 0.99 θ̄ + 0.01 θ after every step
Pφ
the predictor: 2 latents now → 2 latents 0.5 s later
sg(·)
stop-gradient: no learning signal flows into the target
Going deeper (optional): why "no protection" collapses, and what the other two do

If E outputs the same vector for every frame and P passes it through, the loss is exactly zero. Gradient descent finds that solution because shrinking the encoder's weights shrinks both sides of the difference. Stop-gradient plus a slow target removes the pull on the target side, so the predictor keeps chasing a target that has not collapsed yet; this is the recipe of I-JEPA, V-JEPA and V-JEPA 2. The spread penalty (the VICReg and LeJEPA family) instead charges the encoder whenever a latent's spread across a batch falls below 1 or the two latents become correlated.

Why prediction keeps the gap: the nuisance causes are redrawn every frame, so nothing in frame t tells you the glare in frame t+1. The only part of xt+1 that xt can predict is the part driven by the gap and closing speed, which is why the pixel predictor works too. A related result: Littwin et al. (2024) showed for deep linear networks that, for the same target, predicting in latent space favors the most predictive features while predicting pixels favors the highest-variance ones.

Can you trust this result?

Baseline
The autoencoder (same size, rebuilds the frame) and the best straight line through all 64 values, which uses the labels directly.
Split
Encoders train on 100 synthetic drives; probes are fitted and scored on 30 different drives, half each. Labels are used only to grade.
Ugly cases
Nuisance stronger than the signal (the slider). What the toy leaves out: nuisance that changes slowly (a JEPA would keep it, since it is predictable), occlusion, futures that branch. The model is linear and small, so it cannot show why V-JEPA 2 needed a million hours of video.

Plan with it: reach a goal frame, as V-JEPA 2-AC does

Stage 2: freeze the encoder and learn, from a small log with the driver's accelerations, how an acceleration changes the 2 latents. Stage 3: every half second, encode the current frame and one goal frame recorded at the target gap, try 200 acceleration plans 4 s long, keep the one whose imagined latent lands closest to the goal's, apply its first step, and plan again.

The first drive trains the encoders and the stage-2 predictors (a few seconds).

Can you trust this result?

Baseline
A two-gain gap controller, a = 0.2 (g − goal) + 0.7 Δv, given the true gap and closing speed, as a radar would. It sees what the latent planners must infer.
Split
Stage 1 trains on 100 drives with no actions, stage 2 on 20 other drives with actions, and the test drives are new. The goal frame carries its own glare.
Ugly cases
Most test drives include a hard brake by the lead; watch the closest approach. The planner re-plans from one noisy frame every 0.5 s and scores only the end of each plan, so its accelerations are jerky. In the handout's 40-drive run the JEPA planner and the classical controller had RMS gap errors of 2.7 m and 3.0 m, and the classical one had a fifth of the jerk.

V-JEPA 2 at a glance

V-JEPA 2 (Assran et al., June 2025) learns from over a million hours of video with no actions, then learns what actions do from under 62 hours of robot video, then plans by matching an imagined embedding to a goal image. Tab 3 is the same three stages at toy scale.

Takeaway: the expensive stage needs no actions, and video without actions is plentiful; the stage that needs actions only has to learn how an action moves an embedding the encoder already makes.

a*1, …, a*T = arg mina1,…,aT ‖ Pφ(a1:T; sk, pk) − E(xgoal) ‖1
In words: among the plans you try, keep the one whose imagined embedding after T steps is closest to the goal image's embedding. sk is the current frame's embedding and pk the arm's pose (the paper writes them zk and sk).

Limits the authors report

The model must infer the robot's action axes from one uncalibrated camera, and its inferred axes rotated as the camera moved, so camera placement was tuned by hand. Plans look one action ahead; pick-and-place needed three hand-given sub-goal images. Goals are pictures, while real tasks are usually stated in words. For a car, add two more: 16 s per decision versus about 0.1 s, and no safety constraint inside the energy.

Fit card and check yourself

Fit card — JEPA (joint-embedding predictive architecture)

Idea in one sentence
Learn an encoder by predicting the embedding of a hidden or future part of the data from the visible part, never its pixels.
Why it works (the logic)
Unpredictable detail cannot help the prediction, so the encoder keeps what persists and predicts (objects, motion, gaps). Stop-gradient with a slow target encoder, or a spread penalty, rules out the constant-output solution.
Fits these tasks
Pretraining encoders for camera, LiDAR and trajectory data with few labels; anticipating maneuvers; planning toward goals in a learned space. Leverage labels: prediction, classification, extraction.
Needs this data
Raw video, LiDAR sweeps, trajectories or sensor series · large and unlabeled, plus a small action-labeled set for planning · no labels to pretrain, a small labeled set to probe · vehicle fleets, DOT camera networks, naturalistic driving studies, dashcam vendors.
Breaks when
The detail that matters is sudden and unpredictable; predictable but useless content dominates; collapse goes unnoticed; goals are not pictures; decisions need safety constraints and sub-second latency; the camera differs from training.
Keep the classical method when
The state is measured and a physical model exists (a radar gap with a car-following controller, detector counts with the CTM): cheaper, smoother, explainable.
