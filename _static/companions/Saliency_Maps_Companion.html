Saliency maps: what is the network actually looking at?

A convolutional network gives you a number and no reasons. A saliency map is the closest thing it has to a coefficient table — it tells you where in the frame the answer came from. This page builds one on a real Florida arterial, then uses one to catch a lane-offset model that was right for the wrong reason. Every number on the page comes out of the two reproducer scripts named in the footer.

The question a CNN will not answer for you

You are evaluating a lane-keeping system for a state DOT. Over 400 miles of I-275 it holds the lane. At one overpass the estimated lane offset jumps 0.4 m and the car nudges left. The log gives you the number it produced. It does not give you one word about where in the frame that number came from.

The bridge: this is a coefficient table you cannot read

When you fit a crash frequency model you read the coefficients. AADT positive, shoulder width negative, lighting not significant — and that table is most of what makes the model defensible in a report. It is how you argue the model learned the road and not the sample.

A ResNet-50 has 25.6 million weights and not one of them is “the lane line”. There is no coefficient table. What there is is a derivative: the score the network produced is a differentiable function of every pixel and of every intermediate feature, so you can ask which parts of this frame, if they changed, would change the answer. Rendered as a heat map over the frame, that derivative is a saliency map.

What it is and is not. A saliency map answers where. It does not answer how much, it does not answer why, and — as tab 6 shows — it is not evidence until you have checked that it depends on the model at all.

The picture: one forward pass, one backward pass

Grad-CAM in one loop. The forward pass answers the question; a single backward pass from that one score, and nothing else, turns the last convolutional layer into a heat grid.

Three things to notice before you move on

  • The map is class-discriminative, and the numbers say so. Ask for “traffic light” and the signal heads carry —× the heat their pixel area would earn them by chance. Ask for “sports car” on the same frame and that drops to —×, while the lead car climbs from —× to —×. Same pixels, same weights — only the number we differentiated changed.
  • “Sports car” also lights up the red SUV on the left. The map is showing you what the network treats as car-like, not what you meant by “the lead vehicle”. That is information, and it is the kind of thing you only see because you looked.
  • The probabilities are tiny — — for “traffic light”, — for “sports car”. A cluttered arterial is not an ImageNet photo with one object in the middle, and 1000 classes split the mass. Saliency does not need a confident prediction; it needs a score to differentiate. Do not read the heat as confidence.
Switch the rendering to “true cell size”. The smooth blob becomes 273 squares. Everything between those squares was drawn by an interpolation filter, not by the network. If you are about to claim a saliency map proves a detector was looking at a specific sign, a specific pedestrian, or a specific lane line, this control is the reason you cannot.

The arithmetic, once

Two lines. You already did the hard part in the backpropagation block — this is one backward pass, stopped early, with an average taken over space.

Tier 1 — the equation everyone needs

α_k = (1/Z) · Σ_(i,j) ∂ s_c / ∂ A_k(i,j) L_c(i,j) = ReLU( Σ_k α_k · A_k(i,j) )
SymbolWhat it is here
s_cthe score for one class — “traffic light”. One number out of 1000.
A_k(i,j)feature map k of the last convolutional layer, at grid cell (i,j). For this page: k = 1…2048, and the grid is 13 × 21.
Zthe number of cells, 13 × 21 = 273. The average is over space only.
α_kone weight per channel: how strongly feature k, anywhere in the frame, pushes this class score up.
L_c(i,j)the heat at cell (i,j) — a 13 × 21 grid, stretched over the frame for display.

In words: score each of the 2048 learned features by how much it moves this one class, then at every location add up the features that are actually present there, weighted by those scores, and keep only the positive part.

Going deeper — why the spatial average, and why the ReLU (optional)

Take a first-order expansion of the score in the activations: s_c ≈ const + Σ_k Σ_(i,j) [∂s_c/∂A_k(i,j)] · A_k(i,j). Grad-CAM makes one simplification — it replaces the per-cell gradient with its spatial average α_k. That is exactly right when the network global-average-pools before the classifier, which ResNet does, and approximate otherwise. What you gain is a single weight per channel, so the map inherits the channels’ meaning instead of the gradient’s noise.

The ReLU keeps only locations whose features increase this class score. Drop it and the map also lights up evidence against the class, which is a different and usually less useful question. For a regression head — the lane-offset model on the next tab — there is no “class”, so s_c becomes the predicted value and taking |·| instead of ReLU is the honest choice: pushing the offset left matters as much as pushing it right. That is what the reproducer does.

Those last two are scale readings, not measurements — they come from the detector’s own boxes and two assumed real widths: a 1.80 m car and a 0.30 m (12-inch) signal head. The lead car is — cells across, so near the bumper a cell is about the width of a wing mirror. A signal head is — of a cell — one signal head is a fifth of the smallest thing this map can point at, and two adjacent heads sit only — cells apart.

The rule. A Grad-CAM cell is as big as the stride of the layer you took it from. Deep layers are class-aware and spatially blunt; shallow layers are spatially sharp and class-blind. There is no setting that gives you both, and any lane-line-level localisation claim from a deep-layer map is a claim about interpolation.

Lane lines: the thing you actually want it to look at

A stock ImageNet network has no “lane line” class, so to ask the lane question we have to train something. Below is a small CNN that reads one forward-facing frame and predicts the car’s lateral offset from the lane centre, in metres — the core quantity inside any lane-keeping controller.

These road frames are synthetic SYNTHETIC and that is the point. They are drawn from a pinhole camera model, not photographed. Synthetic buys two things no real corpus gives you: we know the exact pixels of every painted marking, so “how much of the heat is on the paint” becomes a number instead of an impression; and we can build one specific, controlled trap and watch whether saliency finds it. The trap is real — the geometry below is a documented lane-keeping failure mode — but the evidence on this tab is evidence about a controlled experiment, not about any shipped product.

Two corridors, one label

Sound-wall corridor

A continuous wall on the left throws a shadow whose edge sits at a fixed — m from the lane centre — every frame, all corridor long. The shadow edge is therefore a perfect proxy for the lane: exactly as informative as the paint, and much bigger and higher-contrast.

Mixed corridor

Sun angle, cloud, overpasses, no wall. The shadow edge lands anywhere between −5 and +1.5 m, and one frame in five has no shadow at all. Here the shadow carries no lane information whatsoever.

Two networks, identical architecture, identical initial weights, identical optimiser and schedule — — parameters each. The only difference is which corridor supplied the 6,000 training frames. Model A learns on the mixed corridor. Model B learns on the sound-wall corridor. Both are then validated the way most people validate: a random held-out split of their own data.

What the map caught, and what proved it

Saliency raised the alarm from a single frame. It did not prove anything. Here is what proved it — and the order matters, because on a real project the alarm is cheap and the proof is expensive.

Evaluate the result

What is the baseline? Predicting the training mean gives — m RMSE; a non-learned fit to the two painted lines gives — m. Both models beat both baselines on their own data, which is why neither looked suspicious.

How was the data split, and why is that honest? It was not honest — that is the finding. A random held-out split is only a test of generalisation to the same corridor. The shortcut is a property of the whole dataset, so every split of it inherits the shortcut. The split that has any power here is by corridor: train where the wall is, test where it is not.

What does it do on the ugly cases? Model B loses a factor of 13 and returns — m — errors of half a metre in a 3.6 m lane, correlated with the scene rather than random, which is the failure mode a controller can least tolerate. Model A is unchanged at — m.

Before you believe it

Everything above rests on an assumption nobody states out loud: that the picture depends on the trained model. There are published saliency methods for which it barely does. Run the check first.

The sanity check

Take Model A. Throw away the learned weights in the classifier head and the last convolutional block, replace them with fresh random ones, and recompute the map on the same frames. If the map is telling you about the model, it must fall apart. If it looks about the same, it was telling you about the image — edges, contrast, texture — and you could have got it from a Sobel filter without training anything.

A pass here licenses this page and nothing else. The check is per method, per architecture, per task. Adebayo and colleagues found that several widely used and visually convincing saliency methods are, in their words, independent of both the model and the data-generating process — they behave like edge detectors, which need neither training data nor a model. Run the randomisation test on your setup before you put a heat map in a report. Adebayo, Gilmer, Muelly, Goodfellow, Hardt, Kim, Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps, NeurIPS 2018, arXiv:1810.03292.

Where it breaks

  • Resolution. A deep-layer map cannot resolve a lane line, a signal head, or a small sign — on this page a whole signal head is a fifth of one cell. Any claim finer than the cell size is a claim about the interpolation filter.
  • It shows correlation with the score, not causation. The honest test of “did this region matter” is to occlude it and re-run the model. Saliency is the cheap screen; occlusion is the measurement.
  • One frame is an anecdote. Both lift numbers on tab 4 are averages over 200 frames for exactly this reason. A single striking map is how most bad interpretability claims start.
  • It cannot tell you what about a region mattered. Heat on the lead vehicle does not distinguish “its brake lights” from “its shape” from “the shadow under it”.
  • Different methods disagree on the same frame. Compare the two methods on tab 2. Reporting the one that best supports your story is a real and common failure.
  • Distribution shift makes maps prettier, not more true. A model can look right on the corridor you sampled and be a shadow detector — that is the whole of tab 5.
  • It is not a safety case. No regulator, and no serious acceptance test, treats a heat map as evidence that a perception system is safe. It is evidence for where to point the next experiment.

Fit card — saliency maps (gradient saliency and Grad-CAM)

Idea in one sentence
Differentiate one output — a class score, a predicted offset — back to the pixels or to the last convolutional layer, and draw the result over the frame to see where the answer came from.
Why it works (the logic)
The last convolutional layer still knows where; the classifier that follows it does not. Weighting those maps by how strongly each feature moves the output puts location back on the answer.
Fits these tasks
Debugging and accepting a vision model you did not train — spotting shortcuts, mislabelled classes, and background reliance; triaging a specific failure frame; deciding where to spend the next data collection. Leverage labels: judgment, with classification and prediction as the models it is applied to.
Needs this data
One trained differentiable model and one frame — no labels, no retraining, no ground truth. Runs on a laptop in under a second per frame. To turn it into a number rather than a picture you also need region masks: hand-drawn, detector boxes as on tab 2, or exact masks from synthetic data as on tab 4.
Breaks when
You need localisation finer than the layer’s stride; you read one frame as a model-level claim; the method fails the randomisation check; or you present it as proof of causation, or as safety evidence.
Keep the classical method when
You need to know why an estimator behaved, not a network — a Kalman filter, a Hough-based lane fit, or a regression already tells you its own coefficients and residuals, exactly and without interpretation. And when the question is “does this model generalise”, the answer is a shifted-split experiment, not a heat map.
