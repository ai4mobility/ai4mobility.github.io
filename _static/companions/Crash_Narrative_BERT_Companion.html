Does BERT earn its keep on crash narratives?

Eight things people claim a transformer encoder can do with crash reports — each one run against 399 real Florida long-form crash narratives from Pasco, Hernando and Pinellas counties, scored against the coded fields in the same records, and compared with the classical method it would replace. Every number on this page came out of that run.

The problem: the story is in the paragraph, and the paragraph is not a field

Florida's crash database has 157 coded columns per record — manner of collision, light condition, lane departure flag, a distraction flag. None of them is the officer's paragraph. That paragraph is where why lives: the driver looked into the back seat, the left-turn arrow was disputed, the pedestrian was in a marked crosswalk and the turning driver never saw her. A safety study that only reads the checkboxes reads the crash with the mechanism removed. So: can a transformer encoder read the paragraph well enough to be worth the trouble?

Step 1 — de-identify, or you cannot use it at all

A Florida long-form report carries driver and owner names, home addresses, dates of birth, phone numbers, driver-licence numbers, VINs and plate numbers. The narrative itself mostly says V01 and NM01 — but not always. Names of the deceased, of witnesses, and of the investigating trooper appear in plain text.

The redaction that produced the corpus on this page does not guess. It reads each report's own structured name fields, builds the list of names that report contains, and removes those strings from the narrative, then sweeps regular expressions for phones, addresses, licences, VINs, plates and any date before 2015 (this corpus is 2021–2025 crashes, so an earlier date can only be a birth date).

How every number on this page was evaluated

The baseline

For each task, the classical method an engineer would actually reach for: a keyword rule, TF-IDF with logistic regression, or a regular expression. If BERT does not beat that, it has not earned its keep.

The split

Five-fold cross-validation grouped by corridor. A random split would put crashes from the same intersection — often written by the same trooper, in the same template — on both sides of the line, and every score would come back flattered.

The ugly cases

Rare classes (36 pedestrian crashes, 3 speeding-coded), narratives over the 512-token limit, "update only" reports with three sentences, and the two spellings of a vehicle ID that turn out to drive the clustering.

1 · What actually comes out of the encoder

word2vec gave you one vector per word, for all time. A transformer encoder gives you one vector per token per sentence: the same string gets a different vector depending on what surrounds it. That is the whole claim. Here it is, measured on the word right — which in a crash narrative means three unrelated things.

Three senses of one word, all from this corpus

32 sentences per sense, each tagged by a pattern that fires for exactly one of the three (a sentence matching two is thrown out). The question: how far apart does BERT put the right token in these three groups, and at what depth does that separation appear?

Read the margin, not the cosine

Two unrelated crash narratives, encoded whole and compared, do not score near zero. Their mean cosine is:

A cosine of 0.93 between two documents means nothing on its own. BERT's output space is anisotropic — everything crowds into a narrow cone. Only the difference between a same-group and a cross-group score carries information. This is the same rule as the median/shoulder result from the Sept 17 opener, now at document scale, and it is the reason panel 6 comes out the way it does.

Where polysemy does not help: signal

The obvious candidate in traffic text is signal — the head over the intersection, or the driver's turn indicator. We went looking for both senses in 3,436 narrative sentences:

230

1

One. In this corpus the turn-indicator sense effectively does not occur, and "left turn signal" means the arrow on the mast arm, not the stalk on the steering column. There is no polysemy to resolve, so a contextual encoder buys nothing on that word — and the same holds for platoon, occupancy and most of our vocabulary.

The rule the three senses of right illustrate: contextual embeddings separate senses that live in different topical neighbourhoods. A maneuver, a body panel and a right-of-way rule are three different worlds. Two senses that both live inside traffic engineering stay merged.
Going deeper — what the layer curve is actually showing

Write hi(ℓ) for the vector of token i after layer ℓ. One attention head computes hi(ℓ+1) = Σj αij (WV hj(ℓ)), with αij = softmaxj( (WQhi)·(WKhj) / √d ). At ℓ=0 there is no mixing yet, so every occurrence of right is the same lookup-table row — measured cosine 1.000, margin +0.001. Each layer folds a little of the neighbours into the token, which is why the margin climbs and then flattens.

The curve dips slightly from layer 11 to 12 (margin +0.221 → +0.210). That is normal: the last layers specialise for the masked-language-model head BERT was pretrained with, not for your task. It is also the practical reason people pool from layer 11 or take a weighted mix rather than always grabbing the top.

We deliberately do not plot attention weights as an explanation of which word did the separating. Head-averaged attention from a target token spreads over determiners and punctuation, and attention is not importance (Jain & Wallace, NAACL 2019). The cosine margin is a claim we can defend; an attention heat map is not.

The same measurement on the word left (maneuver vs. vehicle side, 40 sentences each) gives a smaller margin, +0.155 at layer 12 — because "the left turn lane" and "the left front corner" are closer neighbours than a maneuver and a right-of-way rule.

The cost line nobody puts on the slide. Median narrative here is 181 WordPiece tokens, mean 204.7, longest 726 — and 3 narratives exceed BERT's 512-token window outright. Those get truncated, silently, and the part that gets cut is the end of the paragraph: exactly where the officer writes the conclusion.

2 · Entity extraction — and the job it is actually needed for

Token classification tags every token with a label: this span is a person, that one a place. The textbook demo tags people and organisations in news. The transportation job that genuinely needs it is de-identification — an agency cannot release narrative text with names in it, and doing that by hand across 100,000 reports is not a plan. So we ran an off-the-shelf BERT NER model at that job and scored it.

De-identification: can it find the names?

3 · Relation extraction — who struck whom

Entities on their own are a word list. The safety question is relational: which vehicle struck which, in what order, and which one was already stopped. The coded record stores a manner of collision and a point of impact; it does not store the chain. The narrative does.

The gap between 86.7% and 71.9% is the whole argument for a learned extractor. Seven narratives in eight say a vehicle struck something; only seven in ten say it in a shape a pattern can grab. What falls in between is not exotic English — it is "as a result, the front right portion of V02 collided with the passenger side of V01" with thirty words of geometry wedged between the two arguments, or a three-vehicle chain where V01 hits V02 and V03 in one sentence, or an officer who writes what the drivers each claimed instead of what happened.
Zero passive-voice hits is not a bug — it is a finding. Crash-report training tells officers to write active, subject-first sentences. That is why a regular expression does as well as it does here, and it is also a warning: a model tuned on this writing style will meet a different agency's template and quietly stop working.

What a transformer adds, and what it does not

Adds

Relation extraction as a supervised task — mark the two argument spans, classify the pair — handles the thirty-words-in-between case that kills a regex, because self-attention connects the two arguments directly rather than scanning left to right.

Does not add

It cannot recover a relation the officer did not write. Roughly one narrative in eight here never names a striking vehicle: the report is an update, the case is closed on a hit-and-run with no suspect, or the parties gave conflicting accounts and the trooper wrote "due to conflicting statements, the following is unknown."

TF-IDF with logistic regression wins every row. That is the result, and it is not an artefact of a weak setup — it is what 399 training examples buy you. A frozen BERT encoder gives a general-purpose representation; a TF-IDF model gets to spend all of its capacity on the words that actually separate these labels in this writing style, and at n=399 that specialisation beats generality.

Why grouped by corridor

Crashes on one corridor are often written by the same trooper, on the same approach geometry, with the same phrases. A random split puts those on both sides of the line and the model can win by recognising the corridor. Grouping by corridor forces it to generalise to an intersection it has never read about — which is the deployment condition.

How much does this matter? It is the difference between a number you can put in a report and a number that evaporates in the field. Always state the split before the score.

The row that should worry you

Any injury reaches 0.795 against a base rate of 0.564 — a real lift, but from a narrative that frequently states the outcome outright ("was transported to…", "suffered life-threatening injuries"). The model is not predicting severity; it is reading a sentence that reports it. That is a fine extraction tool and a useless prediction model, and the difference only shows up when someone asks it to score a crash that has not happened yet.

Raw bert-base is not a search engine. Look at the two middle columns: 0.1–0.2 precision against base rates of 0.06–0.22 — at or below guessing. This surprises people who assume "BERT embeddings" means "good embeddings". It is the anisotropy from panel 1 doing the damage: when two unrelated narratives already sit at cosine 0.93, ranking by cosine ranks noise. And TF-IDF is not beaten. It ties MiniLM on the pedestrian query and wins the rear-end one, because those queries contain the words the narratives use. MiniLM's win is on “distracted and not paying attention” — where the narratives say “looked into the back of his vehicle” and “did not realize that they were stopped until the last second” and share no vocabulary with the query at all. That is the case semantic search is for, and it is the only case.
Everything fails the lane-departure query (0.0 across the board). Worth understanding before you trust any of this: only 22 of these crashes carry the lane-departure flag, and in an intersection corpus that flag is usually set for a left-turning vehicle crossing a centre line — which the narrative describes as a left turn, never as leaving a lane. The text and the label are talking about different things. No encoder fixes a label that does not mean what its name says.

And two of them are real. #0 is the pedestrian cluster (48.3% pedestrian-involved, 72.4% injury) and #5 is the signal-phase cluster — red, green, steady, arrow. Unsupervised structure is not nothing; it is just not the structure. An adjusted Rand index of 0.037 against coded crash type means essentially no agreement, and purity 0.639 barely beats the 0.614 you get by calling every crash a left turn.
The lesson generalises past text. This is the same failure as a lane-keeping model that learned the camera mount, or a detector that learned the weather of the day it was collected. Cluster structure follows whatever varies most consistently in the input. If you want clusters that mean something operational, you either remove the confound (normalise V01→V1 before embedding) or you stop clustering and supervise.

Deployment rule. Never ship extractive QA without an answerability check. Either use a model trained with unanswerable questions (SQuAD 2.0 style), or set a score floor and calibrate it on questions you know have no answer — here, 12.3% of answers to an unanswerable question cleared 0.5.

So — does it earn its keep?

Eight panels, one corpus, one honest split. Here is the summary an engineer can act on.

Where the encoder won

  • Semantic retrieval when the words differ. MiniLM 0.5 vs TF-IDF 0.4 at P@10 on the distraction query — the narratives never use the query's words.
  • Extractive QA on questions the text answers. 80.6% recall naming the struck non-motorist, 0.0% false alarms.
  • Sense disambiguation with real separation — the three meanings of right, margin +0.210 at layer 12 from +0.001 at the embedding layer.
  • De-identification as a second pass — 85% recall is useful as a check on a structured-field redactor, not as a replacement for one.

Where the classical method won

  • Every classification target. TF-IDF + logistic regression beat every frozen-BERT probe on all four labels, at n=399.
  • Form-driven extraction. Vehicle IDs 95%, directions 99% — by regular expression.
  • Retrieval when the query shares vocabulary with the documents.
  • Anything the checkbox already stores. Weather, light condition, posted speed: read the field.

Fit card — a BERT-family encoder on crash narratives

Idea in one sentenceTurn each narrative (or each token in it) into a vector whose position depends on context, then put a small task head on top. Why it works (the logic)Self-attention lets every token's representation depend on every other token, so meaning that lives in word order and distant context survives into the vector — which a bag of words throws away. Fits these tasksSemantic retrieval across a corpus nobody can read; extraction of spans the form does not capture; de-identification review. (leverage: retrieval · extraction · classification) Needs this dataNarrative text you may legally hold, de-identified first. For retrieval: none — use a sentence encoder off the shelf. For classification: realistically low thousands of labelled narratives before it beats TF-IDF; a few hundred is not enough, and this page is the demonstration. Breaks whenThe answer is not in the text (12.5% weather ceiling); the narrative exceeds 512 tokens; the labels do not mean what their names say (the lane-departure flag); the strongest signal in the text is who typed it rather than what happened. Keep the classical method whenThe target is form-driven, the vocabulary is shared between query and document, the label count is in the hundreds, or the field already exists in the coded record. TF-IDF fits in under a second and you can read its coefficients in a deposition.

Names, addresses, dates of birth, phone numbers, licence numbers, VINs and plate numbers were removed before any model saw the text. Every figure on this page was computed from that corpus; nothing here is illustrative.
