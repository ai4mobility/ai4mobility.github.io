Where knowledge lives: eight places, one hurricane morning

A strong model knows what a generator and a mast arm are. It has not read FDOT District 7's hurricane signal SOP. This page takes the eight places knowledge can reach an agent and fills each one with the real D7 material from the course kit: the instructions, the example cards, the card format, the skill and its files, the search results, the code, the people the SOP names, and what the model already knows. Then it follows one morning's request through them, step by step, and counts what each step puts in front of the model. It goes with Handout 2, How to Pass Knowledge to an AI Agent.

Eight places knowledge can reach an agent

The left column is in front of the model on every call. The right column stays closed until the model, working through a step, decides it needs a procedure, a passage or a number, and asks for it. A person sits after the model and gets back the decisions the agency gives to people. The weights are the model itself. Click any box to see what it holds for D7 signal response.

You already work this way. On a storm morning at the TMC, the one-page card taped to the console is in front of you all the time: who you serve and what never to do (instructions). The SOP binder sits on the shelf until you need a step (the skill). The MUTCD is down the hall, and you pull one section (retrieval). You use a calculator for clock times, not your head (code). Some calls you hand to the TMC supervisor (a person). And your training tells you what a mast arm is without opening anything (the weights).

Going deeper: why the real total is larger than the meter

The meter counts what sits in front of the model once all the steps have run. A real agent calls the model several times for one request: it reasons, asks for a file or runs a tool, gets the result, and calls the model again with everything so far. Each call re-reads the conversation up to that point, so the tokens billed across the whole run are larger than the meter's last number. Prompt caching lowers the price of re-reading the same opening text, which is one more reason to keep the left column short and stable.

What it costs: every call, or only when a step needs it

Anything you put in the left column is paid for on every request, including the requests that never use it. Move the skill's files between the two columns and see what a storm day costs in tokens. The day is a mix of the eight requests from tab 3 in equal shares; you choose how many requests there are.

Tool results (rules.py output) depend on each request, so they can never move to the left column. The instructions, example cards, card format, skill descriptions and the request itself are always in front of the model: tokens.

Tokens are not the only cost. Pasting the whole SOP into the instructions also takes away the reading path. With all 150 clauses in front of it, the model has to find the right phase on its own every time, which is the job the skill's steps 2 and 3 do for it. In the RAG test, meaning search found only 3 of the 15 items of the 72-hour plan in its top 3, while a filter on the SOP's own sections found all 15.

Fit card: where to put a piece of agency knowledge

Idea in one sentence
Put each piece where it costs least and can still be checked: a short page on every call, the rest opened only when a step needs it, and the agency's decisions left with a named person.
Why it works
The model reasons well but has not read your SOP. The left column sets the job; the skill, retrieval and code bring exact words, exact passages and exact numbers at the moment a step needs them; the person keeps what the agency assigns to people.
Fits these tasks
SOP-driven operations (hurricane signal response, incident management), inspection and damage forms, crash coding, study checklists. Leverage: retrieval, generation, extraction, classification.
Needs this data
The SOP with its version and pages; the agency's form; 10 to 20 hard cases; an hour with someone who does the work. No labels to start; labeled items to measure.
Breaks when
The skill's description never matches the words people use, so it never opens; a number is left in prose for the model to compute; a gap is not written down, so the model fills it; examples come from the test set.
Keep the classical method when
A rule is a formula or a lookup table: code it and skip the model. A trained classifier already meets the agency's accuracy. The value comes straight from a sensor, ATMS or a GIS layer.

Check yourself

1. A teammate moves all of sop_text.md into the instructions "so the agent never misses a clause." What does that do to a request like "what is a mast arm?", and what is lost besides tokens?
One way to answer

That request needed only the left column: tokens. Now every request also carries the whole file, more tokens, used or not. The bigger loss is the reading path: the model must find the right phase among 150 clauses every time, instead of the skill sending it to the one section that applies (Immediately Post Storm holds of them).

2. Crews text "lights at Drew St are dark and the gen went out at 6:40" and the skill never opens. Which two places do you fix?
One way to answer

The skill's description, which decides whether it opens at all: add the words crews use ("the lights are dark", "gen"). Then the glossary, so that once it opens, "dark" maps to non-operational and "gen" to generator before the model looks for clauses. Test both with requests written in field words.

3. Where does "First refueling to occur six (6) hours after initial deployment" go, and where does "Develop an ongoing refueling schedule based on fueling needs" go?
One way to answer

The first is a rule with a number: code (rules.py refuel), and the card cites 3.2.1. The second gives no rule, so the schedule after the first refuel goes to a person, and gaps.md says so.

4. Your agency hands you a 300-page traffic incident management manual and a two-page on-scene checklist. Where does each go?
One way to answer

The two-page checklist is a procedure the agent follows: a skill, with its steps, its numbers in code and its deciders named. The 300-page manual is reference you look things up in: retrieval, with every quote cited to its page. The skill's steps say when to search the manual.

5. When would you fine-tune the weights instead of using the other seven places?
One way to answer

When you have many labeled examples of a pattern that is hard to write down (a style of damage photo, a way narratives are written) and it changes slowly. Not for the D7 SOP: it changes by version, a fine-tuned model cannot point to the clause it learned from, and every card must quote one.

Going deeper: how a Claude skill loads in three stages

Claude's skill documentation describes three stages. The name and description of every installed skill are always in front of the model; for D7 that is characters. When a request matches, Claude reads SKILL.md. It opens a reference file or runs a script only when a step in SKILL.md says to. That is why the description must use the words of real requests, and why each step in SKILL.md names the file it opens. Read more in Skills overview.

For your project

  • Write the left column in under a page: who the agent serves, the output form, and what it must never do.
  • Put your manual in a skill, split along its own numbering, with a reading path that names the file each step opens.
  • Put every number, threshold and deadline in code, with a test on a known answer.
  • Use retrieval only for documents too long to load, and quote every passage with its page.
  • Name the person for every decision your agency assigns, and list what your manual does not say.
