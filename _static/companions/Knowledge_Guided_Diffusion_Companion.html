How transportation knowledge guides diffusion generation

A diffusion model learns what traffic looks like only from its training data. You know things the data may show weakly or not at all: a car cannot brake much harder than 1 g, vehicles are conserved on a road, a lane closure caps the flow, and a near-miss matters more than a thousand ordinary passes. This page shows where that knowledge can enter a diffusion model, what your data must contain for each entry point, and how to test whether the knowledge helped.

Builds on 4.10 Diffusion models: the basics and the 35-paper diffusion companion in 4.12. Paper numbers come from those papers (table numbers given). The worked example in tab 7 is synthetic: simulated traffic on a 4-mile road, so the truth is known and every number can be checked.

Five doors for your knowledge

Knowledge can enter a diffusion model at five places. Two are set at training time and need retraining to change. Three are added while or after sampling and can change from one run to the next. Your data sits under all five. Click a door to see what belongs there.

The five doors · click a door

How to read it. Door ③ acts on the clean guess x̂ at every denoising step. Door ④ holds values fixed in the sample at every step, or sets where sampling starts. Door ⑤ acts on the finished samples.

Two questions help pick the door

Does the knowledge describe this one case (a lane closure today) or traffic in general (a car cannot brake at 3 g)? And must it always hold, or only usually? Pick a piece of knowledge to see where it goes; its door lights up in the diagram.

Compared with Diffusion in the Literature

That handout counted five ways to get a dangerous scenario out. Here the lens is wider (any knowledge, not only danger): the representation becomes a door (①), and fill-in, hard limits and head starts share one door (④). Its five ways map onto the doors as: cost guidance → ③, head start and fill-in → ④, condition → ②, select → ⑤.

Data first: what each door asks of your data

Every door depends on data you prepare before training. Conditioning asks the most: each training example must carry its hint. Guidance asks the least about pairs, but its cost must be computable, in physical units, from the model's output.

Door ①: put the law in the representation

The surest way to make every sample obey a law is to generate something that cannot break it. Diffusion is a training method, not a network, so you are free to choose what x is. Six of the nine safety-scenario papers in the companion do not generate positions at all. They generate each vehicle's acceleration and turn rate, and compute positions by rolling those actions through a simple vehicle model.

next position = position + speed × (cos heading, sin heading) × Δt
next speed = speed + acceleration × Δt;   next heading = heading + turn rate × Δt
In words: the model chooses how hard to press the pedals and turn the wheel; physics decides where the car goes.

Where it breaks

A representation also limits what can be made. A car can no longer jump sideways or teleport, but it can still leave the road, hit another car, or roll backward unless its speed is clipped at zero. A unicycle model cannot slide. SceneDiffuser reuses the log's on/off mask, so no vehicle can enter or leave beyond what the log recorded. Choose the representation for the events you need, not only for the ones that are easy.

Door ②: train it in — match the condition to the information

A condition is any hint about x that you can hand the denoiser both in training and when you sample. The network learns what a hint means only from pairs, so the hint must sit in every training example. How it enters the network depends on its shape. Click an inlet to see which kinds of information use it.

Four inlets · click one

The training loss is the one from 4.10 with one more input:

L(w) = ‖ g(zt, w, t, c) − ε ‖²
In words: name the noise that was added, now with the hint c in view. For a share of the examples, c is swapped for a "no condition" input ∅ (a learned vector, or simply zeros), so one network can run with or without the hint. Ten to twenty percent is common (the course heatmap model drops its three settings, and MagicDrive its camera pose and text, 20% of the time); LangTraj drops its text and history half the time. Classifier-free guidance (tab 4) needs this.

Four lessons from the papers

  1. The context can decide whether samples are usable. Scenario Diffusion's map condition moved the share of path points on drivable area from 0.411 to 0.895. In tab 7, three context numbers cut the fill-in error on a simulated road from to mph.
  2. Pick the inlet by the hint's shape. GAIA-2's authors found ego speed and curvature steered the video more accurately through scale-and-shift than through cross-attention, because an action changes every part of the image. When MagicDrive's boxes were fed only as part of the map image, vehicle mIoU on its generated scenes fell to 5.50 (Table 4).
  3. Check that the network uses the hint. In the course unCLIP run on crash sketches, the first decoder received the sketch embedding at about one sixth of the strength of the time signal and learned to ignore it: two samples from the same seed, with and without the embedding, had pixel correlations of 0.64–0.70. Scaling it up by √512 (about 23 times) made it matter about as much as the noise. Even then, every pipeline driven by the narrative stayed at chance, because the narrative–sketch pairing itself was weak. Always compare samples drawn with and without c.
  4. Conditioning cannot make what the pairs lack. MagicDrive's snow never appears, because its data had almost none. A crash is too rare to pair with every scenario, which is why 8 of the 9 safety-scenario papers steer with guidance (tab 4) instead.

Door ③: steer while sampling — lean on a condition, push down a cost

Guidance changes each denoising step without retraining. It comes in two kinds: lean harder on a condition the model already understands (classifier-free guidance), or push the sample downhill on an engineering cost you write (cost guidance). The first needs a trained condition; the second needs a cost you can compute and take a slope of.

Classifier-free guidance: a dial on the condition

gλ = g(zt, w, t, ∅) + (1 + λ) [ g(zt, w, t, c) − g(zt, w, t, ∅) ]
In words: ask the network twice at each step, with the hint and without it, and step further in the direction the hint points. λ = 0 gives an ordinary conditional sample; λ > 0 leans harder on what the hint implies.

Try it · what leaning does to a distribution (two bells, exact math, synthetic numbers)

Grey: speeds at a detector over all days, a bell centered at 50 mph with spread 20 mph. Green: speeds on days with a lane closure ahead, centered at 30 mph with spread 8 mph. Blue: what classifier-free guidance aims at. For bells it has a closed form: the guided distribution is proportional to p(x | c)1+λ / p(x)λ. Raise λ and the blue bell moves past the condition's own center and narrows. That is why a large λ can make samples more alike and pull them off target.

Papers write the dial in different ways, so always read the formula. DiffTraj writes (1 + ω) ε(x | c) − ω ε(x | ∅) with ω = 3, which is λ = 3 here. MagicDrive uses a guidance scale of 2.0, CCDiff a weight between 1 and 2, and GAIA-2 a scale from 2 to 20, applied only to rare or unusual scenes. If MagicDrive follows Stable Diffusion's convention (scale = 1 + λ), its 2.0 means λ = 1.

The condition does not have to be words. CCDiff asks for two predictions: one where each vehicle reacts to the vehicles that affect it (its "causal parents"), and one where no vehicle reacts to anyone. The parents come from a time-to-collision check (3.0 s threshold) combined with attention weights. Guidance between the two then turns interaction up. Traffic knowledge (who affects whom) has become a dial.

A dial is not information. λ cannot add anything that c does not carry; it only leans harder on it. In tab 7, λ = 3 on the correct context raised the fill-in error from to mph.

Cost guidance: write the knowledge as a cost

gJ = g(zt, w, t, c) + λJ √(1 − αt) ∇zt J(x̂)
J(x̂)
The engineering cost of the clean guess (meters, seconds or mph, scaled to have no units); low = what you want
λJ
How hard sampling leans on the cost, a number you choose (written λ in 4.12, Diffusion in the literature)
√(1 − αt)
Scales the push with the noise level: large early, small near the end
∇ J
The direction that would raise J fastest, found by backpropagating through the network
In words: every step, score the clean guess with your cost and nudge the sample toward a lower score. Papers differ in where they push: CTG takes a few clipped Adam steps on the mean of the next, less noisy step; VBD and MotionDiffuser push on the clean guess x̂.

Try it · what a cost does to frequencies (one variable, exact math, synthetic numbers)

x is an interaction's minimum time to collision (TTC). Grey: the model's distribution before steering, a skewed bell with median 4 s (synthetic). The cost is J(x) = x / 1 s, so pushing toward conflicts favors small TTC. The blue curve is the tilted distribution p(x) e−λJ J(x), rescaled, which ideal cost guidance aims at. A conflict here means TTC below 1.5 s; the dashed line marks the shortest 0.5% of the model's own interactions, the edge of what it has seen. The support cost adds a steep penalty below that edge.

"No overlapping vehicles" is a law, not a preference, so it belongs in door ① or ④ for every task; MotionDiffuser's forecaster uses a repeller cost for it. The warning in the forecast row is about preferences such as "keep a safe gap", which would hide the real near-misses you are trying to forecast.

Write a cost that behaves

  • Use units, then scale them away. Compute J from meters, seconds or mph on x̂, and divide by a typical size for that quantity (for example 1 m for a gap, 1 s for PET, 10 mph for speed), so one unit of J means a similar amount of "wrong" in every cost.
  • Make it smooth. A hard rule ("gap below 2 m") has no slope almost everywhere. Use a soft version: softplus (a ramp with a rounded corner) for a threshold, and a smooth minimum (a soft version of "the smallest value") for the closest distance.
  • Limit the push. CTG and MotionDiffuser clip their gradient steps. A push that is too strong breaks realism before it meets the goal.
  • Guide only the vehicles that matter. CCDiff guides only the key agents. The share of agents whose realism and guidance pulls pointed in conflicting directions fell from about 9.1% (CTG) to about 4.8%.
  • A soft cost is not a guarantee. It makes a violation less likely, not impossible. A law that must always hold belongs in door ① or ④.

Rules against realism

Many papers that guide report the same tension. In CTG, more guidance steps cut rule violations but worsened realism and the failure rate (at its default setting, guidance plus filtering improved both). In the course left-turn example, strong steering made conflicts from gaps no driver accepted; a support cost closed that shortcut, and at λJ = 3 the next one opened (the through driver barely slowed: median slowdown about 0). Judge guided samples by how they reach the goal, not only by whether they reach it.

Going deeper (optional): what guidance does to frequencies

Cost guidance aims at a tilted version of the model's distribution, in which low-cost samples are more likely: pJ(x | c) ∝ p(x | c) e−λJ J(x). A guided sample therefore says nothing about how often the scenario happens. To get back to the unguided model's frequencies (which can themselves be off: 29 against 83 conflicts per 100,000 in the left-turn example), each sample would need the weight p / pJ ∝ e+λJ J(x), normalized over the samples. The "Try it" panel above does exactly this and recovers the unguided share. A real sampler only approximates pJ, so frequency estimates in practice use importance sampling with a proposal whose density is known, as in the crash-generation handout (steered crash share 0.58%, about 440 times the unsteered share, relative standard error 2.4%). Treating −λJ J as a log-probability is what turns classifier guidance into cost guidance: Dhariwal and Nichol (2021); Chung et al. (2023).

Door ④: hold it fixed — paste, project, or start from a draft

Some knowledge should not be a push but a fact the sample must keep. You can paste measurements back in, project the sample onto a hard limit, or start sampling from a classical model's draft. None of these needs retraining.

Paste the measurements back in

zt ← m ⊙ ( √αt y + √(1 − αt) ε′ ) + (1 − m) ⊙ zt
In words: y holds the observations (detector speeds, the logged history), m marks where they are, and ε′ is fresh noise. Before each denoising step, the observed cells are overwritten by the observation, noised to the step's level, and the model fills in the rest to match. In 4.10, the course heatmap model filled a four-cell detector outage this way with 2.34 mph error, against 5.16 mph for interpolation.

Papers paste in different things: PMA-Diffusion pastes sparse detector rows and probe traces; SceneDiffuser keeps the logged history; SceneDiffuser++ keeps the test vehicle that a separate planner drives; SLEDGE keeps a real lane map and generates the traffic on it; RoadFusion uses a mask to say where a pavement defect may appear.

Pasting assumes the measurement is right

A biased detector is pasted in as truth. PMA-Diffusion's ground truth came from I-24 MOTION camera data masked on purpose, and real loop and GPS data add bias and time delay, which the paper assumed away.

Project onto hard limits

  • Re-roll through the vehicle model at every step. CTG and CCDiff recompute positions from the actions after each step, so door ①'s law holds throughout.
  • Clip the scene. SceneDiffuser enforces constraints such as "no overlapping vehicles" at every denoising step. A no-collision constraint raised its composite realism score from 0.516 to 0.558; logged data scores 0.593 (Table 1).
  • Add a physics step. After each step, PMA-Diffusion smooths only the missing cells along the free-flow and congested wave directions of traffic flow theory.
x̂ ← m ⊙ y + (1 − m) ⊙ [ (1 − γ) x̂ + γ S(x̂) ]
In words: S is a smoother that knows the two wave speeds, and γ sets how far each step moves toward it. With a prior trained on complete maps, 5% of detector rows observed and no probe vehicles, PMA's mean squared error on hidden cells (speeds divided by a fixed maximum) was 0.1463 for physics smoothing alone, 0.0172 for diffusion fill-in, and 0.0167 with the physics step added (Table 2). Tab 7 tries the same idea: it helped when the model had only the detectors, and hurt once the model also had the context.

Door ⑤: check, select, reweight

After sampling, your knowledge works as a judge. It can reject impossible samples, keep the most useful ones, or weight them back toward the model's own frequencies. These are three different jobs. Rejecting impossible samples changes frequencies least, but even it shifts the shares when failures cluster in one kind of event. Selecting and weighting change them on purpose.

Select the best of many

Many papers draw several samples and keep one. CTG keeps the best of several guided samples. LangTraj keeps the lowest-collision-loss sample of 64. SLEDGE keeps the busiest of 8 scenes. DiffusionDrive's learned score picks one of its 20 plans. Challenger scores candidates in a physics simulator and re-noises the best for five rounds. Cosmos-Drive-Dreams lets a vision-language model reject clips with visible errors (3% were discarded).

Selection changes frequencies just as guidance does

A set of "worst of eight" scenes cannot give a rate. If the output feeds a number such as crashes per year, count over all the samples you drew and use the selected ones only as test cases, or use a method built for rates, such as the importance sampling of the crash-generation handout.

The final check is a real task

A generated sample counts as evidence only after it improves a result on held-out real data. The papers below test generated data on a real task and report one number you can check:

  • DiffTraj: a flow model trained on synthetic trips reached inflow MSE 4.55, against 4.33 when trained on real trips (Table 2): close to real data, not better. That makes it a stand-in for private data, not an improvement.
  • MagicDrive: adding its images raised a BEV segmentation model's vehicle mIoU by 4.34 (Table 3).
  • Cosmos-Drive-Dreams: generated rain and fog clips raised 3D lane-detection F1 on rainy and foggy test clips by 10.4% and 9.4% (Table 1).
  • DrivingDiffusion: frames focused on rare cases raised BEVFusion's NDS from 0.412 to 0.434 (Table 3).

Notice what these share: a classical or real-data baseline, a held-out real test set, and one number a reader can check.

Worked example: four detectors, one piece of knowledge at a time

On a simulated 4-mile road watched by four detectors, giving the diffusion model three context numbers (starting density, demand and the bottleneck's capacity) cut its error on the hidden cells from to mph. Context that was wrong by a quarter of its range raised the error to mph, worse than straight-line interpolation. Physics knowledge helped only while the model knew little else.

What the numbers say

  1. Detectors alone barely beat a good classical method. Diffusion fill-in reached mph against mph for adaptive smoothing, and won on only of 50 maps. Adaptive smoothing already carries the right traffic flow theory.
  2. Context is information the detectors cannot give. The three settings cut the error to mph and the queue-length error from to mi, and beat adaptive smoothing on of 50 maps. Context alone, with no detectors, gave mph. Neither source is enough by itself; together they halve the error.
  3. Physics helps most when the model knows little. With detectors only, the physics step cut the error from to mph, and starting from the smoothing estimate cut it to mph with half the denoising steps. Once the context was in, the physics step raised the error from to mph: it smoothed away detail the model had right.
  4. Wrong context is worse than none. With each setting off by a quarter of its range, the error rose to mph, above straight-line interpolation ( mph), and the truth fell inside the fills' range only of the time. The model was confidently wrong.
  5. The dial adds no information. Guidance λ = 3 on the correct context raised the error from to mph.
  6. Check the spread, not only the average. The physics step made the fills too alike: the truth fell inside their range of the time ( with context), against an honest 78%. A narrow band that misses the truth misleads an agency more than a wide one that admits doubt.

Before you trust this example

Baseline
Linear interpolation, and adaptive smoothing with the road's true wave speeds, both tuned on validation maps.
Honest split
The model saw only training maps; every setting (smoothing widths, physics share, head-start step) was chosen on validation maps; all scores come from 50 test maps used for nothing else.
Ugly cases
The road is simulated, so adaptive smoothing and the physics step use exactly the right wave speeds, and the detectors have no noise or bias. On real roads both advantages shrink. The context is perfect in every run except the wrong-context one, and the bottleneck's start and duration were never given. With context, adaptive smoothing still won on of the 50 maps (try "Where smoothing still wins" above). Fifty maps is a small test.

Fit card, where it breaks, and check yourself

Fit card — knowledge-guided diffusion

Idea in one sentence
Put what transportation engineering already knows into the diffusion pipeline at the door that fits it: the representation, the condition, the guidance, a fixed value, or a check.
Why it works
The denoiser learns typical traffic from data; your knowledge adds the case-specific facts it cannot see and the laws and rules it learned only loosely, at the step where they act.
Fits these tasks
Generating or predicting several plausible outcomes under known constraints: scenarios for testing, detector gap filling, synthetic trips and OD matrices, sensor data for rare conditions. Leverage labels: generation, prediction.
Needs this data
Ordinary and rare examples with their context on the same clock; pairs for every condition; masks and their causes; physical units; a real test set split by site and time. Agencies hold detectors, signal logs and crash records; labs hold trajectory sets such as Waymo, nuScenes, highD and I-24 MOTION.
Breaks when
The knowledge is wrong or stale; the condition was never paired in training; a preference is applied where honest frequencies are needed; a law is entered as a soft cost.
Keep the classical method when
A physics-based estimator with known parameters already meets your accuracy (adaptive smoothing, a Kalman filter, a calibrated CTM); you need a rate rather than examples (extreme value theory, importance sampling); or you cannot test the generated data on a real downstream task.

Where it breaks

  1. Wrong knowledge, followed faithfully. A wrong wave speed, a stale map or a miscoded crash type is obeyed as if it were true. In tab 7, settings off by a quarter of their range made the fill-in worse than straight-line interpolation ( against mph).
  2. Knowledge the data never paired. You cannot condition on what was not recorded, and guidance toward it pushes the model where it has no data. In the course left-turn example, strong guidance produced conflicts from gaps no driver accepted.
  3. A preference treated as a fact. A collision cost in a forecast, or a large λ in an uncertainty estimate, biases the result. Guidance and selection both change frequencies and narrow the variety of samples.
  4. Soft where it should be hard. A cost makes violations rarer, not impossible: CTG's best speed-limit result still violates by 0.019. A law that must hold goes into the representation or a projection.
  5. Checks that pass for the wrong reason. Every written check can pass while the mechanism is wrong, like the through driver who barely slows.
  6. Seams and leaks. Pasted values can leave seams at the edge of the observed cells, and a "history" feature that peeks into the future makes any condition look good.
  7. The classical method was enough. In tab 7, adaptive smoothing with the right wave speeds came within 1 mph of diffusion fill-in from detectors alone ( against mph). On real I-24 data, PMA-Diffusion found smoothing alone far worse. Test before you assume either way.

Check yourself

  1. An agency has three years of loop-detector data on a corridor and wants to fill gaps during incidents, but it never kept an incident log. Which door would carry the incident information, and what would the agency have to start recording first?
  2. A colleague adds a collision-avoidance cost to a diffusion model that forecasts pedestrian paths at a crosswalk. What goes wrong? For which task would the same cost be right?
  3. Your sketch generator seems to ignore the crash narrative. Describe two checks that tell apart a weak condition signal, a weak pairing, and too little data.
  4. You want crash-like conflicts at a roundabout, but your data hold only 12 conflicts. Which doors are still open to you, and which number could you not report from the guided samples?
  5. In tab 7, three context numbers helped more than the physics step. Why? Describe a situation where you would expect the physics step to matter more.
  6. A vendor says its diffusion model "respects traffic physics". Which three questions do you ask, and which door does each one probe?

Project ideas with course data

  • Structured fields against free text. On the course crash data, condition a sketch generator on a few coded fields (number of vehicles, maneuvers, crash type) and compare it with the narrative embedding, using the same corridor split and the ranking test of the unCLIP crash run.
  • A physics step on real detectors. On PeMS data with real outage periods (not random gaps), compare adaptive smoothing, a Kalman filter, diffusion fill-in, and fill-in with a physics step.
  • Near-misses with a support cost. Generate left-turn conflicts at one intersection with a conflict cost plus a support cost, and check PET, set-up support and braking behavior against recorded near-misses.
  • Is the work-zone log worth keeping? Add lane-closure records as a condition for gap filling, and measure the gain on held-out days with closures.
