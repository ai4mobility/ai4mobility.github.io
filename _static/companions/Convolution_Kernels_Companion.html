Convolution kernels: how a network learns to see shape

You said a CNN sees a bit like we do — shape first, then what the shape is. This page lets you set the nine numbers that do the shape-finding, sweep them over a real dashcam frame, and watch the picture change. Then it stacks them into a layer, and finally shows you the filters a trained network actually learned. Every number on this page is computed live in your browser or by the reproducer named in the footer.

One kernel, one job

A convolution kernel is a small template. You slide it over every position in the image, and at each position you multiply the nine numbers under it by the nine numbers in the template and add them up. That single sum is one pixel of the output. Change the nine numbers and you change what the output is a picture of.

The mechanism, before the equation

y[i,j] = Σa=-1..1 Σb=-1..1 k[a,b] · x[i+a, j+b]
SymbolIn this page
xthe frame, as brightness only: 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B, one number per pixel, 0–255
kthe nine numbers you type below — the kernel, the filter, the template. Same word three ways.
y[i,j]one output pixel: what the template found at position (i,j)
i, jrow and column. The output is 2 rows and 2 columns smaller because the window needs a neighbour on every side.

In words: lay the template on the image, multiply, add, write down one number, slide over one pixel, repeat. (Strictly this is cross-correlation, not the flipped convolution of a signals course. Deep learning calls it convolution and the sign convention makes no difference to a filter that gets learned.)

Three kernels worth understanding before you move on

KernelWhat it doesWhy it works
Vertical edgesBright where brightness changes left to right: sign poles, mast arms, lane lines seen head-on, the sides of a truck. The left column is negative and the right column positive, so the sum is (light on the right) − (light on the left). Flat wall → 0.
Horizontal edgesBright where brightness changes top to bottom: bumpers, the stop bar, the shadow line under a vehicle, the horizon. Same thing rotated 90°. This is the one that finds the back of the car in front of you.
Shift rightNothing changes except that the picture moves. A single 1, off-centre. Every output copies a pixel one column over. Verified against a plain array shift: maximum difference 0.0. A convolution can move an image as easily as it can find an edge — which is exactly the operation Newell's car-following model performs on a time–space diagram.
Check yourself. Set every one of the nine numbers to 1/9 = 0.111. What happened to the frame, and which of the three questions above does that kernel answer? Now set the centre to 5 and the four neighbours to −1. Why does that look like the opposite?

From kernel to layer

One kernel answers one question. A convolutional layer is a bank of kernels applied to the same input in parallel, so the image is replaced by a stack of answers. Then a nonlinearity throws away half of each answer, pooling shrinks it, and a second layer asks questions about the answers rather than about the pixels. That is the whole architecture.

frame→ 4 filters→ → → layer 2

The first two filters are the ones you set by hand on the last tab. ReLU and pooling are toggles — turn them off and watch what comes back.

Layer 1 — four questions asked at every position

Read the ReLU numbers. On this frame ReLU zeroes — of the vertical-edge map and — of the horizontal-edge map, but only 0.0% of the blur map. An edge kernel produces a signed answer — dark-to-light and light-to-dark — and ReLU keeps one polarity. A blurring kernel has no negative side to throw away, which is a hint that it is not doing detection work at all.

Layer 2 — questions about the answers

A second-layer filter does not see pixels. It sees the four maps above, stacked, and its weights say which combination of them to look for. Both filters below are hand-set so you can read them:

This is the feature hierarchy, in two steps. Layer 1 sees a 3×3 patch of pixels. After pooling, one layer-2 output depends on an 8×8 patch of the original frame, and it fires on a combination — a place where a vertical and a horizontal edge both occur. Corners are what a bumper, a licence plate, a sign face and a wheel arch all have. Stack this a dozen more times and the receptive field covers a whole vehicle, and the thing being detected is a vehicle.
Where this stops being free. Layer 1 costs 36 weights — four filters of nine. One second-layer filter already costs 36 on its own, because it must weight all four input maps. Filter counts multiply with depth; that is where a CNN's parameters actually go, not in the 3×3 windows.

Both rows are the same argument. The kernel does not care where the bumper is, so it needs one copy of the bumper template instead of one per location, and it still recognises the bumper when the camera shakes by a pixel. The dense layer has to learn "bumper at (312, 88)" separately from "bumper at (313, 88)", and a 1-pixel camera shake rewrites most of its input.

What a network actually learned

Everything so far you typed in by hand. Nobody types kernels in by hand any more — they are parameters, and gradient descent sets them. Below are the real first-layer filters of a trained ResNet-18: 64 filters, each 7×7 across three colour channels, 9,408 weights, downloaded from torchvision's ImageNet checkpoint and exported by the reproducer. Nothing here is drawn or invented — including the eight that turned out to be dead.

The measured split

Each live 7×7×3 filter was split into the part all three colour channels agree on — a pure shape detector, which is what your hand-built kernels were — and the part they disagree on, a colour-opponent detector that fires on a colour boundary regardless of brightness. Two things to take from the live filters. First, 41 of the 56 rediscovered, from nothing but photographs and a loss function, what you typed in by hand ten minutes ago: oriented edge detectors. The intuition you started with — shape first — is what the optimiser also concluded. Second, the remaining 15 are detectors you would probably not have written. A network trained on your data will find the ones your data needs; a network trained on ImageNet found the ones photographs of objects need, which is not the same thing as what a night-time roadside camera needs.
And then there are the eight dead ones. Filters 2, 4, 7, 9, 13, 36, 38, 48 have a largest weight of 0.0000057 or less, against a median largest weight of 0.432 across the live filters — five orders of magnitude down. They are not weak detectors; they are switched off, and whatever the frame, their output is zero. Training killed them and the network shipped anyway, in one of the most-downloaded checkpoints in computer vision. This is the dead-unit failure mode from earlier tonight, in production: 12.5% of the first layer's capacity does nothing at all, and no accuracy number would ever tell you.
Going deeper — why these are 7×7×3 and yours were 3×3×1

A colour image has three channels, so a first-layer filter is a volume: height × width × input channels. It still produces one output map, because the three channel responses are summed. That is why the count is 64 × 3 × 7 × 7 = 9,408 rather than 64 × 49. ResNet uses 7×7 with stride 2 only at the entrance, to cover ground cheaply; every layer after it is 3×3, because two stacked 3×3 layers see the same 5×5 patch with fewer weights and an extra nonlinearity in between. Simonyan & Zisserman (VGG, 2015) is the paper that settled this; He et al. (2016) is the ResNet architecture the weights above come from.

Fit card & where it breaks

Fit card — convolution / CNN

Idea in one sentenceSweep a small learned template over every position of a grid and record where it matched.
Why it works (the logic)The pattern you are looking for is local and can occur anywhere, so one template reused at every position is both far cheaper than one weight per position and robust to the pattern moving.
Fits these tasksAnything on a grid whose axes mean something: camera and dashcam frames, BEV occupancy grids, time×station speed heatmaps, detector arrays. extraction classification prediction
Needs this dataImages or gridded fields, thousands to millions of them for training from scratch, hundreds to thousands of labelled examples to fine-tune a pretrained backbone. Agencies have the video; almost nobody has the labels.
Breaks whenThe pattern is not position-independent, the grid axes do not carry distance, contrast collapses, or the deployment scene differs from the training scene. See below.
Keep the classical method whenThe camera is fixed and the question is "did anything move" — frame differencing against a median background needs no training, no GPU and no labels. Loop and radar detectors already give you count and speed with a calibration, not a training set. Reach for a CNN when the question needs the appearance of the object, not just its presence.

Where it breaks — measured on these four frames

FailureWhat this page shows
Edge energy is not usefulness The vertical-edge kernel returns a larger mean response on the night frame (45.85) than on the daylight stop-bar frame (18.56) — 2.5× larger, on equal-sized crops. Headlights, streetlights and their wet reflections are enormous edges. The pedestrian, the thing you actually need, is a low-contrast shape in between them. A kernel measures contrast; it has no idea what matters.
Position independence is an assumption, and it is sometimes wrong A shockwave can start anywhere on a corridor, so a kernel is the right tool. A bottleneck at a fixed milepost is a property of that location, and a translation-invariant filter is built to ignore exactly that. Ask "would this pattern mean the same thing 50 pixels to the left?" before reaching for a convolution.
Borders The output on this page is two rows and two columns smaller than the input, because the window needs a neighbour on every side. Pad instead and you invent data at the edge of the frame — which on a corridor heatmap is the upstream and downstream boundary, the two places an operator cares most about.
Trained-on is not deployed-on The 64 filters on tab 3 were learned from ImageNet photographs. 41 are generic edge detectors that transfer to anything; the 15 colour-opponent ones were learned for object photographs in daylight. Fine-tuning changes the later layers cheaply and the first layer barely at all — which is good news for transfer and bad news for a sensor whose statistics are genuinely different (thermal, low-light, fisheye).
Two of these frames are not US roads The night crossing is a UK street and the work zone is not a US site; both are stock frames carried over from the Module 1 CLIP lab. They are here for the lighting and the geometry, not as evidence about US conditions. The two daylight frames are a real Florida arterial approach.

Which layers of a pretrained network would you retrain, and what does tab 3 tell you about whether retraining the first layer is worth the labels you would spend?
