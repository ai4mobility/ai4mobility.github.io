The formula

N(x | μ, Σ) = exp{ −½ Δ² } / ( (2π)D/2 |Σ|½ )
with Δ² = (x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ)

x is a reading (here D = 2 speeds), μ the centre, Σ the covariance. Δ is the distance from the centre measured in the bell's own units (the Mahalanobis distance). Every point on one contour has the same Δ, which is why the contours are ellipses. The rings drawn are Δ = 1, 2 and 3.

A normal distribution is not a set of circles

Each detector has its own spread, and every pair can lean together. The bell is symmetric about its centre but almost never round. Try the probe at (72, 68) and at (72, 72): nearly the same distance in mph, very different likelihood, because only one of them goes with the lean.

39%, not 68%

In one dimension, 68% of a bell lies within one σ. In two dimensions only 39% lies inside the 1σ ring, and in three only 20% inside the 1σ ellipsoid. Real night speeds are a bit more peaked than a perfect bell, so more of them sit inside: compare the two numbers under the map.

Going deeper: the axes of the ellipse, and where high-dimensional noise lives

Write Σ = U Λ Uᵀ. The columns of U point along the ellipse's axes, and the 1σ ring reaches √λi along axis i (Bishop & Bishop, Fig. 3.3). That is the same decomposition PCA uses.

In D dimensions, draws from a round bell N(0, I) sit on a thin shell about √D from the centre, almost never near it. Pure noise for a 20 × 96 traffic heatmap (D = 1,920) is a point on that shell, which matters when diffusion starts from noise.

Every Gaussian is a reshaped round one

Take round noise ε from N(0, I). Stretch each axis by its own spread, let the axes lean together, then move the cloud to the centre μ. The result is a draw from any Gaussian you like: x = μ + L ε, where L is a square root of Σ. The VAE and diffusion draw every one of their samples this way.

The recipe

diagonal Σ: x = μ + σ ⊙ ε
⊙ multiplies element by element. L is any square root of Σ; the page uses the Cholesky factor, as numpy does.

    
Where you will see it again
VAE: the reparameterization trick, z = μ(x) + σ(x) ⊙ ε. The encoder computes μ(x) and σ(x); the randomness sits in ε, so ordinary gradients can train μ and σ.
Diffusion: the noising step, xt = √ᾱt · x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt) · ε. Centre √ᾱt x₀, round spread.

  







  
Slice and squash: conditional and marginal

The formulas (Bishop & Bishop, Sec. 3.2.4–3.2.5)

      
slice:  C | B ~ N( m, v )
  m = μC + (ΣCB/ΣBB)(B − μB)
  v = ΣCC − ΣCB²/ΣBB
squash: C ~ N( μC, ΣCC )

      
For one bell, the slice's centre moves along a straight line: it is the least-squares regression of C on B. Its spread shrinks by the same amount whatever B you saw. The squash needs no computing at all.

    
The slice of a mixture is a mixture
With three bells, each bell is sliced on its own, and the slices are reweighted by how likely each bell makes the B reading (a soft guess from B alone). The best fill is no longer a straight line in B, and the answer can have more than one hump.

    
Why the VAE and diffusion care
A VAE decoder is a slice, p(x | z): a bell whose centre depends on the hidden z. Each diffusion denoising step is a slice, p(xt−1 | xt). And p(x) = ∫ p(x | z) p(z) dz is a squash over the hidden variable. For a neural-network decoder that integral has no closed form, which is why the VAE needs the ELBO.

  

  
Going deeper: the linear-Gaussian rule behind probabilistic PCA and diffusion
    
If z ~ N(μz, Λ⁻¹) and x | z ~ N(A z + b, L⁻¹), then x ~ N(A μz + b, L⁻¹ + A Λ⁻¹ Aᵀ), and z | x is Gaussian too (Bishop & Bishop, Sec. 3.2.6). Probabilistic PCA is this rule with a linear decoder. Diffusion applies it once per noising step, xt = √(1 − βt) xt−1 + √βt ε; chaining the steps gives the one-jump formula xt | x₀ ~ N(√ᾱt x₀, (1 − ᾱt) I).

What maximum likelihood gives a Gaussian (Bishop & Bishop, Sec. 3.2.7)

      
μML = (1/N) Σn xn
ΣML = (1/N) Σn (xn − μML)(xn − μML)ᵀ

      
The sample mean and the sample covariance. Every mean speed and standard deviation you have reported was a maximum-likelihood fit of a bell.

    
Squared error is a Gaussian log-likelihood

      
ln N(x | μ, σ²) = −(x − μ)²/(2σ²) − ½ ln(2πσ²)

      
With σ fixed, the second term is a constant, so the highest likelihood is the lowest squared error. The stats under the histogram check this identity live. It is why a VAE trained with mean-squared-error reconstruction assumes a Gaussian decoder, and why diffusion's squared error on the noise is a maximum-likelihood loss.

  

  
Going deeper: N or N − 1, and fits with no formula
    
ΣML divides by N, so it runs slightly small on average: E[ΣML] = (N − 1)/N · Σ. With 1,380 readings that is under 0.1%: C's spread is  mph either way. The closed form is special to one bell. For a mixture, the log of a sum has no closed-form maximum, so it is fitted with EM. For a VAE the log of an integral cannot even be computed, so it maximizes a lower bound, the ELBO.

  







  
Mix bells: a mixture of three Gaussians

  
Over a whole day, detectors B and C switch between free flow, congestion and the transitions in between. One bell cannot describe that. A mixture adds three bells, each weighted by how often its regime happens: p(x) = Σ πk N(x | μk, Σk), with the weights πk adding to 1. The sliders start at the mixture fitted to May by EM.

What each knob does
π moves weight between bells; their shapes stay put. Raise one weight and the others shrink in proportion so all three still add to 1. μ moves a bell. σ stretches a bell along one detector only. r tilts it: set the congested bell's r to 0 and B and C would vary independently inside a jam, which neighbouring detectors do not.

    
Narrow bells are tall
Every bell holds a volume of exactly πk. Free flow is packed into a patch about 2 mph wide, so its peak towers over the congested hill even though their weights are similar. The surface shows height, not share.

    
The soft guess
γk(x) = πk N(x | μk, Σk) / Σj πj N(x | μj, Σj)
Which bell made this reading? Bayes' rule answers with a probability, not a label. EM repeats two moves: soft-guess every reading, then refit each bell with those guesses as weights.

    
Beating the score is not the whole story
More bells or sharper bells can always raise the May score. The June score is the honest one, and even a better June score does not make a bell a physical regime: the "in between" bell is a catch-all for transitions.

  







  
From mixtures to the VAE and diffusion

  
Both are mixtures of Gaussians with a hidden cause. In a mixture the hidden cause is one of K regimes. In a VAE it is a continuous z, and a network places one bell for every z. In diffusion, the noised data are a mixture of round bells, one per training reading.

No weights are learned. The network decides how fast the curve moves as z changes. It lingers where readings are dense and races where they are sparse, so less of the prior lands there. 

      
The encoder's job is a soft guess over z
Click a reading: the green curve is p(z | x), the z values that could have made it. A VAE encoder learns to output a narrow bell like this, q(z | x) = N(μ(x), σ(x)²), for every reading at once. We could add up the bells exactly only because z here is one number. With a 16- or 64-dimensional z no grid is possible, and the ELBO stands in for the missing integral.

Noise turns data into a mixture
xt = √ᾱ · x₀ + √(1 − ᾱ) · ε
p(xt) = (1/N) Σn N(xt | √ᾱ xn, (1 − ᾱ) I)
Each May reading xn becomes a round bell, and all bells have the same weight 1/N. As the noise grows, the bells widen and merge until only N(0, I) is left. Generation starts there and runs the film backwards with a learned slice p(xt−1 | xt) at each step.

        
The best denoiser is a soft guess over the training readings
Its estimate of the clean reading is a weighted average of all readings, weighted by their responsibility for the noisy point. At light noise a few dozen readings matter; at heavy noise nearly all of them do. If a network learned this rule exactly, it would only reproduce training readings. Real diffusion models generalize because they learn a smoother rule; how is an active research question (Kadkhodaie et al., ICLR 2024).

Fit card — Gaussian mixture model

  

    
      
Idea in one sentenceDescribe a complicated distribution as a weighted sum of a few Gaussian bells, one per hidden regime.

      
Why it worksEach regime is roughly bell-shaped; πk says how often it happens; with enough bells the sum can follow almost any smooth shape.

      
Fits these tasksRegime detection, gap filling, outlier flags (very low p(x)), sampling low-dimensional states, multimodal predictions. Leverage: classification, prediction, generation.

      
Needs this dataA few variables (roughly under 20); hundreds of readings per bell; no labels; any detector archive (PeMS, FDOT SunGuide, RITIS).

      
Breaks whenThe data are high-dimensional (heatmaps, images), bounded or heavy-tailed, or K is picked on the training fit.

      
Keep the classical method whenA fixed threshold is enough for a regional report; you need a physical model (fundamental diagram, CTM) that extrapolates to new conditions; or a percentile from the raw data already answers the question.
