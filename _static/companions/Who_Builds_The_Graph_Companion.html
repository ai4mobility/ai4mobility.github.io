Who builds the transportation graph?

On a road network the map gives you the graph. After a hurricane, nobody gives you one, and building it is often the hardest part of the project. This page asks three questions. Can a model learn the graph by itself, the way a transformer learns who attends to whom? Why is that harder for a transportation system than for a sentence? And when the graph is the hard part, what do you do? It ends with a real test: a transformer asked to discover perimeter control on a simulated city, which did not.

Attention already learns a graph

A transformer is a graph network on a complete graph, so its attention weights are a learned graph. A mask is how you hand it the edges you already know. On a road network the adjacency matrix is the mask, and attention only has to learn how much each neighbor counts. Take the mask off, and the model must also learn who its neighbors are.

The second kind needs one equation, because you will meet it, or a close variant, in many forecasting papers (Graph WaveNet, MTGNN, AGCRN).

Ã = SoftMax( ReLU( E₁ E₂ᵀ ) )
N
the number of detectors (207 on METR-LA)
E₁, E₂
two N × c tables of learned numbers: every detector gets one "sender" vector and one "receiver" vector
E₁E₂ᵀ
an N × N table with a score for every pair of detectors
ReLU
sets negative scores to zero, so weak pairs end up with small weights
SoftMax
row by row: turns each detector's scores into weights that sum to 1

In words: two detectors are linked when one's sender vector points the same way as the other's receiver vector. Training on forecast error moves the vectors, and the graph moves with them.

Going deeper (optional): Graph WaveNet keeps the road graph

Graph WaveNet does not throw the road graph away. Its graph convolution adds three terms: one over the forward road graph, one over the backward road graph, and one over the learned graph Ã, each summed over k hops. So the learned graph is added on top of the known one. The paper also tested the learned graph alone, with no road graph, and it did nearly as well as the forward road graph alone on METR-LA and PEMS-BAY. Source: Wu et al., Graph WaveNet, IJCAI 2019, equations 5 and 6.

A post-storm system needs both kinds

The link from a signal to its feeder is a fixed fact. Whether that link matters right now depends on whether the feeder is down, which is the same "the neighbor that matters flips with the state" effect you saw in 3.7.

Check yourself

  1. Set the toy to its starting position, then turn detector 4's receiver vector to 0°. Detector 1 now splits its message between two detectors. Which two, and why?
    AnswerDetectors 2 and 4. Detector 1's sender vector points at 0°, and now two receiver vectors (2's and 4's) point the same way, so both pairs get the same high score.
  2. Shrink every vector to length 0.5. Why does the graph turn almost uniform, even though no vector changed direction?
    AnswerThe scores are lengths times cosines. Short vectors give small scores, and a softmax of small numbers is nearly even. The length of the learned vectors sets how sharp the learned graph is.

Why a storm graph is harder than a sentence

BERT can learn who attends to whom because it reads billions of sentences that share one grammar. A post-storm system gives you a few events, links that hide until something fails, things that fail together, and nodes of many kinds.

Too few events for too many pairs

Every ordered pair of nodes could be an edge: N(N − 1) of them. The storms are the only days the hidden links work where you can see them.

1 · Too few events

A system with 1,000 nodes has 999,000 ordered pairs that could be edges. A few storms cannot tell you which of them are real.

2 · Links hide until failure

On a normal day every signal has power, so a signal's status says nothing about its feeder. Normal-day data, however much of it you collect, never shows that edge at work.

3 · Common cause

In a storm, assets fail together because the same event hits them, not because one caused the other. Infrastructure researchers call this a geographic interdependency: a local event changes the state of everything nearby (Rinaldi, Peerenboom and Kelly, 2001). A model that learns edges from storm data will join everything in the surge zone. Those links help prediction, but they do not mean that restoring one asset restores the other.

4 · Mixed node types

Before attention can compare a signal with a work order, each type needs its own encoder into a shared space (section 3.5). Each kind of relation (powers, controls, drains) usually needs its own query and key weights too.

So what?

"Take the mask off and let attention learn" is a bet that the data shows the links at work. For a sentence the bet is safe. After a storm it usually is not.

Check yourself

  1. Pick "Both" and "What the data shows". Signal G1 and pump P1 changed together. Is there an edge between them? What made them move together?
    AnswerNo edge joins them. Substation S1 feeds both, so S1's trip changed both at once: a common cause inside the dependency graph. A model that joins G1 and P1 would forecast well on this storm and still be wrong about what restores what.
  2. You have ten years of five-minute signal status data, all from normal days. How many of the twelve dependency edges in this city could a model learn from it?
    AnswerNone from status changes alone. On a normal day no source fails, so no edge is ever seen at work. More normal days do not help; you need failures, real or simulated, or the records that state the edges.

Two graphs: what depends on what, and what helps this task

The same nodes can need different edges for different tasks. It helps to split "the graph" in two: a dependency graph of facts, and a relevance graph that changes with the task. Attention learns only the second.

Pick a task. The nodes and the fact edges stay the same; the edges the task needs change. The generator crew and the police deputy are added to the city of section 3.1 for this example.

The dependency graph holds facts about the world

In the illustrative city, substation S1 feeds signals G1 and G2. This generator crew is assigned to G1. The sheriff posts a deputy at G1 when it goes dark. These facts do not change with the task. If you know them, write them down as edges; do not ask a model to rediscover them.

The relevance graph holds whatever helps predict this target

For "which signals are still dark 48 hours after landfall?", the power edges and the crew assignment matter. For "how long does the queue on corridor R1 last?", the edge from G1 to the road it controls and the deputy directing traffic matter more. Same nodes, different edges.

Attention learns relevance, not dependency

A large attention weight means "this node was useful for this task's loss." It does not mean "this node depends on that one." Jain and Wallace (2019) showed that attention weights often disagree with other measures of what a model relies on, and that quite different weights can give the same prediction. Treat a strong learned edge as a hypothesis to check, not a finding.

A link back to BERT

Different attention heads tend to track different relations, such as a verb and its object (Clark et al., 2019). Several heads are a little like several edge types learned at once. On a graph you usually know the edge types (powers, controls, drains), so it is cheaper to give them to the model than to hope the heads find them.

Check yourself

  1. A model trained on storm data puts a strong attention weight from a stormwater pump on a signal 300 m away. A colleague concludes that the pump powers the signal. What would you check before you agree?
    AnswerA common cause first: are both in the same surge or flood zone (a geographic link)? Then the utility's feeder records, and a knock-out test: switch the pump off in the input and see whether only that signal's prediction moves. A large attention weight shows usefulness for the task, not dependency.

Every step from "the graph is given" toward "the model learns the graph" needs more data and gives you a graph you can trust less. In this module's own experiments, telling the model which neighbors matter was worth more than letting it learn how much they count.

By network: trained on 3 synthetic networks, tested on 3 unseen networks of an unseen size, 6 seeds.

A question this recipe cannot answer

"If we restore this feeder first, how many signals come back?" is a question about cause and effect. A graph learned for prediction does not answer it. Causal discovery methods (Granger causality, PCMCI, NOTEARS) try to, but they need interventions in the data or strong assumptions.

Case study: can a transformer discover perimeter control?

A shared transformer could hold a perimeter and could keep one it was given, but it did not discover one on its own. The reason was not that attention could not see downtown. Training never gave that link a reason to matter.

The question

Perimeter control is traffic flow theory's answer to a congested downtown: hold traffic at the boundary so the inside stays near the density where it discharges the most (the macroscopic fundamental diagram). If every signal can attend to every other signal, will a learned policy find that rule by itself?

The test bed and the model

The course's torus simulator (Rule 184 lanes) at 12 × 12 signals with a 6 × 6 CBD. Vehicles drive trips, and 62.5% of trips end in the CBD. Each signal can pick one of six phases, including four that serve a single approach, so a boundary signal can hold only its inbound direction.

Each signal is a token with a positional code, and every token attends to all 144 tokens plus one network token. One shared policy head picks each signal's phase, trained with reinforcement learning (PPO). Training from scratch was too slow on two CPU cores, so each run started from a copy of max-pressure or of the hand-built gate. Two rewards were tried: trips completed in the whole network (one number shared by all 144 signals), or vehicles discharged by each signal's own approaches.

The torus · 144 signals, a 6 × 6 CBD, edges wrap around

Gold: the 20 boundary signals and the 24 approaches that bring traffic into the CBD.

There is something to find. A hand-built gate runs max-pressure everywhere, and holds the 24 inbound approaches while the CBD holds more than 288 vehicles (40% of its 720 cells). Over the second half of the run its gain over max-pressure is 23%, and the CBD stays near 0.43 vehicles per cell instead of 0.72.

Before you trust this result

What is the baseline?
Two-phase max-pressure, and the hand-built gate. Random phases and six-phase max-pressure are far worse (table above).
How was the data split, and why is that honest?
Every number is 32 test runs on seeds not used in training; validation used a separate seed. Load 0.55 lies outside the training range of 0.30–0.50.
What does it do on the ugly cases?
Each training run used one seed, and gaps of 1–2% between the trained policies are about the size of seed-to-seed noise. The budget was small: 31 rounds, 83 minutes on two CPU cores. An earlier 5 × 5 grid study with the same model found the same pattern.

Why some links are very hard to learn

A model can learn an edge only if training contains cases where that edge changes the outcome. The link from the boundary signals to the CBD never did, so attention had nothing to learn from.

Many signals must act together

One approach held for 10 steps changes the CBD by a handful of vehicles. The gate works only when the 24 inbound approaches hold together for hundreds of steps. Standard PPO exploration samples each signal's action on its own, so that joint pattern almost never appears.

The payoff is late and far away

The cost of lost green is immediate and at the boundary. The benefit shows up minutes later, downtown.

One reward is shared by 144 signals

A network-wide trip count cannot say which signal earned it. This is the credit assignment problem. The torus runs suggest, from one seed each, that this noise even loosened a gate the network reward was handed: inbound green at CBD density 0.45–0.50 crept from 0.00 to 0.11.

A decent answer already exists

Max-pressure works well, so small random departures from it mostly look worse.

The same root cause as the storm feeder

  • Storm: feeders rarely fail on a normal day, so the signal–feeder link never shows in normal-day data.
  • Perimeter: under max-pressure nobody ever holds traffic, so the boundary–CBD link never shows in the training data.

The difference: in prediction you wait for failures or simulate them. In control, the agent has to create the cases itself. That is what exploration means, and it is why reinforcement learning makes the problem harder.

Easy or hard to learn?

The link

Fit card: learning the graph structure

Idea in one sentenceLet the model decide who reads whom, either fresh for each input (attention beyond a mask) or once for a dataset (a learned adjacency), instead of fixing every edge in advance.
Why it works (the logic)Training keeps the links that lower the loss, so useful links nobody wrote down can surface, as long as the data shows them at work.
Fits these tasksForecasts on sensor networks whose links are unknown or incomplete; finding missing dependencies between infrastructure records; fusing many sources after a storm. Leverage labels: prediction, classification (is this pair linked?), extraction (edges read from text).
Needs this dataMany samples in which the links vary: months of 5-minute detector data (METR-LA: 207 detectors, 4 months), or simulated failures. A candidate graph from IDs, inventories and SOPs held by the DOT, the county, the utility and contractors.
Breaks whenLinks show only after rare failures or joint actions; things fail together from a common cause; one reward is shared by many nodes; someone reads attention weights as dependencies.
Keep the classical method whenThe network is known and trusted (the road as the mask, graph convolution, max-pressure); you need cause and effect for a decision; an engineering rule already exists, such as a perimeter gate set from the fundamental diagram.

Check yourself

  1. You have two projects: (a) speed forecasts for METR-LA's 207 detectors with four months of data; (b) dependencies among 40 critical facilities from one storm's records. For which one would you let full attention learn the graph, and why?
    Answer(a), with care. Four months of detector data show the links at work every day, and Graph WaveNet's learned graph does well there. Still compare against the road graph as a mask. For (b), one storm cannot pin down the edges among 40 facilities (1,560 ordered pairs); build the candidate graph from records instead.
  2. A learned signal policy on a 50-signal arterial network beats max-pressure by 2% but never holds traffic upstream of a known bottleneck. Name three changes you would try first, and say which research question each one changes.
    AnswerFeed the bottleneck's occupancy to the upstream signals (this asks "does it emerge given the right input?"); coordinated, persistent exploration (same question, better search); a difference reward (same goal, cleaner credit). Then more compute and three seeds before you claim anything.
  3. A model run reads 120 edges out of a county's debris-management plan. Every edge's quote is on the page and both ends are known entities. Is the graph right?
    AnswerNot yet. Those checks protect you from made-up support, not from wrong edges: in Lab 8 they removed no wrong edge on the gold pages. Only a labeled sample of pages tells you precision and recall, and a second run tells you how stable the edges are.
  4. A colleague says: "The torus result proves transformers cannot learn perimeter control." What would you say back?
    AnswerIt shows less. The same network represented the gate and kept it; it did not discover it in 31 rounds from a max-pressure start, with one seed per run and a network-wide reward. The fair claim is about discovery under that training setup, and the levers in tab 7 each test a different version of the question.

The page in six lines

  1. Attention is graph learning: a mask hands the model the edges you know; no mask asks it to learn them.
  2. A learned graph is either fresh for each input (attention) or one per dataset (a learned adjacency, as in Graph WaveNet).
  3. Split the graph in two: dependency facts you should write down, and task relevance the model can learn.
  4. Climb down the ladder only as far as your data allows. On roads and in Lab 5, knowing who the neighbors are beat learning how much they count.
  5. A model learns an edge only if training shows that edge changing the outcome. Storm feeders and perimeter gates both fail that test.
  6. When the link is hard: give the structure, make the missing cases happen, clean up the credit, and say which question you answered.
