From word2vec to BERT — and then to a working classifier

Last week a word had one vector, for ever. Tabs 1–3 introduce the model that computes a fresh vector for every occurrence, measured over complaint narratives. Tabs 4–7 do the thing people actually do with it: take complaints that NHTSA analysts have already coded by component, and ask BERT to do the coding. Every number here is a measurement, including the ones that make BERT look bad.

A lookup table with twelve layers bolted on top

You built a word2vec table by hand in Lab 2: one row per word, and you read the row out. BERT still has that table. The difference is that it does not stop there — it passes the whole sentence through twelve more layers, and the vector you finally use is the one that comes out of the top.

The tokenizer does not split on spaces

Real complaint text, run through the wordpiece vocabulary. Orange chips are continuation pieces — the vocabulary had no row for the whole word, so it was broken up. Numbers under each chip are the row index in the table.

Why this matters for us. A vocabulary of pieces was built from Wikipedia and books. Transportation terms of art are not in it as whole words, so they arrive as fragments and the model has to reassemble their meaning from pieces. Keep that in mind when a model looks oddly weak on a term your agency uses every day.
Going deeper — the one equation behind "every token may look at every token"
attention(q, K, V) = Σj αj vj with αj = softmaxj( q·kj / √d )

q is the token being updated (the query it sends out), kj and vj are what token j advertises and what it hands over, αj is how much of token j gets mixed in, and d =  is the width of one head. In words: the new vector for a token is a weighted average of every token in the sentence, and the weights are set by how well each pair matches. The √d keeps the dot products from growing with width and driving softmax into a corner where one token takes everything. This is the same softmax you met as the multinomial logit — the scores are utilities and α is a choice probability. Bishop ch. 12.

Nobody labelled anything. It learned by filling in blanks.

BERT's training task is deliberately dull: hide about 15% of the tokens and predict what was hidden. The text supplies its own answers, so a corpus of plain prose is all it needs — no annotation, no crash codes, nobody reading complaints. The reason this produces useful vectors is the point of this page: you cannot fill the blank without reading the rest of the sentence, so the model is forced to build, for every position, a representation of that token as used here.

Two of these six are wrong, and that is not a bug in the demo. bert-base-uncased was pretrained on Wikipedia and books. It has read about traffic lights, so the intersection frames land. It has never read a defect complaint, so it fills "the ___ light on the back of the truck" with porch and overhead. That gap is what fine-tuning and domain adaptation are for — the Oct 1 session.
But notice what does not follow. The same model that cannot produce the word tail still separates the three senses of light at accuracy (a linear probe on those same complaints). Telling things apart is an easier job than naming them, and a representation can be good enough for your classifier while the model's own vocabulary is still out of its depth. That is the difference between a representation and a generator, and it is why "which model is best" is the wrong question until you have said what the downstream task is.

The top of the stack is shaped by the fill-in-the-blank objective, so the final layers drift back toward predicting a word rather than describing one. This is why people so often tap a middle layer, or several, instead of automatically taking the last one — a design decision you now have a reason to make deliberately.

Pretraining gave you a description. It did not give you an answer.

Everything so far has been about the quality of the description: the vector for a token depends on its sentence, the senses separate, the margin peaks around layer 10. None of that is a decision. BERT has never been told what a brake is, what a component code is, or that your agency has six of them. Filling in blanks is not your job. So here is a real one.

The task: route a complaint to a component

An owner types free text into NHTSA's complaint form. Before anything can be counted, trended, or turned into an investigation, somebody has to decide which part of the vehicle the complaint is about. That somebody is currently a person reading it. These are real narratives with the component an ODI analyst actually assigned — the labels are the agency's, not ours.

Where the data came from

The trap in the raw file

NHTSA's flat file has one row per complaint × component. A complaint coded under two components appears twice — same narrative, different label. Split those rows at random and the identical text lands on both sides of your split, so your test score is partly a memory test.

What we did about it. Kept only complaints coded under exactly one component ( dropped for this reason alone), deduplicated the narrative text ( more), and keyed the split on the complaint id so no narrative can appear twice. The build script asserts this and refuses to write the file if it fails.

The control: take away the words the label is made of

A complaint that says brake eleven times is not much of a test of reading comprehension. So every measurement on this page is run twice — once on the real text, and once on a copy where every word appearing in any of the six NHTSA label strings is replaced by [thing]. That is words, chosen by a rule rather than by hand, so the control cannot be accused of being tuned. Replaced rather than deleted, so the sentence keeps its shape.

Two ways to use a pretrained model, and they are not the same thing

The fill-in-the-blank head is thrown away either way. The -parameter layer that predicted words over the 30,522-word vocabulary has no use here — you want six component codes, not English. What you keep is the stack underneath it.

Inside one training step

Both halves of this page train the same way. Guessing a hidden word and guessing a component code are the same algorithm — the same scores, the same softmax, the same loss, the same update. What changes is only what counts as the right answer, how many answers there are, and who had to produce them. The loss

p = softmax(z) so pk = ezk / Σj ezj L = − log py the probability the model gave the one right answer
symbolin words
zthe K raw scores the head produces. Any real numbers — nothing stops them being negative.
pthose scores turned into probabilities that sum to 1. This is the multinomial logit you already know: scores are utilities, p is a choice probability.
ythe index of the true class — here the component the ODI analyst coded.
Lone number. 0 if the model was certain and right; it grows without bound as the probability on the right answer goes to 0.

In words: look up the probability the model assigned to the answer that was actually correct, and penalise it for being small. Nothing else about the other classes enters directly — but because the probabilities must sum to 1, pushing the right one up pushes the rest down.

Where the starting point comes from. An untrained head spreads its probability roughly evenly, so py ≈ 1/K and L ≈ log K = for six classes. Why this loss and not “maximise accuracy”

Accuracy counts how often the top score is right. Nudge a weight a little and the top score usually does not change, so accuracy does not change, so there is nothing to descend — its gradient is zero almost everywhere. Cross-entropy always moves, and it cares how wrong you were, not just whether:

Being confidently wrong is punished far harder than being unsure. That is the behaviour you want from anything that will route a safety complaint.

One step of gradient descent

θ ← θ − η ∇θ L every weight moves a little way downhill
  • Why "stochastic". ∇L is computed on a mini-batch of complaints, not all . Partly memory — the whole training set at 128 tokens does not fit — and partly because the noise from a small sample helps the search escape places a perfectly-averaged gradient would settle into.
  • What a step is. ÷ = steps per epoch; epochs = steps in total. Each one touches every complaint in its batch exactly once and updates all parameters.
  • What η does. Too large and the loss bounces or diverges; too small and you run out of budget before it converges. Here η peaks at — tiny, because the weights being moved are already good and we only want to nudge them.

Going deeper — what AdamW actually does, and why there is a warm-up

The update above is plain gradient descent. The run used AdamW, which differs in three ways that matter to anyone turning the knobs:

  • A step size per parameter. Adam keeps a running mean of each parameter's gradient and a running mean of its square, and divides one by the square root of the other. A weight whose gradient is consistently small still moves; a weight whose gradient is large and erratic moves less. η sets the scale, not the distance.
  • Momentum. The running mean carries motion across steps, so the path does not reverse every time one awkward batch disagrees.
  • Decoupled weight decay (the "W"). Shrinking weights is applied directly to the weights instead of being folded into the gradient, which is the correction Loshchilov & Hutter (ICLR 2019) showed Adam needed.

Why warm-up. Those running averages start empty, so the first steps would otherwise take badly-scaled jumps with a freshly-random head attached to a carefully pretrained backbone — a good way to wreck it before it learns anything. So η is ramped from zero over the first steps, then decayed linearly to zero. You can see both halves in the gold curve above. The first step of the run has η = 0 exactly and changes nothing; that is the schedule, not a bug.

Why clip at 1.0. An occasional batch produces a huge gradient and one such step can undo an epoch. Gradient norms are rescaled to a ceiling of 1.0 before the update. In this run of the steps were above that ceiling and got rescaled. That is not a rare safety net: at this learning rate clipping binds on most steps, which in effect normalises the direction of every update and leaves η in sole charge of how far each one goes.

Devlin et al., NAACL 2019, §A.3 for the fine-tuning recipe; Loshchilov & Hutter, ICLR 2019, for AdamW.

The thing to carry out of this tab. There is no separate "BERT algorithm". There is one loop — score, softmax, compare with the answer, step downhill — and it is the same loop whether the answer is a word the text was hiding or a component code a person typed. Choosing what goes in the answer slot is the modelling decision, and it is the one part nobody can do for you.

Going deeper — what these 64 numbers are

Shipping 2,940 × 768 numbers to your browser would be a 9 MB page, so each complaint's mean-pooled BERT vector was compressed to 64 principal components, keeping of the variance, and quantised to one byte per number. That compression costs accuracy, which is why the two dashed lines are not in the same place: scikit-learn gets on these 64 features and on the full 768. Your curve should approach the lower line, not the upper one. A widget that quietly compared itself to the wrong target would be flattering you.

When to reach for it — and when the old thing wins

The measurements on this page do not say "use BERT". They say something more useful and less comfortable.

Fit card — BERT fine-tuned for text classification

Idea in one sentence
Take a model that already knows how English works, add a six-way switch on the end, and let the whole thing adjust until the switch is right.
Why it works (the logic)
Pretraining on fill-in-the-blank builds a representation of every token in context. Fine-tuning re-shapes that representation around your labels, so the classifier's job becomes easy.
Fits these tasks
Short free text you must classify, tag, match or search: crash narratives, 511 event text, complaints, inspection notes, maintenance work orders. (classification · extraction · retrieval)
Needs this data
A few thousand labelled examples — here, . No labels at all if you only want the vectors. The text has to look like ordinary prose. You already have it: it is sitting in your complaint and crash databases.
Breaks when
The text is codes and abbreviations (V1, POV, NB, MP 12.4); a document runs past 512 tokens, so anything after roughly the first wordpieces is simply not read; your label is rarer than about 2%; the answer turns on numbers rather than words.
Keep the classical method when
TF-IDF plus logistic regression already gets you there — on this task it scored in , against in for fine-tuning on two CPU cores. If you cannot beat it, that is a finding. It is also the model you can explain to a review board line by line.

Evaluation close

  • Baseline. TF-IDF + logistic regression, on the same split. Not a strawman — on this page it beat the frozen pretrained model.
  • The split. By complaint, never by row: the source file repeats a narrative once per component code, so a random row split trains and tests on the same text. Deduplicated narratives too. In your own work the equivalent is splitting by crash report, by corridor, or by year — never by sentence.
