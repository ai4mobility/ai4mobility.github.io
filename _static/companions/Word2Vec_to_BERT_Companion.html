From word2vec to BERT

Last week a word had one vector, for ever. This page introduces the model that computes a fresh vector for every occurrence — what it is made of, how the training task forces it to read context, and what that buys on a real task. Every number and every point on this page was measured on real NHTSA complaint narratives.

A lookup table with twelve layers bolted on top

You built a word2vec table by hand in Lab 2: one row per word, and you read the row out. BERT still has that table. The difference is that it does not stop there — it passes the whole sentence through twelve more layers, and the vector you finally use is the one that comes out of the top.

The tokenizer does not split on spaces

Real complaint text, run through the wordpiece vocabulary. Orange chips are continuation pieces — the vocabulary had no row for the whole word, so it was broken up. Numbers under each chip are the row index in the table.

Why this matters for us. A vocabulary of pieces was built from Wikipedia and books. Transportation terms of art are not in it as whole words, so they arrive as fragments and the model has to reassemble their meaning from pieces. Keep that in mind when a model looks oddly weak on a term your agency uses every day.
Going deeper — the one equation behind "every token may look at every token"
attention(q, K, V) = Σj αj vj with αj = softmaxj( q·kj / √d )

q is the token being updated (the query it sends out), kj and vj are what token j advertises and what it hands over, αj is how much of token j gets mixed in, and d =  is the width of one head. In words: the new vector for a token is a weighted average of every token in the sentence, and the weights are set by how well each pair matches. The √d keeps the dot products from growing with width and driving softmax into a corner where one token takes everything. This is the same softmax you met as the multinomial logit — the scores are utilities and α is a choice probability. Bishop ch. 12.

Nobody labelled anything. It learned by filling in blanks.

BERT's training task is deliberately dull: hide about 15% of the tokens and predict what was hidden. The text supplies its own answers, so a corpus of plain prose is all it needs — no annotation, no crash codes, nobody reading complaints. The reason this produces useful vectors is the point of this page: you cannot fill the blank without reading the rest of the sentence, so the model is forced to build, for every position, a representation of that token as used here.

Two of these six are wrong, and that is not a bug in the demo. bert-base-uncased was pretrained on Wikipedia and books. It has read about traffic lights, so the intersection frames land. It has never read a defect complaint, so it fills "the ___ light on the back of the truck" with porch and overhead. That gap is what fine-tuning and domain adaptation are for — the Oct 1 session.
But notice what does not follow. The same model that cannot produce the word tail still separates the three senses of light at accuracy (tab 5). Telling things apart is an easier job than naming them, and a representation can be good enough for your classifier while the model's own vocabulary is still out of its depth. That is the difference between a representation and a generator, and it is why "which model is best" is the wrong question until you have said what the downstream task is.

0 — the embedding table

Read the margin, not the cosine. Two vectors in this space are never near zero even when the words are unrelated, so the number that means something is the gap: how much closer two occurrences of the same sense are than two occurrences of different senses. It starts at and peaks at .
The last two layers give some of it back. The margin dips after layer 10. The top of the stack is shaped by the fill-in-the-blank objective, so the final layers drift back toward predicting a word rather than describing one. This is why people so often tap a middle layer, or several, instead of automatically taking the last one — a design decision you now have a reason to make deliberately.

Two tasks, and each space fails a different one

A linear probe — logistic regression, 5-fold stratified cross-validation — on exactly the same occurrences. Two questions: which of the four words is this, and which of the three senses of light is this. The dashed mark is the majority-class floor: what you score by always guessing the commonest answer.

The third column repeats the sense task after deleting the word the label came from — "engine", "tail", "red". Without that control the whole experiment is circular: the labels were derived from those words, so a model could score well by reading the giveaway and nothing else.

A — the token's own vector

Perfect at naming the word, and mathematically incapable of the sense task. All 750 inputs are the same vector and the labels differ, so no classifier on top of it can ever do better than guessing. That is a ceiling, not a weak model.

B — average the neighbours

You can get context into a static space by averaging the words around the target, and it works. But the average no longer says which word you were describing — it barely beats the floor on the first task. Order is gone too: "merged left of the truck" and "the truck merged left of me" give the identical vector.

C — the token in context

Carries both, and keeps most of the sense when the giveaway word is deleted — so it is reading the sentence, not the word next door. The cost is real: and a forward pass per sentence instead of a table lookup.

When to reach for a contextual embedding

Idea in one sentence
Instead of looking a word's vector up in a table, compute it from the whole sentence, so the same word gets a different vector in a different context.
Why it works (the logic)
Predicting hidden words forces the model to read the rest of the sentence; the by-product of doing that well is a per-occurrence representation.
Fits these tasks
Anything whose label varies within a word or document type — sense and intent, entity roles, sentence-level classification of free text. Leverage labels: extraction, classification, retrieval.
Needs this data
Free text, one forward pass per item. No labels for the representation itself; a few hundred labelled items for the probe on top. Runs on a CPU at this scale; a GPU once you are into tens of thousands of documents.
Breaks when
The vocabulary is out of domain (see tab 2), the two senses live in the same topical world, the text is shorter than its context, or the label depends on something the text never says.
Keep the classical method when
The task is topic-level and a keyword rule or TF-IDF already separates it; the categories come from a fixed controlled vocabulary; you need an auditable rule an agency can publish; or the corpus is small enough to read.

  2. Tab 5 shows representation B barely beating the floor at naming the word. Describe a transportation task where that failure would not matter at all, and one where it would sink the project.

  4. Your sense probe hits 0.96 on held-out complaints and 0.61 when the same model is pointed at a neighbouring state's crash narratives. Name the two most likely causes and the cheapest check for each.
The one-line version. A representation is not good or bad in the abstract — it is good or bad for a task. Word2vec throws away the occurrence; averaging throws away the word; a transformer keeps both and charges you a forward pass. Decide what your task cannot afford to lose, then pick.
