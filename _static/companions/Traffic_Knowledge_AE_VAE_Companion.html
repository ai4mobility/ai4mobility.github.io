How to integrate traffic domain knowledge to guide AE/VAE generation

A generated time–space heatmap only makes sense to a traffic engineer if its shockwaves do: sharp boundaries, moving at speeds the fundamental diagram allows. This page checks that on the course's CTM heatmaps, shows why a plain VAE smears fronts, tries four ways to put traffic flow theory into an autoencoder, and asks whether a GAN's discriminator would do better.

Two things every real heatmap shows

Each heatmap covers 4 miles of road (upstream at the bottom) over 9.6 minutes (time to the right), as 20 road cells × 20 time steps of speed, from the course's cell transmission model (CTM). Traffic flow theory says a real one must show two things. Its fronts are sharp: a queue's back is a jump, not a ramp. And every front moves at the speed the fundamental diagram gives it. The short marks on the heatmap are the measured direction of every strong edge.

Blue marks fail test 2 (a congested front not moving at −15 mph). Test 1: the band every wave stays in

c = − (∂v/∂t) / (∂v/∂x) the speed of an edge in the speed field v(x, t)
−w ≤ c ≤ u → −15 mph ≤ c ≤ +75 mph

v is speed, x is distance along the road (downstream positive), t is time. u = 75 mph is the free-flow speed and w = 15 mph the backward wave speed of the course CTM's triangular fundamental diagram. In words: no change in traffic can travel upstream faster than 15 mph or downstream faster than 75 mph. This test says a speed is possible, not that it is right.

Test 2: the shock formula, where a speed heatmap can check it

s = (q₁ − q₂) / (k₁ − k₂) both sides congested → s = −15 mph

s is the speed of a front between traffic states (k₁, q₁) and (k₂, q₂): density and flow on either side. A speed heatmap hides half of this. Every free-flow density from 0 to 33 veh/mi/ln reads 75 mph, so at the back of a queue k₁ is unknown. Where both sides are congested (below 60 mph here), both densities are known, and on the straight congested branch of the triangular diagram every chord has slope −15 mph. A congested front more than 3.75 mph off fails.

Sharp fronts

In the CTM a queue's back is a shock: speed drops from free flow to a crawl within one cell. The checks call the biggest jump between road neighbours in a heatmap its front sharpness. The real test heatmaps average mph.

Why real heatmaps get flagged too

Each image cell averages 2 fine CTM cells and 6 time steps, which bends edges a little. The tests allow slack (test 1: −18.75 to +93.75 mph; test 2: ±3.75 mph) and still flag of real edges on test 1 and of real congested fronts on test 2. That is the noise floor: a generator is only worse where it flags clearly more.

Going deeper: why every wave moves between −15 and +75 mph (optional)

In the LWR model a small disturbance travels at the slope of the fundamental diagram, Q′(k): +u on the free-flow branch and −w on the congested branch of a triangular diagram. A shock between two states (k₁, q₁) and (k₂, q₂) moves at the slope of the chord between them, s = (q₂ − q₁) / (k₂ − k₁) (the Rankine–Hugoniot condition). A chord of a concave diagram is never steeper than its steepest branch, so s also lies between −w and +u. The CTM is a discretized LWR model, so its heatmaps obey the same bound. For a front with free flow on one side, a speed heatmap still gives a tighter bound. Count the cars crossing the moving front: k₁(v₁ − s) = k₂(v₂ − s), so what enters from behind leaves in front. Cars never drive backward, so a front can move downstream no faster than the cars on its slower side: −w ≤ s ≤ vslow. On these heatmaps that bound flags almost nothing beyond test 1, so the page uses tests 1 and 2. See Daganzo (1994) for the CTM and Newell (1993) for the kinematic-wave view.

Why a squared-error model smears the front

The loss picks the answer

argmin over y of E[(y − x)²] = mean of x
argmin over y of E[ |y − x| ] = median of x

x is one possible speed in a cell, y is the model's output for that cell. When the future is uncertain, squared error rewards hedging: an in-between speed is never far from any possibility. The in-between speeds are what turn a jump into a ramp.

This is what the VAE does

A 2-latent VAE cannot say exactly where every front is, so its decoder learns the average heatmap of all training heatmaps that share a code. The course's VAE handout measured this: its decoder sits 2.98 mph from that neighbour average, against 8.68 mph from any single neighbour.

The median is not the fix either

The median keeps one sharp front, but at the middle start time only. It throws away the other possibilities. To keep both sharpness and the spread of possible futures, a generator has to sample one future at a time, which is what diffusion models and GANs do well.

Four places traffic knowledge can enter

An autoencoder squeezes a heatmap x into a few latents z and rebuilds y. Traffic flow theory can enter at four points. Two of them guarantee valid physics; two only push the model toward it.

Conservation comes free

k = − ∂N/∂x q = ∂N/∂t ⇒ ∂k/∂t + ∂q/∂x = 0

k is density (veh/mi), q is flow (veh/h). Any smooth N that never decreases in time gives a k and q that conserve vehicles exactly, because the two mixed derivatives of N are equal. A generator that outputs N cannot create or lose a vehicle. The shock formula is this same balance written across a jump, so it comes free too, as long as the flow also follows the fundamental diagram. To force that, let the network draw only the boundary curves and fill the inside with Newell's formula (below).

Why a speed heatmap cannot be checked

Every free-flow cell reads 75 mph whether it holds 5 or 33 vehicles per mile. So conservation, and the queue's shock speed, cannot be checked from speed alone. If physics matters, generate density or N, and convert to speed for display.

Going deeper: Newell's simplified model is a min over N (optional)

With a triangular fundamental diagram, N at a point equals the smaller of two shifted values: the count arriving from upstream at free-flow speed, and the count arriving from downstream at the backward wave speed plus the vehicles that fit in between at jam density (Newell, 1993). The course companion on Newell's shift and the LWR shockwave as convolutions builds exactly this as a network layer, a min-plus convolution. A decoder built from such layers produces kinematic-wave solutions by construction.

Swap the rebuild loss for a judge: a GAN

A GAN trains its generator against a second network, the discriminator, which learns to tell real heatmaps from generated ones. There is no rebuild error, so nothing averages heatmaps and nothing smears the front. Three versions, each trained twice for steps on the same 3,200 heatmaps: (a) the VAE's own decoder network as the generator, so only the loss differs from the plain VAE; (b) the same, with the discriminator also shown where each heatmap's fronts fail tests 1 and 2; (c) a standard small convolutional generator with 8 latents.

How a GAN trains · the generator never sees a real heatmap; it only hears the judge's score

discriminator loss = max(0, 1 − D(x)) + max(0, 1 + D(G(z))) generator loss = − D(G(z))

D(·) is the discriminator's score, x a real heatmap, G(z) a generated one. In words: the discriminator learns to score real heatmaps above +1 and generated ones below −1; the generator learns to raise the score of what it draws. Neither loss mentions traffic flow.

Where a physics rule belongs

A discriminator is a learned check. It enforces only what it notices, only as well as it has learned it, and the generator can satisfy it by drawing fewer kinds of traffic. A rule you can write down belongs in the generator (places 1 and 2), where it holds by construction. Published physics-informed GANs do this: PI-GAN builds the differential equation into the generator, and TrafficFlowGAN adds the traffic model as a loss on the generator. Use a discriminator for what you cannot write down, such as realistic noise.

Before you trust these results

Baseline: the plain VAE and the simulator decoder above, same 3,200 training heatmaps, 400 draws each, scored the same way. Each GAN was trained twice (seeds 0 and 1). GANs are sensitive to their settings, so read these as one honest try, not a ceiling. Hard cases: heatmaps that start congested; coverage is there to catch a generator that draws only easy traffic.

The shock formula on the live run

The queue's back moves at s = (μ − qup) / (kqueue − kup): μ is the bottleneck's flow and kqueue the matching congested density; qup, kup describe the traffic arriving from upstream. That is first the light starting traffic, then the inflow, so the blue line bends where the inflow's front, moving at 75 mph, reaches it. When the closure lifts, the queue discharges from the front at −15 mph (gold). Both lines come from the formulas, not from the picture. They are drawn when the road starts in free flow and both arriving flows exceed the bottleneck's.

The price

Rebuild error is slightly higher: mph against . The simulator has no room for the small density bumps in the real starting states; even the true five inputs rebuild a test heatmap only to mph (median). On real detector data there would also be physics the CTM lacks: capacity drop, stop-and-go waves. A common fix is simulator plus a small learned correction.

The latents now mean something

Because z only feeds the simulator's inputs, each latent reads as a mix of knobs. Check, repair, then judge

A repaired draw is no longer what the generator made. If many draws move far, the generator and the physics disagree, and that is worth knowing before anyone uses the draws.
