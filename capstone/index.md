# Capstone -- Final Project

*Runs from the beginning of term through the final submission. See the {doc}`../syllabus`
for graded weights.*

## Overview

The final project is completed in teams and is worth **50% of the course grade** — 20% for the
proposal and progress updates, 30% for the final product, report, and presentation.

Both research-oriented and practice-oriented topics are offered. You are encouraged to use AI
methods, hardware-integrated prototypes, or agentic AI workflows to address a real
transportation problem. The best projects usually come from a problem someone actually has,
not from a method someone wants to try.

Project teams receive feedback from an **advisory committee of local transportation experts**.
That review is the most valuable thing the course offers: practitioners will tell you whether
your idea survives contact with data availability, procurement, and operations — the
constraints that don't appear in papers.

## Rotating project progress reports

Beginning Oct 1, most Thursday meetings open with a short rotating project progress report.
With approximately four to five project teams, one team normally presents each week, with
additional short check-ins or project clinic time as needed. Every team presents twice before
the Nov 19 dry run.

Presenting teams should report current progress rather than wait for polished results. A
useful update includes the current problem statement, data or hardware status, method or
workflow, baseline or comparison plan, early findings, failures or blockers, next steps, and
specific questions for the class.

Students who are not presenting are expected to participate as reviewers. They should ask
constructive questions during discussion and submit brief feedback after the presentation:
one technical question, one suggestion for improvement, and one connection to that week's
course topic. This keeps the whole class engaged and lets every project benefit from a wider
set of perspectives.

## What makes a good topic

A strong project has a problem statement that survives the question *"and then what?"* — a
result that someone would act on. Concretely, look for:

- **A real operational pain point.** Something an agency, a research group, or an operator
  currently does slowly, manually, or not at all.
- **Data you can actually get, in the time you have.** This is the constraint that kills most
  projects. Topics are confirmed Sept 17 and presentations are Dec 3 — eleven weeks, and only
  seven of them after the graded proposal on Oct 15. Confirm access before you commit to the
  topic, not after.
- **A defensible baseline.** If you can't say what your method is better *than*, you can't
  claim it's better. A tuned classical baseline is worth more to your grade than a second
  neural architecture.
- **A scope you can finish.** A working narrow thing beats an ambitious broken thing. Every
  time.

The {doc}`../intro` page lists example open mobility challenges contributed by practitioners.
These are real problems, and picking one up gets you a domain contact.

## Deliverables

- **Proposal** — problem, why it matters, data and access plan, method, baseline, evaluation
  plan, risks, and team roles.
- **Progress updates** — honest status, including what failed. A progress update reporting only
  success is less useful than one reporting a dead end you've now ruled out.
- **Final product** — the working artifact: code, notebook, prototype, hardware demonstration,
  or workflow.
- **Report** — methods, results, limitations, and practical implications.
- **Presentation and demonstration** on Dec 3, to the class and the advisory committee.

## Team expectations

Grades may include both a team component and an individual component. Peer evaluation may be
used to assess contribution, collaboration, and reliability. Teams that divide work by
component and never integrate tend to discover the integration problems in the last week —
integrate early and often, even with placeholder pieces.

## Data, privacy, and external review

Two requirements that are easy to overlook and expensive to fix late:

```{important}
Projects that involve collecting video, imagery, or other data about identifiable people
require **instructor approval and IRB consultation before any data collection begins**. This
includes dashcam footage, roadside video, and Wi-Fi/Bluetooth probe data. Start this
conversation when you select your topic, not when you're ready to collect.
```

Because project materials are shared with reviewers outside the university, you may request
that identifying information be withheld, and you are never required to share proprietary or
personal data as part of the project.
